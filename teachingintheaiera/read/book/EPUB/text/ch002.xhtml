Introduction. Why This Book, and How to Read It

A student can now ask a machine for a finished essay and receive one in seconds. Teachers can ask the same machine for a lesson plan, a quiz or a letter to families. Few of us could make either request a few years ago. If a student hands in such an essay next week, the teacher will need a clear and fair way to respond.

This book is for the teacher standing in the middle of that change. Much of the talk about AI reaches teachers as either promise or alarm. Neither helps much on an ordinary Tuesday. Teachers need something more reliable. They need to know what has changed, what the evidence can and cannot yet say, and what to do with the students in front of them.

The change also arrived in an unusual way. Unlike earlier waves of classroom technology, generative AI came in through students’ pockets, and it produces the very work schools use to judge learning. It also spread very fast. Chapter 1 sets out those differences. They explain why the old lessons from earlier waves of technology still matter, and why they now feel urgent.

My promise is practical. By the last page you should be able to use AI where it helps learning and set it aside where it harms. You should be able to explain both choices to a colleague, a parent or a student. Each chapter also helps you tell a strong finding from an emerging or contested one, because the evidence is young and moves quickly.

The book makes no promise that AI will rescue schools, and no claim that it will ruin them. It argues for something quieter. Teaching has always rested on a person who knows the learner and answers for the judgements made about them. That still holds when a machine helps, though the ways of keeping it true have changed.

Why a teacher’s book, and not another policy paper or a guide to one product? Policy papers speak to systems, and product guides date within months. A teacher’s book can stay with the questions that arise in an ordinary classroom, with thirty students, one timetable and perhaps one weak internet connection.

Who this book is for, and the three lenses behind it

This book speaks first to school teachers, in primary and secondary classrooms and in any subject. It also speaks to the people who work alongside them, from heads of department and school leaders to teacher educators. University teachers have a chapter of their own, and much of the rest will travel to the lecture hall. The examples come from many kinds of classroom, including those with one shared phone, an unreliable signal or no device at all.

Phase matters throughout. Primary teachers will find that most sensible AI use sits on their side of the desk, since their students are too young for most tools. Secondary teachers will find much of the evidence on homework, tutoring and assessment drawn from classrooms like theirs. Whenever a finding comes from a university, the chapters say so, so that no reader confuses a university result with a school result.

I taught in schools, so school teachers sit at the heart of this book. My own years in Pakistan’s education system ran to more than a decade. They included work as an elementary school teacher and as an assistant education officer in Gujranwala. My doctoral thesis also explores teachers’ pedagogical digital competence and teaching practices in higher education. That gives me first-hand knowledge of how these changes are unfolding at different levels of education. Chapter 1 begins where my own teaching began, in an elementary classroom, with the printed guidebook that offered students a ready answer to every exercise in the textbook.

Three lenses shape what follows. Through the classroom lens, I ask what an idea will do to next week’s lesson. The research lens asks what the evidence shows, for whom and how firmly. With the engineering lens, I ask how a tool produces its output and where it is likely to fail. Each lens corrects the others. A teacher’s guess about what works needs testing, a study needs a real classroom, and a tool needs a teacher who understands how it works.

The engineering lens comes from my work as an AI engineer, building automation and multi-agent systems, and from running PakEdX, an AI-powered platform for teachers in Pakistan. PakEdX is my own platform, so I name it as experience and never as evidence.

My doctoral study surveyed 127 teachers at one public university in Pakistan and then interviewed 32 of them (Ilyas, 2026, p. xii). It used DigCompEdu to describe their competence and explored how teachers at different levels brought technology into their teaching (pp. 3, 9). Teachers at higher levels did not use more tools. They chose fewer, with more care and closer oversight, and all of them worked with the same unreliable infrastructure (pp. 229–230). These were university teachers in one institution, so I treat their accounts as a view from one setting and never as evidence about schools (p. 241).

The argument in brief

Thirteen chapters carry one argument. AI has changed what students can hand in far more than it has changed what learning requires. Effort, feedback, relationships and judgement still do most of the work. What has changed is the evidence a teacher can trust, the shortcuts a student can reach and the work a teacher must do before a tool helps. A teacher stays answerable for what reaches students, and that needs time, support and a voice in how tools arrive.

Several themes carry that argument from chapter to chapter. The first concerns fluency. A language model produces text that fits the patterns of its training, and a fluent answer can still be false. That idea, set out in Chapter 2, grows into a theme about knowing. Chapter 5 names it, Chapter 6 sharpens it for assessment and Chapter 12 gives it its fullest form for universities. Across them runs one question. What can this learner explain, use and defend without the machine?

A second theme concerns what stays human. Chapter 3 tests the familiar claim that relationships, judgement, care and modelling form the human core of teaching. It finds the evidence uneven: strongest for relationships, though that evidence shows links rather than causes, and weakest for modelling. Chapter 13 returns to the claim once newer evidence is in, and keeps it in a narrower form. What endures, on that reading, is a professional commitment to relationships, care and accountable judgement. Neither chapter claims that machines could never do such work, only that the decisive tests have not yet been run.

Two terms of my own run through the book, and Chapter 4 defines them. Teacher-design burden is the effort a teacher must put in before a tool helps learning, as distinct from simply having access to it. It grows from work on the knowledge teachers need to fit technology to content and pedagogy (Mishra & Koehler, 2006, p. 1017). Infrastructural prerequisites are the conditions that must hold before a tool can work in a classroom at all. That idea extends Peggy Ertmer’s first-order barriers, such as missing equipment, time, training and support (Ertmer, 1999, p. 50). Neither term is a label from DigCompEdu or UNESCO.

Other themes are quieter but appear throughout. Every chapter has a section called ‘When resources are thin’, so no idea assumes a well-equipped school. The book keeps teacher-facing uses apart from student-facing ones, because age limits and school rules restrict what students may use. Evidence carries honest labels as strong, emerging or contested. Tools appear only as dated examples, so the principles can outlast the products.

The book also looks from more than one place. Many of its studies come from England, the United States and Europe. Others come from Pakistan, India, Nigeria, Ghana, Kenya and Sierra Leone, where many classrooms work with far less. The book tries to treat neither setting as the normal case.

Two published frameworks guide the book. The first is DigCompEdu, the European framework for educators’ digital competence. It applies to educators at every level, from early childhood to universities and adult education (Redecker, 2017, p. 9). It describes 22 competences in six areas, and six stages of progress, from Newcomer to Pioneer (p. 9). The second is UNESCO’s AI competency framework for teachers. It combines five aspects of AI use with three levels, called Acquire, Deepen and Create (Miao & Cukurova, 2024, pp. 21–22). It says that teachers should stay responsible for teaching decisions, as long as governments, teacher education institutions and schools prepare and support them (p. 17). Each chapter also links to my own Infrastructure-First Digital Competence (IFDC) framework, which Chapter 1 introduces (Ilyas et al., 2026b). Most chapters end by naming their links to these frameworks, and a framework map at the back of the book gathers them.

How to read this book

You can read this book from cover to cover, and the argument builds that way. Many readers will not, and the book allows for that. Each chapter opens with a scene and a central question, builds its case and then turns to practice. Near its end sit four familiar features. ‘In practice’ gives worked examples to adapt, ‘When resources are thin’ shows the same idea with little or no technology, and ‘Questions for reflection’ invite a conversation with colleagues. Most worked examples are proposals, and the chapters say so, since nobody has yet tested them as programmes. ‘Framework connections’ closes each chapter.

If you need help on Monday, start with Chapter 2 and read its ground rules before you try any routine with AI. They take minutes to read, and the most important of them need no device. Then go straight to Part II, beginning with Chapter 4 on planning and Chapter 5 on protecting thinking, and keep Chapter 6 close when you set your next assessment. Read Chapter 8 soon after. Chapters 1 and 3 can wait for a quieter week.

With one shared phone, a weak signal or no device, read Chapter 7 alongside Part II. Then turn to ‘When resources are thin’ in each chapter. Many of the routines work on paper. Teacher-facing preparation needs no student account, and students can learn to question an answer that the teacher reads aloud or copies onto the board. No student needs a personal account to begin learning about AI, as Chapter 9 shows.

School leaders may start with Chapter 11, then read Chapters 8 and 10, since policy, safeguarding and staff learning meet there. University teachers can begin with Chapter 12 and then return to Chapters 5 and 6, where the theme of knowing first appears. Teacher educators and anyone planning training will find Chapter 10 the natural place to start, with the framework map close by. Readers who want the deeper argument first can read Chapters 3, 12 and 13 together, though Chapter 13 makes most sense after the rest.

Whatever your route, a few habits help. Treat every tool named here as a dated example, because products change faster than books. Check age limits and your school’s own rules on the day you act, since they differ by country and change often. Read the evidence labels as carefully as the advice. Where a chapter says a finding is emerging, it means just that.

A journey in four parts

Part I, Understanding the shift, sets the ground in three brisk chapters. Chapter 1, The Day the Homework Changed, asks what has really changed. Help became cheap, detection became a trap and finished work stopped reliably showing learning. What learning requires stayed the same.

Chapter 2, What AI Is, and What It Is Not, gives a working picture of how a language model produces text, and shows why fluent is not the same as true. It ends with nine ground rules for safe use, the habits to start with on Monday.

Chapter 3, The Part of Teaching No Machine Can Do, weighs relationships, judgement, care and modelling one by one. Its conclusion is careful. Machines can help with almost every task around the teaching relationship, but they cannot yet be the person in it.

Part II, AI in everyday teaching, is the practical heart of the book. Chapter 4, Planning with a Tireless Assistant, asks how much time AI really saves. Two English trials found real but modest savings, measured in minutes a week. The chapter introduces teacher-design burden and argues that a generated plan is not yet a lesson.

Chapter 5, Learning When Answers Are Free, separates doing a task with AI from learning it. Practice scores can rise while what a student can do alone stays flat or falls. The chapter proposes an order of work that needs no particular tool. Students try first, help comes next, and later they show without help what they can do.

Chapter 6, Fair Assessment in the AI Era, starts from validity, not from cheating. The question is what a piece of evidence lets us claim about a learner. The chapter keeps learning decisions apart from conduct decisions and turns to redesign through paired products and explanations, protected checks in class and structured questioning.

Chapter 7, Access, Equity and AI, asks who gains from AI and who gets left behind. Here infrastructural prerequisites move to the centre, from power and devices to language and support staff. It ends with routes for one teacher phone, a weak internet connection or no device.

Part III, Responsibility and growth, widens the view from the lesson to the school. Chapter 8, Safe, Fair and Honest, gives the fuller account of responsible use that Chapter 2 began. It covers privacy and permission, monitoring, bias, disclosure and students who turn to chatbots for advice or company, and it shows how much can begin before a school buys anything.

Chapter 9, Raising AI-Literate Learners, treats AI literacy as more than knowing which buttons to press. Students need to understand how a system produces an output, judge whether it deserves trust and create something whose choices they can explain. Its lesson designs can run on cards, paper and printed outputs.

Chapter 10, The Teacher as Learner, turns to the teacher’s own growth. Feeling trained is not the same as being competent, and learning a tool well costs hours before it saves minutes. The chapter asks who pays, and it reads DigCompEdu and UNESCO’s framework as maps for growth, never as certificates.

Chapter 11, The Whole School, closes Part III by asking how scattered experiments become shared practice. Shared practice grows where a school already has readiness, time and a reason, and it stalls when a mandate or a purchase comes first.

Part IV, The deeper question, asks what all of this means for knowing and for the teacher. Chapter 12, Real Understanding in the AI Era, speaks mainly to university teachers. It asks what it means to know something when machines can produce fluent answers, and what a degree can still certify. School teachers will find it useful too, since their students carry these habits into university.

Chapter 13, What Endures, is the shortest chapter and brings these themes together. It tests the claim of Chapter 3 against newer evidence and keeps it, in a narrower form. The teacher this era needs is an agent: someone who shapes tools rather than simply using them. It closes with a call for careful daily practice and a shared professional voice, and a short last word returns to the classroom where the book began.

After the last chapter, the back matter carries the practical weight. A glossary explains the book’s terms in plain words, and a framework map relates each chapter to the three frameworks. Four templates turn chapter routines into drafts to check against local rules. They are a classroom AI policy, a letter to families, an assessment redesign checklist and a set of planning prompts, followed by the full lesson cards from Chapters 7 and 9. The references come after the last word.

Before you turn the page

The questions in this book are old ones in new clothes. What does this student understand? Which help did they use, and what can they do alone? Who decided, and who will answer for it? AI has made those questions harder to dodge and, in some ways, easier to ask. A teacher who keeps asking them is doing work that this book argues should stay with people.

Chapter 1 begins where many teachers first felt the change, with homework. It asks what AI has changed in classrooms and what has stayed the same. Turn the page, and bring your own classroom with you.
