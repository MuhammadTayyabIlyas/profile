Appendix A. Glossary

This back matter answers one question. Where can a teacher turn for quick help? The glossary explains the book’s terms in plain words. A framework map then shows which chapters speak to which official competences. Four toolkit templates turn chapter routines into drafts a school can adapt. School teachers come first throughout, and higher education connections carry a clear label.

Each entry gives a plain meaning, often with a classroom example. Chapter numbers point to the fuller discussion. Two terms, infrastructural prerequisites and teacher-design burden, are my own analytical vocabulary, not labels from any framework.

Academic integrity. Honest work that shows what a learner actually did and knows. The book treats it mainly as a matter of task design and clear rules, with detection playing a small part (Chapters 6 and 12).

Accessibility. Whether a learner can use a resource, or show learning, despite a disability, a language barrier or missing equipment. A protected task should keep agreed access arrangements, such as a writing aid the student normally uses (Chapters 6 and 7).

AI Assessment Scale. A published five-level scale for deciding how much generative AI use an assessment task permits, from no AI to full AI (Perkins et al., 2024a). Its authors designed it for higher education, and Chapter 6 discusses it while the toolkit only points to the source.

AI companion. A chatbot built for ongoing personal or emotional conversation, sometimes presented as a friend. Chapters 3 and 8 treat students’ use of these tools as a safeguarding matter, so a teacher attends to the student before the product.

AI detector. Software that estimates whether a machine wrote a text, though its scores can be wrong, can fall after paraphrasing and are disputed for second-language writers. A score never proves misconduct by itself (Chapter 6).

AI literacy. Understanding how a system produces an output, deciding whether that output deserves acceptance and creating work whose choices you can explain. Chapter 9 maps how students build it from primary to secondary school, and Chapter 10 turns to teachers’ own version.

Artificial intelligence. A broad name for computer systems that do tasks we link with human thinking. Generative AI is only one kind, since adaptive practice tools and spam filters also use AI to classify, rank or recommend (Chapter 2).

Assessment validity. How far the evidence from an assessment supports the claim a teacher wants to make about a learner. A polished essay may show what a student can produce with help, not what they understand alone (Chapters 6 and 12).

Authentic assessment. A task that resembles how people use knowledge beyond the classroom, such as a real decision, project or performance. Authenticity alone does not prevent AI help, so the task still needs evidence of the learner’s own thinking (Chapters 6 and 12).

Authorship. Who can honestly claim a piece of work as their own. Chapter 12 separates where a product came from (originality), what help was used (disclosure) and what the learner can defend (ownership), and Chapter 9 adapts this for students.

Automation bias. Trusting an automated aid without checking it ourselves, which a major review found among novices and experts alike (Parasuraman & Manzey, 2010). Chapter 8 notes that other studies find a more mixed picture.

Bias. A systematic skew or unfair pattern in outputs or in how people are treated, which can stem from training data and from the people who tune a model. One example can reveal a problem, while several help a teacher judge whether a pattern exists (Chapters 2 and 8).

Chatbot. Conversational software that replies to typed or spoken messages, often built around a language model. Its friendly manner can make a reply feel more trustworthy than it is (Chapter 2).

Cognitive offloading. Using an action or tool to reduce the mental demand of a task, such as setting a phone reminder (Risko & Gilbert, 2016, p. 676). Offloading is often sensible, but it can remove practice a student needs when the task exists to build that very skill (Chapter 5).

Consent. Agreement given freely by someone who understands what they are agreeing to, and a separate question from data protection and safeguarding. A missing reply to a letter never counts as agreement (Chapter 8).

Deepfake. Realistic fake audio, images or video of a real person, made with AI. AI-generated sexual images of students go straight to the safeguarding route, and the teacher does not ask for a copy (Chapter 8).

Deskilling. Losing a skill because a machine now does the work, while upskilling inhibition means never gaining it because the machine always supplied it. This book’s reviewed sources establish no teacher outcome evidence on AI deskilling, so Chapter 10 suggests regular practice without AI.

Differentiation. Teachers adjusting tasks, support or pace so different learners can reach a shared goal. AI can draft versions at several levels, though evidence that this helps is thin, so the teacher still decides what each group needs (Chapters 1, 4 and 7).

DigCompEdu. The European Framework for the Digital Competence of Educators, setting out 22 competences in six areas and six proficiency levels from Newcomer to Pioneer (Redecker, 2017). Its author says it is not intended as a tool for performance appraisal (p. 28).

Digital competence. Confident, critical and responsible use of digital tools for work, learning and taking part in society. For teachers, DigCompEdu divides it into professional, teaching and learner-facing areas (Chapters 9 and 10).

Digital divide. Unequal access to devices, connection, skills and support, and the unequal benefit that follows. Chapter 7 traces three layers of access, skilled use and benefit, showing how equal headline use can hide unequal experience.

Digital resources. Materials in digital form that teachers find, create, adapt and share, from worksheets to video. DigCompEdu’s second area covers selecting, modifying and protecting them, and AI-generated material belongs here and needs checking before use (Chapter 4).

Disclosure. A clear statement of what an AI tool did and what the learner changed, which should not by itself trigger suspicion. In safeguarding, the word also means a student telling an adult about harm, which follows its own route (Chapter 8).

Epistemic injustice. Unfairness in whose knowledge others believe or hear. In higher education, Chapter 12 applies it to generative AI, whose quality varies by language, so second-language students can be doubly disadvantaged.

Feedback. Information about current work that helps a learner take a next step. AI can draft comments quickly, but a comment counts as evidence of progress only once the work itself changes (Chapters 6 and 10).

Formative assessment. Assessment whose evidence teachers and students actually use to decide what learning needs next, not simply the collection of more work (Chapter 6). A weekly quiz with devices closed and no marks recorded is one simple form (Chapter 5).

Generative AI. AI that produces new text, images, audio or code in response to a prompt, by predicting plausible output from patterns in its training data. Plausible is not the same as true (Chapter 2).

Hallucination. A fluent, confident output that is false, an ordinary result of predicting plausible text that shows most visibly as a fabricated reference (Chapter 2). One preprint traces it partly to training and evaluation that reward guessing over admitting uncertainty (Kalai et al., 2025).

Human accountability. The principle that a person stays answerable for decisions made with AI, which UNESCO’s framework applies to teachers’ pedagogical decisions (Miao & Cukurova, 2024, p. 17). It also names that framework’s Deepen block for the human-centred mindset (p. 22).

Human agency. People’s capacity to make and own their choices when working with AI. UNESCO’s framework names it the Acquire block of the human-centred mindset, where Chapter 3 sits (Miao & Cukurova, 2024, p. 22).

Inclusion. Arranging teaching so every learner can take part and progress, whatever their needs or background, a goal DigCompEdu pairs with accessibility in competence 5.1. An accessible worksheet does not settle a student’s part in the rest of the lesson (Chapter 7).

Information and media literacy. The ability to find, judge and use information well, including spotting weak or false claims, and DigCompEdu competence 6.1 covers teaching it. With AI, it includes checking a fluent answer against an independent source (Chapters 2, 5 and 9).

Infrastructural prerequisites. My own term, not a framework label, for the conditions a tool needs before it can work at all, such as electricity, a device and time to learn. It extends Ertmer’s first-order barriers, such as access, planning time and technical support (Ertmer, 1999, p. 48), and Chapters 4 and 7 develop it.

Large language model. A model trained on vast amounts of text to predict the next token in a sequence, whose fluency comes from patterns with no built-in check against reality. The model itself looks nothing up, though the product around it may search (Chapters 2 and 12).

Machine learning. A way of building AI in which a system learns patterns from examples instead of following hand-written rules. Students can see it at work by training and testing a simple model (Chapter 9).

Oral assessment. Assessment through talk, from a quick question at the desk to a short planned conversation, in which a learner shows understanding live. Fluent speakers can seem to know more than they do, so a good rubric separates language from knowing (Chapters 6 and 12).

Personal data. Information that identifies a person or could do so, such as a name, mark or health detail. The first ground rule keeps anything that identifies a student out of tools the school has not approved (Chapter 2).

Personalisation. Tailoring difficulty, order, pace or presentation to an individual learner, usually by software. Chapter 7 asks whom it actually serves, since every group might improve while the gap between groups grows.

Process evidence. Evidence of how a piece of work developed, such as plans, drafts, notes and revisions, which helps a teacher see the learner’s thinking. It also adds work, and a learner can still produce it with help (Chapters 6, 9 and 12).

Professional agency. Teachers’ capacity to act on their own judgement, shape their work and sometimes say no. Chapter 13 shows that it grows in conditions a school can build, such as time and a real voice in decisions.

Professional learning community. A group of teachers who learn together over time by examining practice and students’ work, which some systems build into the weekly timetable. Where meetings are hard, a WhatsApp group of a few teachers in one tehsil can be a first step (Chapters 10 and 11).

Prompt. The instruction or question a user gives an AI tool, ideally stating the goal, context and limits. It remains a request, and no prompt guarantees an accurate answer (Chapter 4).

Reflective practice. Looking back on your own teaching to understand and improve it, which DigCompEdu places in professional engagement as competence 1.3. In the framework map, more chapters link to it than to any other competence.

Responsible use. Using AI safely, fairly and honestly, with attention to wellbeing and risk (Chapter 8). DigCompEdu’s competence 6.4 asks teachers to help learners use technology this way.

Retrieval practice. Working to recall what you have learned, instead of simply rereading it, which helps students remember more (Chapter 1). A short quiz from memory also shows what a student can do without help (Chapter 5).

Rubric. A set of criteria and quality descriptions for judging work, ideally separating the features that matter, such as interpreting evidence and presenting an argument. Colleagues compare samples before relying on it (Chapter 6).

Safeguarding. The work of protecting children from harm and acting on concerns. Any worry goes to the school’s safeguarding lead through the agreed route, never to a chatbot (Chapters 3 and 8).

Self-regulated learning. Learners planning, monitoring and adjusting their own learning, which appears in this book as DigCompEdu competence 3.4. Chapter 5 links it to deciding when and how to offer help.

Teacher-design burden. My own term, not a framework label, for the effort a teacher must put in before a tool helps learning, as distinct from simply having access to the tool. It grows from TPACK (Chapter 4).

Testimony. Knowledge we gain by trusting what others tell us, from teachers to textbooks. Chapter 12, a higher education discussion, asks whether a language model can be such a source and what trusting it would require.

Token. A small piece of text that a language model reads and produces, such as a word, part of a word or one character. The same passage can need far more tokens in one language than another, which raises cost and slows replies (Chapter 2).

TPACK. Technological pedagogical content knowledge, the knowledge a teacher needs to fit a tool to a subject and to learners, which the original paper calls TPCK (Mishra & Koehler, 2006). It grounds teacher-design burden (Chapter 4).

Training and testing data. In a classroom model-building activity, the examples a model learns from and the separate examples kept back to check it. Fresh test examples show whether the model works beyond what it has seen (Chapter 9).

Training data. The large body of text, images or other examples from which a model learns its patterns. Gaps and slants in that data shape the model’s errors and bias (Chapter 2).

Unaided performance. What a learner can do without AI or other help, often checked later. Practice scores with a tool can rise while unaided performance stays flat, so the book keeps the two apart (Chapters 5 and 12).

UNESCO AI competency framework for teachers. UNESCO’s 2024 framework of fifteen competency blocks across five aspects and three progression levels, Acquire, Deepen and Create (Miao & Cukurova, 2024, p. 22). It offers a reference pathway, not fixed steps, and the framework map uses it.
