Chapter 7. Access, Equity and AI

A teacher in Islamabad wanted to use the lesson plans. The phone would not cooperate. In a focus group, the teacher described trying to open the app while the accompanying training also required online access. ‘I tried till 2 AM at night’ (Aslam et al., 2025, p. 59).

That is all the source tells us about the scene. We know nothing about the room, or the lesson planned for the next morning. But two things are clear. The teacher wanted to use the plans, but the technology would not allow it.

The account comes from EdTech Hub’s evaluation of AI-generated lesson plans made by Taleemabad for the NIETE programme. GSMA funded the research, through an innovation programme paid for by the UK. After teachers asked again and again, the app was changed so that they could save plans offline, although the training still needed a connection (Aslam et al., 2025, p. 59). The change met a practical need. A lesson plan cannot help anyone while it is stuck inside an app that will not open.

Chapter 5 promised to return to who gains from AI and who loses out. Here is that question in full. Who gains from AI, and who gets left behind?

Personalisation offers one answer. A tool might change practice to fit a student’s needs, read text aloud or help a family understand a school message. Each of these is worth a close look. Each also depends on conditions that teachers cannot take for granted.

A student needs access at the right time. Someone must check whether the tool understands the language and whether its interface works with the student’s support. Teachers need time to make the resource useful. Schools must notice whose learning improves after all that effort.

Wanting to use a tool and being able to use it are different things.

I heard the same gap in my own interviews. A botanist wanted her plant anatomy students to see plant structures on video. She and her colleagues had arranged four or five screens on their own. Sometimes a student pulled out a cable. Sometimes the connection failed.

‘What other difficulties do you face?’ I asked.

‘The biggest issue is comfort,’ she said. ‘Sometimes the fans are working, sometimes not … In the cold weather there’s no heater. If students aren’t comfortable, they can’t really learn’ (Ilyas, 2026, Participant L5).

A teacher educator added the biggest problem of all: ‘four to six hours of load-shedding’. Load-shedding means planned power cuts, and here they happened even during university hours (Ilyas, 2026, Participant L3).

Before a tool can help, the room has to work.

This chapter follows the gap between having a tool and being able to use it. It looks at personalisation, disability, language and unequal access. Then it asks what teachers can do with the resources they really have.

What must hold before a tool helps

Chapter 4 named the conditions a tool needs before it can work at all. It called them infrastructural prerequisites. In this chapter they move from the edge of the discussion to its centre.

An older idea from Peggy Ertmer helps here. Building on Brickner’s work, she described first-order barriers, which lie outside the teacher: access, planning time and technical support (Ertmer, 1999, p. 48). Second-order barriers lie inside the teacher. They concern beliefs about teaching and learning. Beliefs and practice do not always match. In a survey of 150 primary and elementary teachers in Gujranwala, most held progressivist or perennialist beliefs, yet most taught in an essentialist way (Ilyas & Zaman, 2023, p. 1843). Put simply, what teachers believed about learning was often not what they did in class, where most taught mainly by passing on set knowledge and skills. A teacher can face both kinds of barrier at once. Training that builds confidence will achieve little if the teacher still cannot open the resource.

The distinction helps us avoid a familiar mistake. We can mistake a missing condition for a person’s unwillingness. A teacher who abandons an unreliable app may make a sensible decision about tomorrow’s lesson. The Islamabad teacher at the start of this chapter is one example.

My own doctoral research with university teachers in Pakistan gives this frame another grounding. Participants were university teachers, and their accounts concern higher education. One described technology provision in very plain terms. ‘We don’t have much technology’ (Ilyas, 2026, p. 163, Participant M02). The teacher then described a single computer used for entering examination grades.

Elsewhere, the thesis describes reliable access as a condition for developing competence. Teachers need opportunities to experiment without expecting equipment failure (Ilyas, 2026, p. 222). A journal article from the same study sets out this infrastructure-first framework. It treats infrastructure and institutional support as the conditions under which teachers express and develop competence (Ilyas et al., 2026b). Its proposed framework comes from one institution. I present it as an empirically grounded proposition, with wider applicability still open to testing (p. 223).

The school evidence makes the material question equally hard to avoid. ASER’s brief reports internet access in 14% of rural government schools in Pakistan. Only 12% had a computer lab (Idara-e-Taleem-o-Aagahi, 2024, p. 3). These figures describe the surveyed schools in the 2023 data. They cannot tell us whether a connection works throughout a lesson. Before the lesson, test the tool in the same room at the same time of day. Keep a paper version of the task ready.

A school can own a device and still lack usable access. The battery may need charging. A repair may take weeks. Its screen may be too small for the material, or the available software may lack the language students use. Ownership answers only the first question.

Costs go on after the device is bought. ITU compares the price of a small mobile data allowance with national income per person. The figure for low-income countries is far higher than the figure for high-income countries (International Telecommunication Union, 2025b, p. 11). Calling a chatbot free says little about what it costs a family to reach it.

The strongest new generative AI trial in this chapter supplied much more than a login. Google’s Sierra Leone trial placed a field monitor in every participating school. Monitors charged devices and helped teachers resolve technical problems (LearnLM Team et al., 2026, p. 10). That support belongs inside our account of the intervention. A school copying the tool alone would copy only part of what researchers tested.

Prerequisites also include accessibility. A connection does little for a student who cannot use the interface. Nor does a translated resource help if its meaning has changed. These conditions sit beside power and hardware. Each can stop a student from taking part before any learning starts.

Yet providing them does not guarantee learning. In Peru, an experiment with researchers from the Inter-American Development Bank tested One Laptop per Child in rural primary schools. Many more computers became available. Yet mathematics and language scores showed no gain that the study could detect (Cristia et al., 2017, pp. 296–297). The experiment was well designed, but it supports only a narrow conclusion. In that setting, equipment alone did not produce those subject gains.

The Global Education Evidence Advisory Panel reaches a similar judgement about spending. Its report warns against buying hardware without thinking about how people use it (Akyeampong et al., 2023). The panel brings together evidence for major public funders. In its view, adaptive software sits inside a wider set of conditions. These include the equipment a school already has, teacher training and good content to use.

Think of prerequisites as conditions the school must keep supplying. A connection that works on the day it is installed does not settle access for the term. Someone must check charging, repairs, account access and whether accessible materials are still there. This job needs a named person and some time in the working week.

This also changes how we read a successful pilot. If researchers supply technical support, a school should ask who will provide it after they leave. Counting the device while forgetting the person would understate the intervention. A cheaper version may simply move the missing work onto teachers.

That leaves two duties side by side. Institutions must provide what makes it possible for students to take part. Teachers and schools must then judge what makes taking part worthwhile. The second duty is no excuse for neglecting the first.

Personalisation for whom

Personalisation sounds simple when a vendor describes it. Each student receives what they need next. In practice, tools personalise different things. Some change the difficulty of questions. Others change the order, pace or presentation of material. A chatbot may change its explanation after a student asks again.

Those differences matter when we read the evidence. An adaptive practice system can choose from content that has been checked. A generative model produces new text. Evidence for the first does not show the value of the second. Chapter 5 looked at how tutor design affects thinking. Here the further question is which students gain from that design.

An EdTech Hub-funded meta-analysis provides strong evidence about older personalised technologies in lower-income settings. Major and colleagues combined 16 randomised trials. The average gain was 0.18 standard deviations (Major et al., 2021). That is a modest positive effect across varied programmes. It gives us reason to investigate well-designed uses, without promising a tutor for every child.

Tools that adapted to each student’s level showed larger effects than less personalised designs. The review also notes that technology often gave students more time to learn (Major et al., 2021). More practice may explain some of the gain. We should keep that possibility in mind beside any claim about how clever the software is.

The same review makes an easy claim about teachers harder to hold. It found no significant difference between programmes that used technology only and those that combined teachers with technology (Major et al., 2021). It cannot show that taking a teacher out of a given programme would leave its results the same. Nor does it prove that a teacher’s part always improves outcomes. As a teacher, I would not read this finding as permission to leave students alone with software. It compares different programmes. It says nothing about which students in a class are being missed.

A newer trial in Kenya gives a clearer picture. Major and colleagues studied EIDU, a learning app, in government pre-primary classrooms. Children used shared Android devices alongside their normal lessons. The programme was more than the app. It also included digital versions of the Tayari lesson plans and trained support staff (Major et al., 2026, pp. 485–486).

The research was funded through EdTech Hub by the UK’s Foreign, Commonwealth and Development Office, and one of the authors works for EIDU. The software adjusted to each child, but it did not use generative AI or chat. The trial found a large effect, 0.534 standard deviations (Major et al., 2026, p. 494). In education research, an effect of that size is unusually big. Because the trial was well designed, it gives strong evidence that this whole package worked in those classrooms.

The pattern over time deserves equal space. Most of the gain appeared before the midline assessment. The later period showed no significant additional advantage (Major et al., 2026, p. 495). Researchers discuss exhaustion of suitable content as a possible explanation. Personalisation cannot select useful material after useful material runs short. This suggests a simple check. After the first weeks with an adaptive tool, look at whether students are still meeting new material, or only repeating work they have already done. If the second is true, the time may be better spent on other teaching.

The equity findings also resist a simple headline. Researchers found no clear difference in treatment effects between lower and higher baseline attainment groups. Girls and boys benefited similarly (Major et al., 2026, pp. 495–496). Those findings support neither a widening nor a narrowing claim within the groups tested. The paper does not establish benefits for disabled children separately.

Google’s Sierra Leone findings point another way. Its partners tested Gemini Guided Learning in junior secondary mathematics classrooms, and EducAid helped run the trial. Funding came from Google.org and the Gates Foundation. Its technical report gives emerging evidence from a short trial that the vendor ran.

On average, the effect was 0.258 standard deviations. But the range of uncertainty was wide, from 0.027 to 0.488 (LearnLM Team et al., 2026, p. 3). That range fits both a very small gain and a large one. The report also turns the result into years of learning, but that would suggest more precision than the evidence allows.

Students who were stronger in mathematics before the trial gained more. The report also shows differences between grades, but it used an analysis that changed from its first plan (LearnLM Team et al., 2026, p. 28). We should treat those subgroup findings with care. Together, they question the idea that chat-based help will by itself give the most to the learner furthest behind.

An average and a gap answer different questions. Every group might improve while the distance between groups grows. Alternatively, equal gains might leave a large existing gap intact. A school that wants to reduce inequality needs to look beyond its overall average.

Imagine judging a reading resource after a short classroom trial. The class average rises, but the students who need the most support rarely finish a session. If you looked only at the average, you would want to expand its use. Those students’ experience would tell the school to fix their participation first. Both pieces of evidence belong in the decision.

The level a system assigns should remain open to challenge. A wrong answer might reflect unfamiliar vocabulary, trouble using the screen or an interruption. It need not reveal a settled limit in the student’s understanding. Teachers need access to the task and an ordinary way to revise the decision.

Ask what happens after a learner succeeds too. Does the system offer richer reasoning, or simply more questions at the same level? A student should have opportunities to move beyond the category the tool first assigned. Personalisation serves little purpose if yesterday’s difficulty becomes tomorrow’s fixed ceiling.

Individual practice also needs a route back into shared learning. A student who practises a prerequisite skill should still return to the class’s conversation. Otherwise, adapting work can quietly become assigning a narrower education. The teacher must keep that larger learning purpose in view.

Disability and the right kind of help

For a student who struggles to read printed text, hearing it aloud may open a lesson. Another student may need a visual sequence or a way to communicate without typing. These needs differ. Special educational needs and disabilities, often shortened to SEND, do not describe one learner or one useful tool.

Assistive technology also extends well beyond generative AI. Text-to-speech reads existing text aloud. Speech-to-text turns spoken words into writing. Other tools support communication or access to the screen. A school should compare these specific functions before choosing a product because it carries an AI label.

Wood and colleagues reviewed text-to-speech and related read-aloud support for learners with reading disabilities. Their meta-analysis found a positive comprehension effect of 0.35 standard deviations (Wood et al., 2018, p. 77). An adjustment for possible publication bias reduced it to 0.24. The review included school, postsecondary and adult samples, and lacked randomised controlled trials.

This is a firmer starting point than the evidence for generative AI for students with special educational needs and disabilities. Questions remain about the learner, the task and the support. Listening to a passage read aloud may help a student understand it. That is not the same as helping the student learn to read the words alone. A teacher needs to know which of these the lesson is for.

Chapter 5 warned about protecting thinking, and that warning needs this qualification. Removing a barrier to access can make thinking possible. Reading aloud in a history discussion may let a student reason about the evidence. The same support in a task that tests decoding would change what the result means. Chapter 6’s concern with assessment still applies here.

England’s Department for Education asked IFF Research to evaluate training in assistive technology. In its small follow-up survey, staff said the training helped students become more independent. The figure was 92%, out of 36 staff (IFF Research, 2024, p. 58). This is emerging evidence from what staff believed. It is not an independent measure of change in attainment.

The same evaluation found limited staff time remained a major barrier after training. Again, the follow-up base was small (IFF Research, 2024, p. 50). Knowing a feature exists differs from helping a child use it comfortably. Staff may need to adjust the voice, prepare the document and teach the routine.

This is the teacher-design burden of Chapter 4. Someone must turn an available feature into a workable form of support.

The burden also shows up in individual education plans. Waterfield and colleagues asked US special education teachers to write goals with and without ChatGPT. Teachers worked from a made-up student case. Raters found no significant difference in quality between goals written by teachers alone and goals written by teachers with AI (Waterfield et al., 2026, p. 63). The study judged the plans, but it did not test children’s learning.

That result does not rule out useful help with drafting. It does question the idea that fluent goals show better provision. A plan must match what the child can do now, the support they have and the chances teachers can give. A well-written sentence cannot make up for observations nobody has made.

Older research on visual activity schedules offers another useful boundary. Knight and colleagues found support for schedules, usually alongside systematic instruction in using them (Knight et al., 2015, p. 171). The evidence concerns the taught practice. It does not establish the effectiveness of a schedule produced by a chatbot. If a teacher uses a chatbot to draft a schedule, the part to plan most carefully is how the student will be taught to use it.

Schools should also ask how support feels to the student. In IFF’s evaluation, a staff member valued making tools available across the class. Students needing support could use them without feeling singled out (IFF Research, 2024, p. 69). The account gives a practical reason to teach accessible features openly. Availability can become ordinary classroom provision.

Begin a small accessibility trial with the student’s account of the difficulty. Agree what improvement would look like together. It might mean joining discussion, completing work with less adult prompting or choosing a book independently. Keep the support that helps, and review any feature the student finds tiring or embarrassing. Their experience belongs beside the teacher’s observation.

Review access support differently from temporary teaching prompts. A student may gradually need fewer hints while learning a method. That does not mean the student should lose the communication device or reading support that lets them participate. Independence can include choosing and using an aid well.

Consider a student who dictates an explanation. The teacher can listen for the reasoning and check the written text with the student. A wrong transcription may hide a sound idea. In the same way, a fluent transcription does not show that the explanation makes sense. Talking it through in class helps tell these cases apart.

Where specialist advice is scarce, teachers can still describe the barrier exactly and ask for help with a narrow question. They should not let a chatbot turn that description into a diagnosis. An AI explanation of behaviour may miss the classroom conditions or what the child says. Observe first, and be open about what is still uncertain.

Support also belongs in shared activities. A student who receives accessible individual practice still needs a way to contribute to group discussion. Ask whether classmates can hear, read or respond to that contribution. An accessible worksheet does not settle participation in the rest of the lesson.

Among the sources I hold, the evidence that generative AI improves results for students with SEND is still thin and disputed. No school trial among them shows those benefits across different disability groups. The careful route starts with one specific barrier and one specific support. It keeps specialists, the student and the family involved.

A language the learner understands

A tool may offer a language in its menu without serving that language well. Teachers in multilingual classrooms need a more demanding test. Does the explanation preserve the meaning? Can the student use it to discuss the subject? Will a family understand what the school asks them to do?

UNESCO’s multilingual education guidance puts comprehensible teaching at the centre of access. It describes learning through languages students understand as an educational concern extending beyond technology (UNESCO, 2025a). AI translation is one part of that concern. It cannot replace the work of recognising languages already present in a classroom.

Pakistan makes the issue easy to see. A resource may move between English, Urdu and students’ home languages. Even if each word were translated correctly, someone would still have to decide on familiar examples, script and subject vocabulary. Those decisions need knowledge of the learners. Making another version is only the start of the work.

Microsoft researchers tested OpenAI models across languages in the MEGA benchmark. On one language inference task, GPT-4 scored 84.9 in English and 68.1 in Urdu (Ahuja et al., 2023, p. 4263). These are benchmark results from 2023. They do not measure learning in classrooms today, or the quality of every Urdu explanation.

Their value lies in challenging an assumption. Support for a language does not establish equal performance across languages. A later model may change the size of a gap. Teachers still need checks on the task they plan to use. An English demonstration cannot settle whether a particular Urdu resource will work.

A school communication study gives the concern a concrete form. Researchers from UNSW Sydney and the New South Wales education department examined the department’s NSWEduChat translations. One example began as a request for parents to park legally. The Khmer version shifted to banning dogs on school grounds, as revealed through back-translation (Srirag et al., 2025, p. 13).

That example needs careful handling. Researchers used another model to translate the text back into English. Back-translation can expose a major shift, though it does not replace a competent human review. The study provides emerging evidence about one tool and a small collection of messages. It does not establish that every translation into Khmer will fail.

For teachers, the practical risk is clear. A polished message can carry the wrong request. Families may never know the English original. The member of staff who checks only the English can mistake fluency for a faithful translation. A second translation by the same kind of system offers limited reassurance.

Evidence on machine translation in language learning also needs a clear label for who was studied. Klimova and colleagues reviewed studies drawn mostly from universities. Their review suggests that advanced language learners can use translation tools better (Klimova et al., 2023). Learners need enough knowledge to notice errors. These findings cannot show benefits for younger students who are taught in a school language they do not know well. With younger students, a translation tool works better as a support the teacher checks than as something students rely on alone.

The difference between access and instruction matters again. Translating a science instruction may let a student begin the investigation. Asking AI to write the student’s English composition may remove the language practice. A teacher can allow the first and set limits on the second. Chapter 5 gives the learning reason for that choice.

A multilingual resource should keep a route between languages open. For example, a student might discuss an idea in a familiar language, label a diagram and then practise the school’s subject terms. The teacher checks whether the student understands the relationship between those terms. Simplifying every version until the subject disappears would offer little help.

Translation can also change relationships with families. Asking a parent to correct an unclear message should never make them responsible for the school’s accuracy. Their response can reveal a problem. The school still owns the communication and must arrange a reliable way to resolve it.

Our sources leave major gaps here. They offer no learning evaluation in Urdu-medium school classrooms and no trial of AI-translated messages for Urdu-speaking parents in British schools. That absence calls for modest claims. Teachers can test a limited use while preserving a human route for questions and corrections.

Access, use and unequal benefit

The digital divide has more than one layer. First comes reaching a device and connection. Next comes using them with enough skill and freedom to achieve a purpose. Finally comes the benefit that use brings. A school can improve the first layer while leaving the others almost untouched.

Figure 7.1. Three layers of the digital divide: access, use and benefit. A school can widen the outer ring and leave the inner ones unchanged.

Research with Dutch adults helped name that final layer. Van Deursen and Helsper studied who gained benefits offline from being online. Their survey linked higher social status with greater benefits (van Deursen & Helsper, 2015). This is evidence from adults reporting on themselves in a wealthy country. It gives a useful distinction, but no estimate for Pakistani students.

School survey evidence shows why the difference helps. The National Literacy Trust asked teenagers about their use of generative AI. Students on free school meals (a marker of low family income in England) used it almost exactly as often as their peers. The figures were 66.7% and 67.0% (Picton et al., 2025, p. 2). This survey gives emerging evidence about the use students report. It says nothing about learning outcomes.

Frequency and reported help differed. Students receiving free school meals used AI weekly somewhat less often. They also reported less help with learning when adults were unavailable (Picton et al., 2025, pp. 2, 4). Equal headline use did not mean identical experience. Neither measure tells us whose understanding improved. This survey shows the layers of the divide described above. Use was equal, but reported benefit was not. So asking students whether they use AI tells a teacher little. It is more useful to ask how often they use it, for what, and whether it helped them when no adult was there.

Income can shape support inside the school too. Chapter 4 described unequal access to staff training. That gap carries into this chapter whenever teachers must judge a language version or set up an accessibility feature. A student may sit in a connected classroom with a teacher who has had little support to prepare. A list of equipment would miss the gap.

Pakistan’s household statistics show a different kind of hidden inequality. In the latest held HIES social report, women and men each recorded 57% internet use. The measure covers anyone aged ten or older using the internet within three months (Pakistan Bureau of Statistics, 2025, p. 107). It says little about daily freedom to use a device.

Phone ownership gives another picture. In the same age range, 69% of men owned a phone, against 31% of women. In rural Sindh, 11% of women owned one (Pakistan Bureau of Statistics, 2025, p. 98). These official figures give strong descriptive evidence. They do not measure girls’ AI use or explain why each household decides as it does.

The figures do not contradict each other. Someone can use the internet on a borrowed phone and still not own a phone. A task that needs a personal account every evening assumes control that occasional access cannot give. Schools need to ask about that control before they set the task.

The mobile industry body GSMA offers related survey evidence on adults. Among Pakistani women who used mobile internet, 35% used it only on someone else’s phone. For men the figure was 6% (Jeffrie, 2025, p. 36). Its Indian figures also show women borrowing phones more often than men. These surveys concern adults, so they cannot tell us how large the gap is among school students.

They can still warn us about household assumptions. The phone a parent lists may travel to work with someone else. Another family member may decide when a child can use it. A student’s access can change during the week. Asking whether a home has a smartphone will miss much of that variation.

Where people live also makes a difference. ASER’s figures for rural schools show the real level of access, which demonstrations in cities often take for granted. A national average can hide differences between provinces, and between rural and urban homes. Pakistan’s official tables show these differences clearly (Pakistan Bureau of Statistics, 2025, pp. 98, 107). A school should start with the conditions of its own families.

Disability, language, income and gender also meet in the same lives. A learner may need spoken text on a phone they share, in a language its voice handles poorly. Separate solutions can fail when combined. Giving that student an English audio file would leave both the language problem and the access problem unresolved.

Schools should treat a missing digital task as a reason to ask about access before judging effort. A private conversation can establish what happened without demanding a public account of poverty. Offer the classroom route at the same time as the home route. Nobody should need to announce hardship to receive the learning material.

Schools can learn about access without collecting a detailed account of family finances. Ask what the student can use, when they can use it and which alternative would work. Keep the information within the staff who need it. Review it when circumstances change.

That enquiry should include adults’ time. A digital homework route may assume a parent can translate instructions or supervise a long session. Families differ in the time and confidence they can offer. Schools should keep essential teaching within a route the student can reliably reach, with support available there.

More use is also not a sure sign of fairness. A student might spend longer with a tool because its explanations keep failing. Another may finish quickly because a teacher prepared the resource with care. Time online cannot stand in for opportunity or learning. Chapter 6’s attention to meaningful evidence belongs here too, when we talk about fairness.

My cautious starting point is a common learning entitlement with more than one way to reach it. Students should know the shared goal and the support available. Schools can then ask who participates, who learns and who carries extra costs. Each answer may point to a different repair.

In a university course. Before requiring any AI tool, check who can actually use it. Ask students about their device, data and connection, and offer a route without AI that can earn the same marks. Where licences are too expensive, choose free or open tools that every student can reach.

When resources are thin

Working with fewer devices still needs careful design. It should also have an evidence label. The source set contains no learning trial of one teacher phone supplying generative AI to an entire low-resource classroom. These examples extend the research cautiously. They should not carry the results of better-equipped trials.

As the start of this chapter showed, teachers in the Islamabad evaluation asked for plans they could save offline (Aslam et al., 2025, p. 59). Can teachers save the materials and use them without the internet? That is a useful first question when choosing any tool for a school with a weak connection. The answer says nothing about learning, but it decides whether the tool can be used at all.

The botanist from the start of this chapter also sent material out ahead of time, but in a different way. Videos went out through WhatsApp before teaching, in case of equipment problems in class (Ilyas, 2026, p. 157, Participant L5). The Islamic Studies lecturer in my study kept paper copies in case the electricity or internet failed (Ilyas, 2026, p. 157, Participant M04). These are two teachers who dealt with the problem in different ways. Neither of them assumes that a live connection will hold.

  • With one teacher phone, prepare while the connection works. A primary teacher might save a checked passage, diagram and questions before school. During the lesson, she can copy the passage onto the board or use a printed master. Students work with the material together. Their learning does not depend on taking turns holding her phone.

  • A secondary teacher might save a worked explanation and deliberately choose a question students will discuss. She keeps the textbook beside it when checking the draft. At school, students compare the explanation with their own reasoning. If power fails, the paper copy preserves the planned discussion.

  • Where a few devices exist, protect turns for students who cannot use one at home. Keep any accessibility support available during those turns. Device allocation should follow learning needs and available alternatives. The most confident volunteers should not quietly become the only users.

  • When the internet connection is unreliable, the lesson needs a stopping rule. After a brief attempt to reconnect, move to the prepared alternative. Teachers should not have to sacrifice the whole class’s time to an uncertain repair. Schools can record recurring faults and assign responsibility for resolving them.

Offline generative AI offers another possible route, with substantial prerequisites of its own. An independently funded Telangana pilot ran a local model on desktop computers. Its 19 high school students reported more confidence and autonomy, though the study did not assess learning gains (Gattupalli et al., 2025). This emerging evidence shows a technical possibility. It cannot justify buying a lab on a promise of improved attainment.

There is stronger evidence, from an experiment, for a simpler channel. When schools in Botswana were closed, Angrist and colleagues tested text messages combined with weekly calls. The calls lasted 15 to 20 minutes. Learning improved by 0.121 standard deviations, while messages alone produced no significant gain (Angrist et al., 2022, p. 941). This was human support through basic phones, without AI.

Its relevance lies in the comparison schools should make. When a family has only a basic phone, a short human conversation may offer a better starting point than a chatbot. The Botswana result came during school closure. It should guide consideration of a route, without promising the same effect in every setting.

EdTech Hub’s rapid review also examines educational radio in low-resource settings. Damani and Mitchell describe uses that combine audio with guided participation. Their review warns that audio-only provision can exclude deaf learners (Damani & Mitchell, 2020). Low technology can reproduce exclusion too. A fallback needs its own accessibility check.

  • With no connection and no student devices, keep the learning goal and change the delivery. Use shared reading, objects, diagrams, oral explanation and paper practice. Where organised radio or recorded audio exists, prepare pauses for discussion and a print or visual alternative. A teacher or facilitator still checks understanding.

These routes ask schools to spend according to the barrier. A missing translation check needs language expertise. An inaccessible text may need a readable copy or audio support. A broken charger needs replacement. Buying another subscription answers none of those problems.

This chapter’s answer begins with those ordinary conditions. AI can help some learners under some designs, while leaving others with fewer chances to participate. Chapter 8 takes up the responsibility that follows. What does safe, fair and honest use require when a school chooses tools on children’s behalf?

In IFDC terms, this whole chapter is about the bottom level, the basic conditions (Ilyas et al., 2026b). Power, devices, connections and accessible materials are not extras. They decide whether a teacher can use their skills at all. Providing them is the institution’s job, not the teacher’s.

Questions for reflection

Use these questions with colleagues, keeping a particular class in mind.

  1. Think of one AI tool you plan to use with your class: what must be in place for it to work, and which of your students do not have that?

  2. What would show that a tool improves access, and what further evidence would show learning?

  3. Whose language, disability or shared device might your usual classroom resource overlook?

  4. If the average result improves, how will you check whether the students needing most support also benefit?

  5. What equivalent route can students take when the connection fails or their family cannot provide a device?

Framework connections

In DigCompEdu, this chapter belongs to empowering learners: accessibility and inclusion, and differentiation and personalisation (Redecker, 2017, pp. 70, 72). These competences ask teachers to think about each learner’s conditions and offer suitable alternatives. UNESCO’s AI competency framework for teachers links the careful use of AI with equity, inclusion and teaching that fits different learners (Miao & Cukurova, 2024, p. 26). Its social responsibility competence adds inclusive societies and social justice (p. 38). Both frameworks support asking who benefits before a school counts a new tool as a success.
