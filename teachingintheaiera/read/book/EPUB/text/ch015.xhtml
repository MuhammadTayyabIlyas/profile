Chapter 13. What Endures

Katie Sanchez’s first instinct was to hand her students a chatbot. She teaches third grade in Sacramento, California, and she had joined a professional learning group on AI. A chatbot could give her young writers feedback on their work. Then she stopped. She liked reading their writing herself. ‘I like to sit down and confer with them,’ she said (Waite et al., 2025, p. 1). So she kept that ‘human connection’ for herself, and gave the machine part of her lesson planning instead.

She did not refuse the tool. She did not hand it to her students either. She decided which part of her work was hers to keep, and why. Every chapter of this book has been leading to that kind of decision.

Her story comes from a report on 18 California schools, most of them charter schools, that explored AI together. The Silicon Schools Fund organised and paid for the group, and it also paid for the report (Waite et al., 2025). I treat this as one teacher’s story in a report paid for by the group’s own funder. It shows what a careful decision can look like. It does not show how many teachers make such decisions, or what conditions they need to make them.

Her decision was small, but it answers the question this last chapter asks. What kind of teacher does the AI era need?

First, the claim of Chapter 3 survives the newer evidence, but in a narrower form. Second, this era needs teachers who shape their tools, and teachers can do that only where the conditions allow it. Third, teachers remain answerable for what a machine helps to produce, and that responsibility must come with matching power. The chapter ends with a call that joins each teacher’s daily habits to a shared professional voice.

Testing the claim from Chapter 3

Chapter 3 argued that teaching rests on relationships, judgement, care and the example a teacher sets. It named three kinds of study that would change the claim. One would compare AI tutoring with a teacher who knows the class over a full year. Another would show a machine noticing a child’s distress and responding safely over time. A third would track whether hours saved by AI go back to students. No study I have found answers any of the three.

What has arrived is smaller and mixed. In one study of 173 students and six teachers, teachers’ closer checks on AI analytics went with students’ learning gains (Karumbaiah et al., 2023, p. 1043). It is correlational and short, so the evidence is emerging. A design account adds a telling case. Lumilo, a classroom tool shown in a teacher’s smart glasses, flagged a seventh grader as possibly off task (Holstein & Aleven, 2022, p. 6). The teacher knew the student and judged the behaviour out of character. A quiet question revealed a break-up the weekend before. The machine saw behaviour, and the teacher found the reason.

Safeguarding shows a gap. A RAND report found that no studies had examined the accuracy of the AI suicide-risk monitoring used in schools (Ayer et al., 2023, p. 9). Chapter 8 looks at school monitoring more closely. On modelling, the newer material comes from interviews, and I have found no study that observes it in a school classroom. An adult English teacher called Jane taught learners how to use a tool ‘and also what some of the dangers are, as well’ (Barnes & Tour, 2026, p. 31).

One trial points the other way, and it needs a careful label. It is one unreviewed study of one tool in one country, and it says nothing about teachers who use AI with care. Sungu and colleagues randomly assigned school departments in a Turkish school chain to a GPT-4o assistant for one semester. Their analysis covers 193 teachers in 14 schools (Sungu et al., 2026, p. 4). Exam scores were already high, with a control-group mean of 89.2 out of 100 (p. 8). With a median of two prompts, teachers ‘typically accepted AI-generated outputs with minimal iteration’ (p. 11). Their students’ intrinsic motivation fell by about a tenth of a standard deviation (p. 6). This result is a warning about one way of using AI, not about AI in general. The practical step I take from it is simple: read an AI draft closely and change what does not fit your class before it reaches students.

Time saved remains the weakest link, since no study in my reading follows saved minutes into time with students. The EEF trial measured a real saving of about 25 minutes a week on science lesson preparation (Roy et al., 2024, p. 4). Even so, only 31 of the 68 survey respondents who had used ChatGPT felt it saved them time (p. 73). That arm of the trial held 129 teachers (p. 4). Chapters 3 and 4 met the larger Gallup figure, a self-estimate of 5.9 hours a week from weekly users (Gallup & Walton Family Foundation, 2025, p. 6). Back in the EEF trial, some interviewed teachers found it ‘difficult to characterise where any saved time was used’ (Roy et al., 2024, p. 73). Together, these figures show that saving time and feeling that you saved time are not the same thing, and neither tells us where the time went. Of the two, I give more weight to the measured saving in the EEF trial. If AI does save you time, it is worth noting what you used that time for.

So I keep the claim, in a narrower form. The newer evidence does not show that machines can do what Chapter 3 described. Nor does it prove that they never will, because the decisive tests have not been done. What lasts, on this evidence, is a professional commitment to relationships, care and responsible judgement. Care stays in, because nothing here weakens the experiments Chapter 3 drew on. Modelling stays only as a hope, because no study I have read has observed it in a school. The evidence for this commitment is thin but consistent. Strong new studies would make me change it.

Newer evidence also lets me make Chapter 3’s tests sharper. The second test could be a trial that compares AI warnings about student distress with what teachers notice, counting the false alarms. The third could be a time-use study that follows the minutes AI saves, to see whether they reach students and their learning. I would add one new test: whether teachers’ own noticing fades after years of relying on AI alerts. Teachers’ representatives already take a firm position. ETUCE, the European teachers’ union body, calls the human relations between students and teachers ‘irreplaceable’ (European Trade Union Committee for Education, 2024, p. 8).

Agency grows in conditions

If this era needs teachers who shape AI, what makes shaping possible? Research on teacher agency gives a useful answer. Priestley and colleagues describe agency as ‘a relational effect’ of a teacher’s capacities meeting a setting’s resources and limits (Priestley et al., 2012, p. 11). Agency, in this view, is something a teacher achieves in a particular place and time.

Their case of Gerald shows the point without any AI at all. Gerald, a Scottish technology teacher, wanted more dialogue and practical work. Yet three of his four observed lessons were largely teacher-led, with students listening and taking notes (pp. 24–25). Short periods, missing equipment, no technician and the attainment agenda all pressed on him (p. 25). ‘At the end of the day, I am judged on my results,’ Gerald said (p. 26). The authors ‘tentatively conclude’ that a clear educational philosophy is ‘a necessary but not sufficient condition’ for agency (p. 34). Gerald’s case comes from before chatbots, but I think it applies directly to AI. A teacher can believe in careful AI use and still be unable to practise it with short lessons, missing equipment and pressure on results. When a colleague seems slow to use AI well, I would first ask what they lack, before asking what they believe.

Eteläpelto and colleagues give a practical definition. Professional agency means that people, alone or in groups, influence their work, make choices about it and take a position on it (Eteläpelto et al., 2013, p. 62). Taking a position counts, and so does acting together.

A mixed-methods study of university teaching describes teacher agency as including ‘a collective response to shifts in the epistemic agency of students’ (Kahn et al., 2025, p. 1). In plainer words, teachers respond together when students start to build knowledge in new ways, for example with AI. Only 45 of 318 staff completed its survey, so the evidence is small (p. 4).

Shared language matters too. In a later Scottish study, some teachers’ talk about education seemed ‘rather limited and closely connected to policy discourses’ (Biesta et al., 2017, p. 52). One teacher, Sara, said, ‘But obviously I am powerless’ (p. 50). AI policy talk risks repeating this pattern. It offers a vocabulary of efficiency, integrity and compliance, with little about what education is for.

Time and support decide a great deal. Among 368 Australian teachers, school support for generative AI predicted their later confidence with it (Collie et al., 2024, p. 7). On a seven-point scale, the same teachers rated time pressure at 5.84 and AI support at 2.62 (p. 4). The design is observational, so this is emerging evidence. The practical lesson is that a school that wants confident AI use should arrange support first, and should not expect teachers to find the time for it on their own.

The two terms from Chapter 4, teacher-design burden and infrastructural prerequisites, help here, although none of the studies in this chapter uses them. Selwyn and colleagues ask what ‘“background” monitoring and maintenance work’ automation now demands of school staff (Selwyn et al., 2023, p. 17). Shaping AI adds to the design burden, because checking outputs, joining design work and adapting content all take hours. Agency has its own prerequisites too: time, a meeting where teachers can speak, a say in what is bought, a connection, and a union or association that speaks for them.

Does involving teachers change results, or does it only make decisions seem fairer? The answer so far is thin and indirect. The best trial tested data tools for teachers, not the process of designing tools together. Teachers who helped design Lumilo wanted to take control of the software whenever they needed. The researchers summed up that wish as ‘Allow me to override the technology’ (Holstein et al., 2019, p. 33).

In a 2018 experiment, glasses that showed teachers live information about each student led to small learning gains compared with normal teaching, r = 0.21 (Holstein et al., 2018, p. 161). The live information itself added r = 0.11 beyond the glasses alone (p. 161). Teachers also gave more time to students who started with less knowledge (p. 163).

Participation is often shallow. A 2021 review of 72 participatory design studies with teachers (Tuhkala, 2021, p. 6) predates ChatGPT. Drawing on earlier design research, it notes that participants ‘can still be held back from influencing any actual decisions’ (p. 4). In a longer partnership, many of the 76 middle-school maths teachers wanted the final say (Lawrence et al., 2024, pp. 823, 840). They also asked for a reason behind each suggestion (p. 838). One put it simply. ‘Because even if I want to accept it, then I at least know precisely what I’m accepting’ (p. 838). The Lumilo teachers asked for the same two things. I think they are a fair test for any tool a school offers. If a tool cannot explain its suggestions, or does not let a teacher overrule it, I would ask why before using it with students.

Saying no, and saying it well

Agency includes saying no. Priestley and colleagues write that ‘agency can be seen validly as agency in opposition to policy’ (2012, p. 36). Eteläpelto and colleagues also count taking a critical stance, or resisting a proposed change, as agency (2013, pp. 46 and 61). Beijaard and colleagues offer a gentler reading, drawing on earlier work about teachers’ life stories. Resistance ‘may also reflect an effort to maintain a story to live by’ (Beijaard et al., 2004, p. 122).

I name refusal carefully. The kind that counts as professional is selective, reasoned and spoken through a channel. A teacher who declines a tool for their students’ sake, explains why and raises it with colleagues is exercising judgement. Blanket refusal that never looks at evidence is something else.

Teachers’ representatives now ask for such routes, in statements of position that report no changed rollout. A joint statement from UK education unions asks for ‘the time, support, and authority to make informed decisions and select tools or reject proposals’ (NAHT, 2026). The American Federation of Teachers opposes adoption of unnecessary, unsafe or untested AI without consultation (American Federation of Teachers, 2026). It will also fight for workers’ right to refuse such adoption, and defend them from retaliation. That union also runs the AI academy described in Chapter 10, with Microsoft as lead partner and OpenAI and Anthropic as founding partners (Boyer, 2026). The union’s partnership with AI companies does not cancel its position, but I keep it in mind when I read that position. A teacher can still use the wording of these statements when raising concerns in their own school.

Having a voice is not the same as having power. The St Paul teachers’ agreement is the only negotiated contract I hold, and its published copy has blank signature lines. Its AI section says that members must not lose their jobs ‘as an immediate and foreseeable result’ of generative AI (Saint Paul Public Schools & Saint Paul Federation of Educators, 2025, p. 149).

The union’s own account claims that teachers won a direct role in choosing and buying tools (Boyer, 2026). Yet the agreement contains no such clause. Boyer also notes that the job clause could let the district replace retiring teachers with AI (Boyer, 2026). On wider AI questions, the two sides only ‘may discuss’ them at an existing committee (Saint Paul Public Schools & Saint Paul Federation of Educators, 2025, p. 149).

Refusal can also be silent. Korea’s AI textbooks, which Chapter 11 describes and which mostly went unopened, show quiet non-use on a national scale (Lim, 2025). Silent refusal gave teachers no protection and no say in what happened to the books. My own doctoral research adds a counterweight. It argues that early engagement ‘is preferable to avoidance’ (Ilyas, 2026, p. 237). It also sees a widening split between teachers who build a critical way of teaching with AI and those who feel their authority is in crisis, with each side moving further apart.

Identity under pressure

Identity is where teachers feel agency most. Beijaard and colleagues call agency ‘an important element of professional identity’ (2004, p. 122). Studies of teacher identity and AI are small and recent, and most rely on what teachers say about themselves.

Some teachers hide their use. A history teacher in a fee-paying Delhi school said, ‘I generally avoid using AI in front of my students’ (Tripathi et al., 2025, p. 9). The aim was to keep students believing that their teacher knows the subject. In an English college for overseas students, a manager told a teacher called Shu to type AI text without showing learners (Barnes & Tour, 2026, p. 33). Concealment protects authority for a while. It also removes the chance to model careful use in the open.

Two small studies show identity moving in different directions. Wei and colleagues followed two Chinese university English teachers for about a year (Wei et al., 2026, p. 1). One became a technology-enhanced pedagogy innovator. The other became a cautious, selective integrator and a trusted adviser to peers. ‘AI is not here to replace my judgment, but to give me options,’ Zhang wrote in a reflective journal (p. 9). Both paths were sensible.

Kim and colleagues studied only two university teacher educators in Indonesia, and they warn against drawing general lessons from two cases (Kim et al., 2026, p. 25). Trying to keep up with generative AI made both educators’ emotional work harder (p. 1). The authors show how the two turned that strain into rewards, partly by valuing what their students knew (pp. 1, 20 to 23). Their abstract links the strain to ‘the financial and epistemological constraints of the Global South’ (p. 1).

Taken together, these small studies suggest that identity, more than skill, is often the real barrier. A teacher who feels pressure to keep up may burn out. I would rather see schools make open, careful use normal, so that no teacher has to choose between looking competent and being honest.

Not every fear in my interviews was about AI itself. I asked a design lecturer what skills future university teachers need. He began with a confession. It was about training run by Pakistan’s Higher Education Commission, the HEC.

‘Based on my experience with HEC training, I can say that I became very stressed and disheartened,’ he said. He saw the HEC moving to online learning. Whole courses would be made in advance and put online for anyone. ‘This, in turn, might lead to the phasing out of my role’ (Ilyas, 2026, Participant H05).

Then he turned the fear into a plan. First, he said, ‘teachers need to become digital’. Courses, lectures and teaching methods all had to work ‘with technology; otherwise, no alternative remains’ (Ilyas, 2026, Participant H05).

Fear and resolve in one breath. That is what identity under pressure sounds like.

Fear is real and measurable. Li tracked 348 Chinese school teachers through the first months of an AI curriculum reform (Li, 2026). Their replacement anxiety rose from 3.653 to 3.950 between October and December 2025 (p. 10). Emotional support weakened how far early stress carried into later stress (p. 13). The study concerns teaching a new AI curriculum, which differs from using AI to save work. Replacement fears are older than chatbots, too. Teachers voiced them while helping design Lumilo years earlier (Holstein et al., 2019, p. 32). For school leaders, a meeting where teachers can talk openly about these fears may matter as much as training in the tool.

Labour market data tell a calmer story. The International Labour Organization concludes that ‘transformation of jobs is the most likely impact of GenAI’ (Gmyrek et al., 2025, p. 1). Its tables give secondary teachers a score of 0.30, in the band it calls ‘Not Exposed’ (p. 55). These scores are judgements about tasks, made with the help of a model. They do not count jobs actually lost. The only study I have found on AI and teachers leaving their jobs measured only whether they intended to leave (Yonghe & Pan, 2026).

Across the world, the bigger problem is the teacher shortage that Chapter 3 described. UNESCO estimates that 44 million more primary and secondary teachers will be needed by 2030 (UNESCO & International Task Force on Teachers for Education 2030, 2024, p. 18).

Who remains answerable

Chapter 12 ended with a question. Who remains answerable for a claim, once a machine has helped to make it? In a school, that claim might be a lesson plan, a report comment or feedback on an essay.

Chapters 5 and 12 heard the botanist in my study describe the authority crisis that instant ChatGPT answers create for teachers (Ilyas, 2026, p. 167, Participant L5). Here I read that crisis as one of answerability as well as knowledge. Whatever the tool drafts, the teacher still signs off what students receive.

Imagine a teacher of Year 6 (ages ten to eleven) whose tool drafts thirty report comments in a minute. Chapter 2 said that AI can draft such a comment and a teacher decides. Each comment still goes home under the teacher’s name. A parent may ask why it says their child struggles with fractions. The teacher has to answer from what they know of that child, whatever the tool produced.

Selwyn and colleagues ask the hard question plainly. ‘How accountable should a classroom teacher be for the outcomes of an automated system whose functioning they have little understanding of?’ (Selwyn et al., 2023, p. 18). UNESCO’s framework keeps teachers responsible for teaching decisions, provided they receive preparation and support (Miao & Cukurova, 2024, p. 17). If tools are checked before schools use them, a step it calls ‘prior validation’, teachers would be spared ‘ethical governance beyond their role and/or capacities’ (p. 18). Molenaar argues that schools need ‘enough human control’, and favours automation that hands control back to the teacher when needed (Molenaar, 2022, p. 639).

The Sungu trial adds an uncomfortable twist. Its authors suggest that the teachers ‘most enthusiastic about using AI’ are ‘precisely those least positioned to notice’ its harms (Sungu et al., 2026, p. 15). Enthusiasm needs a habit of checking. Responsibility also runs beyond the classroom. Lawrence and colleagues (2024, p. 841) tell designers they ‘must reflect on how this responsibility can be ingrained in designs’.

Responsibility without control is not professional agency. A teacher can answer for what they can see, check and change. No teacher can answer alone for a vendor’s model, a district’s contract or a system nobody explained. Australia’s framework, which calls itself aspirational, ties teachers’ control of decisions to their accountability in one guiding statement (National AI in Schools Taskforce, 2023, pp. 3, 7). A call to agency must not ask teachers to repair structural failures alone.

Principles for the next decade

Every set of principles I have read is a position, a recommendation or a piece of guidance. I have found none that has been tested in practice. That does not make them empty. It means each principle should say openly what it rests on.

Six principles seem to me worth holding.

  • Teachers are answerable only for what they can see and control, a line drawn from Australian and UNESCO texts.

  • Teachers may question, pause and decline a tool through a known route. Scotland’s guidance, met in Chapter 11, leaves the choice of whether, when and how to use AI to teachers (Scottish Government, 2026, p. 10), although that choice must still follow the local authority’s permissions.

  • Taking part should mean having a real say in decisions, which consultation alone does not give. The call by ten unions for authority to ‘select tools or reject proposals’ supports this (NAHT, 2026).

  • Time and support should come before expectations. The Australian survey offers correlational evidence for this.

  • Schools should judge a tool by what students gain or lose, and stop using it if they lose.

  • Infrastructure and its costs belong to institutions and public budgets, not to teachers. The UN panel’s deliberations and my thesis, both discussed below, support this last principle.

Most of these principles rest on values and on agreement among professionals. Only the fifth has a trial behind it, the Sungu preprint, and even that offers only one caution. Values are a fair basis for a closing chapter, as long as we say so.

Principles should also come before products. Tools named in this book will date quickly. The profession’s claim to shape its tools is much older. Back in 1966, an international recommendation on teachers’ status said teachers should have ‘the essential role’ in choosing teaching material (International Labour Organization & UNESCO, 1966, para. 61). Teaching aids, it adds, ‘should not be regarded as a substitute for the teacher’ (para. 88(1)). Its authors could not foresee chatbots. They did see that tools serve teachers.

The call that follows has two halves. One is individual practice, the habit of checking a tool’s work and recording what you changed. The other is collective voice, through a staff group, a subject association or a union. Their powers differ sharply between countries, and even between districts. Neither half works for long without the other. None of this promises proven gains for students. Together they claim something more modest, that teachers have a legitimate right to shape the tools of their own work.

Katie Sanchez’s choice fits inside that claim. She tried a tool, kept the part of her work that depended on knowing her students and let the machine save her effort elsewhere. Her cohort gave her a learning group and a pilot to design (Waite et al., 2025, p. 1). A small grant to her school covered release time (p. 1). Many teachers have none of these. What endures is the judgement she showed, and the conditions that let her show it.

I sometimes picture the elementary classroom from Chapter 1, with its notebooks full of the same sentence. Suppose I walked back into it tomorrow, and a boy held up a page that a chatbot had written for him in seconds.

‘Read me this sentence,’ I would say. ‘Now tell me what it means.’

If he can, the page has done him some good. If he cannot, we have found the place to start.

I would still look up from the notebook to the face. I would let a machine help me prepare, translate a letter for a parent and find ten more examples when the first five failed. And I would expect my school to give me the time, the connection and the voice to decide which was which. The shortcut has changed. The job of seeing the thinking behind the work has not.

In practice

These moves turn the argument into things a teacher could start next week. I offer them as hypotheticals to adapt, and no study has tested their effect on students. Each rests on a source or carries a tag marking my own synthesis.

Bring a problem before a product. Take one problem to a staff group before anyone names a tool. The California cohort asked teachers to identify problems of practice and then design their own pilots (Waite et al., 2025, p. 1). EdTech Hub’s sandbox report likewise advises starting ‘with teacher pain points, not technology’ (Burgess, 2026, p. 35).

Ask who decides, checks, pays and stops. Who decides whether, when and how? Which person checks the outputs? As use grows, who pays, given that AI costs rise with use (Burgess, 2026, p. 36)? And who can stop it? The NEA’s sample bargaining language lets either party initiate a suspension of an AI tool for just cause (National Education Association, 2026). Chapter 11 gives the questions to put to suppliers.

Pilot with a stop rule. The NEA’s policy statement allows pilots where research is lacking, but only while schools gather evidence (National Education Association, n.d.). Schools must also agree to stop if the benefits fail to appear. As a hypothetical, a two-week record of what one class gained or lost would give that rule something to work with.

Keep a short decision log. Note what the tool drafted, what you changed and why. The European Commission’s guidelines ask teachers whether they know what to review in AI outputs before using them (European Commission, 2026, p. 23). A few lines in a notebook turn that question into a habit. They also show who answered for the result.

Speak through a representative. The National Education Union (NEU) asks its representatives to find out members’ views on introducing AI, as part of its regular negotiations with employers (National Education Union, n.d.-a). Where union rights are weak, a subject association can carry the same message.

Name the costs. The teachers’ union NASUWT asks that AI tools add nothing to teachers’ workload, and that impact assessments check this (NASUWT, 2025b, p. 3). Count the hours and the data costs that Chapter 4 showed teachers paying themselves, and raise them through a representative.

In a university faculty. Give teaching staff a formal route to question or decline a tool, such as a standing item on the faculty teaching committee’s agenda, and record the decisions. The design lecturer earlier in this chapter feared that online courses might phase out his role (Ilyas, 2026, Participant H05). A known route for such worries turns fear into a voice.

When resources are thin

In thin-resource systems, agency often runs through a phone. Chapter 7 met an Islamabad teacher who was still trying to open a training app at 2 a.m. (Aslam et al., 2025, p. 59). In another Pakistani study, an urban primary teacher prepared everything with ChatGPT at home and carried printouts to class (Ali et al., 2026, p. 545). Both teachers absorbed the cost of making the tool work.

Designs led by teachers can work in such settings. An EdTech Hub report describes a Pakistani sandbox, a space for testing new tools. EdTech Hub itself is supported by UKAid, the Gates Foundation, the World Bank and UNICEF (Burgess, 2026, p. 2). The team at Taleemabad, a Pakistani EdTech company, spent a month in schools before building Lesson Genie on WhatsApp (pp. 6, 16). Taleemabad’s own data tracked how it was used. The tool spread from 17 to 200 teachers as they shared it with each other, without pressure from above (p. 18).

I read that spread as a sign of uptake and agency, since its effect on learning is still ‘yet to be determined’ (Burgess, 2026, p. 36). In Tanzania, teachers turned a project meant for peer learning into a tool for lesson preparation (p. 34). The report concludes that ‘When teachers are given genuine agency, they direct tools toward the problems they actually face’ (p. 34). For a teacher, the practical idea is to start from a problem colleagues already have, and to share what works with each other.

Cultural fit adds another kind of design work. A kindergarten teacher in Taiwan said, ‘usually we consult the elders in the village to confirm culture and norms’ (Xiao et al., 2026, p. 23). That is teacher-design burden in its plainest form. The model’s defaults did not fit, so the teacher did the adapting.

Teachers’ voice is weaker still. As Chapter 3 noted, the copy of Pakistan’s National AI Policy that I hold never uses the word ‘teacher’ (Ministry of Information Technology and Telecommunication, 2025). I found no statement on generative AI from any Pakistani teacher association, though one may exist offline. The UN panel’s summary of its discussions calls connectivity ‘a prerequisite’ that needs public funding (United Nations Secretary-General’s High-Level Panel on the Teaching Profession, 2024, p. 33).

Where data, power and time are scarce, teachers need basic conditions before they can have a voice. A WhatsApp group, a paper record of decisions and one protected meeting can still give teachers a place to speak. They work only when someone above the classroom listens and pays.

My IFDC framework ends where this book ends (Ilyas et al., 2026b). It has four levels: basic conditions, feeling safe to try, chances to learn and skilled choices. Teachers can shape their own work only when all four are in place. AI arrived in every education system at the same moment; what each system makes of it will depend on the conditions it builds for its teachers. The next ten years will show which systems give their teachers what they need to master it.

Questions for reflection

Take these questions to your next department or phase meeting, or into a quiet moment after school.

  1. Which part of your work, like Katie Sanchez’s writing conferences, would you keep for yourself, and how would you explain why?

  2. The last time you used an AI output with students, could you say what you changed and why?

  3. Who in your school can pause or stop a tool, and how would a teacher raise a concern?

  4. What time, data or design work are you absorbing that your school or system should carry?
