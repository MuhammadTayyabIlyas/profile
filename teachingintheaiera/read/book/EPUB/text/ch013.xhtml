Chapter 11. The Whole School

Ko Ho-dam, a high school junior on Jeju Island, met a new kind of textbook in March 2025. South Korea had approved about a dozen publishers to build AI-powered digital textbooks for maths, English and computer science. The government promised personalised learning, fewer dropouts and a lighter load for teachers. Ko’s first weeks went differently. ‘All our classes were delayed because of technical problems with the textbooks,’ he told a reporter (Kwon, 2025).

Korea’s Board of Audit and Inspection later examined the programme, and a business newspaper reported its findings in December 2025. In schools that had chosen the textbooks, the audit found that 8.1% of students had used the textbooks on ten or more days between March and May. On average, 60% of students never opened the system at all. Among 513 English and maths teachers at pilot schools, 85.5% had never used the textbooks or had stopped. The commonest reason, given by 53%, was that the product was little more than a digitised paper textbook (Lim, 2025).

A second measure tells a related story, and I keep it apart from the first. Data compiled by a Korean lawmaker showed school adoption falling from 37% in the first semester to 19% in the next, after the textbooks lost their official status (Kwon, 2025). The government had spent more than 1.2 trillion won, about US$850 million, including equipment and teacher training (Kwon, 2025). According to the audit, the decision passed through seven internal meetings without collecting the views of students, parents or teachers (Lim, 2025). Both reports are journalism about an audit I have not read in full, so the evidence is emerging. The pattern is still hard to miss.

Here was a national system with money, devices, trained staff and political will. What it lacked was a reason teachers recognised, time to test the tool and a voice for the people who would use it. That gap is this chapter’s subject. Its central question is how a school moves from scattered experiments to shared practice.

Shared practice grows where a school already has readiness, time and a reason, and it stalls when a mandate or a purchase arrives first. Leaders shape it mostly through teachers, and through the policy, training, buying and parent contact that they own. Where resources are thin, the order of these steps matters more than the tool.

Chapter 10 kept to the individual teacher and handed me the systems around each one. The ethical principles a school must honour belong to Chapter 8, and I cite them here without repeating them. What students should learn about AI belongs to Chapter 9. This chapter is about the school as an organisation, the place where one teacher’s good idea either spreads or quietly dies.

Where schools are now, from Wales to Estonia

Most schools are nearer the start of this road than the end. In Wales, the inspectorate Estyn surveyed 324 school staff and visited 21 schools in 2025. Its verdict was plain. ‘Overall, many schools are still in the early stages of exploring AI’ (Estyn, 2025, p. 1). Use usually began with individual staff who had an interest in digital innovation. At the time of the review, ‘very few schools had developed standalone AI policies’ (p. 24).

Estyn, the schools inspectorate for Wales, chose most of its visits because schools had said they were using AI well. It also warns that most people who answered its survey were confident, positive users (Estyn, 2025, pp. 32, 50). So across Wales as a whole, schools are probably at an even earlier stage. The inspectors’ judgements from their visits carry more weight than the survey percentages, and I treat the finding as early evidence.

A small number of Welsh schools had gone further. Barry Island Primary began with a small enquiry into using AI for differentiation and workload. Leaders introduced tools gradually, starting with planning, resources and reports. They paired staff for peer coaching, ran regular professional learning and drafted an AI policy. The enquiry grew into a whole-school initiative tied to the school’s aim to ‘work smarter, not harder’ (Estyn, 2025, p. 23). Teachers report time savings, and the inspectors note that the school kept teacher judgement at the centre. Barry Island is one school so I read it as an example of a route, not proof that the route works everywhere. What I would take from it is the sequence: a small question first, tools for staff tasks next, then peer coaching and a written policy.

England’s inspectorate found a similar pattern among enthusiasts. Ofsted interviewed leaders in 21 schools and colleges already invested in AI. Many were updating their AI policies ‘as often as monthly’, and they encouraged open talk about AI among staff (Ofsted, 2025a). These schools were chosen because they were adopting. They tell us what keen schools do, which may differ from what typical ones do.

Estonia offers the most complete whole-system example I know. Its national programme, AI Leap, signed agreements with 154 of 156 upper secondary schools in its first year. About 5,000 teachers received paid AI accounts, and learning circles sat at the heart of the teacher programme (TI-Hüpe, 2026, pp. 15, 21, 40). By spring 2026, 95.9% of surveyed teachers had used AI in their work. They estimated an average saving of 1.8 hours a week (p. 24). The share of schools running AI-focused learning circles rose from 70.5% to 83.4% (p. 26). The programme worked with OpenAI and Google, and these figures come from its own surveys, so I read them as emerging.

Students were harder to reach than teachers. By May 2026, 45.9% of students who had activated the programme’s student app used it in a given month, against a target of 60% (TI-Hüpe, 2026, p. 21). Teachers came closer to their own targets. The team writes that reaching students and achieving active use ‘proved more challenging than expected’ (p. 39). Teacher-facing use spread first, even in a system built for both. A leader can plan for that gap and set separate goals for each group, instead of treating slow student uptake as failure.

The programme’s own lesson matters more than its numbers. Schools differed in AI experience, culture and available time. Support ‘proved most effective in schools that already had both the organizational readiness and the time to experiment’ (TI-Hüpe, 2026, p. 39). The team also admits that its communication with schools should have started earlier and been more systematic (p. 39). Korea had money and devices but did not ask teachers, while Estonia found that prepared schools gained most. For a leader, I think the first question is whether staff have readiness and time, not which tool to buy.

Earlier technology waves teach the same lesson from the other side. A review of experimental studies found that programmes giving students computers raised computer use and skills. Effects on achievement were more mixed, with more promise when devices came with learning software (Escueta et al., 2017, p. 10). Access alone rarely changed learning. In that light, Korea’s textbooks look like an access programme with AI inside.

What readiness means

The two terms from Chapter 4, teacher-design burden and infrastructural prerequisites, take a particular shape when the unit is a whole school.

At school level, infrastructural prerequisites are organisational as much as technical. A pilot needs time in the timetable and a staff channel that works. It needs someone who owns the policy, and a budget or a clear rule about free tools. Estyn found that the schools making most progress saw AI as part of curriculum innovation, professional learning and school improvement (Estyn, 2025, p. 24).

Hardware belongs on that list, though in richer systems it matters less than many buyers assume. A survey of 2,247 upper secondary teachers in 112 Swiss schools tested what carries a head’s influence into lessons. The quality of school infrastructure did not carry it once teachers’ attitudes and skills were counted (Schmitz et al., 2023, pp. 9, 11). The authors add that in less technologically developed countries, earlier work found infrastructure mattered more (p. 11). I return to that point when resources are thin.

Teacher-design burden changes shape too. When a school adopts a shared practice, every teacher has to invest before the school sees a return. Researchers, two of them affiliated with the edtech company ALEF Education, surveyed 792 teachers using one national adaptive platform. They set a blunt condition. The platform must not ‘lead to an increase in teachers’ existing workload (even if it does not lead to any decrease in it)’ (Cukurova et al., 2023, p. 9). A school that asks for shared practice without paying for that investment has moved the cost onto each teacher’s evening.

Leadership that works through teachers

Leadership matters for learning, and the evidence has said so for a long time. A synthesis for the Wallace Foundation concluded that leadership is ‘second only to classroom instruction among all school-related factors’ that shape what students learn (Leithwood et al., 2004, p. 5). Leaders mostly act indirectly, through other people and the organisation (p. 13). That older finding is strong, and newer studies of technology add detail to it.

The Swiss study is the clearest case. Teachers rated their head’s transformational leadership, the kind that sets a vision and supports individuals. That leadership had no significant direct link to how teachers used technology for learning (Schmitz et al., 2023, p. 11). Its influence ran through teachers’ attitudes, their technical skill and their teaching skill with technology. The model explained 28% of the variance in technology use (p. 9). The sample is large, though the study predates generative AI.

Studies about AI itself are newer and fewer. In Lower Saxony, Germany, 179 school leaders answered a survey at a conference in August 2023. Two styles of leadership predicted how much AI their schools said they used (Pietsch & Mah, 2025, pp. 1052, 1056). One was transformational leadership, which inspires change. The other was digital instructional leadership, which guides teaching with technology.

Leaders who combined both styles predicted AI use even better. They first gave staff room to experiment, and then made the useful practices standard (Pietsch & Mah, 2025, p. 1057). The authors call their study exploratory, and every measure comes from the leaders’ own reports (p. 1059). For a head, I read the two steps as a practical order: first let staff try things, then make the practices that worked the school’s normal way of doing things.

In Hong Kong, a survey of 204 principals, middle leaders and teachers across 60 schools asked about digital leadership for AI. Respondents rated their school’s digital leadership low, on the disagree side of a six-point scale. They rated teachers’ barriers high (Cheng & Wang, 2023, pp. 5–6). Even so, the authors found that digital leadership was linked to AI uptake. They say leaders have a key part in reducing teachers’ barriers, but leadership alone cannot solve the problem (p. 9). All respondents came from one community of practice, so this is emerging evidence too. Taken together with the Swiss and German studies, my reading is that leaders matter mainly because they can lower the barriers teachers face. A leader who wants to help could begin by asking staff which barriers are largest, before announcing a vision.

A head cannot carry this alone, and the research on distributed leadership explains why. One review of two decades of that work concludes that ‘it was how leadership was distributed that mattered most of all’ (Harris et al., 2022, p. 440). The authors also note a ‘dark side’ when sharing leadership becomes a way to coerce people under a democratic label (p. 439). They say plainly that their overview is not a systematic review (p. 445). For AI, the warning is practical. Naming a digital champion without time, authority or a budget hands out the work and keeps the power.

What should leaders aim at? Michael Fullan and colleagues, writing an editorial, argue that the task is less about fitting AI into current schools. It is more about how ‘we refine our education systems around AI’ (Fullan et al., 2024, p. 343). They also note that no large-scale evidence yet shows AI tools working in education (p. 340). Their advice is a long-term vision that puts children’s learning needs first (p. 343). That is a position, and it sits comfortably with the studies above.

A policy that lives

Written policy is spreading faster than shared practice. In a nationally representative RAND survey in the United States, 45% of principals reported school or district AI policies or guidance (Doss et al., 2025, p. 2). Whether teachers found them useful depended on training. Among teachers with AI training, 37% said their policies were helpful, against 13% of those without it (p. 10). The association is strong, though a survey cannot show that training caused the difference. I would plan the policy and the training together, and launch them at the same time.

National guidance mostly leaves the choice to schools. England tells schools they ‘are free to make their own choices about the most suitable use cases’ within their statutory duties (Department for Education, 2025b). It adds that teacher-facing use brings ‘more immediate benefits and fewer risks’. Scotland’s non-statutory guardrails lean further towards teachers. ‘Teachers are able to decide whether, when, and how to use AI in their classrooms’ (Scottish Government, 2026, p. 10). AI outputs ‘must not be used to monitor or analyse teachers’ performance’ (p. 14). Schools must also update their digital policies in consultation with unions, students and parents (p. 10). In either country, a leader cannot rely on national rules to settle the decisions that matter most in a classroom.

Singapore sets clearer rules from the centre, staged by age. From Primary 1 to 3, students learn about AI, but schools ‘will not assign any work that requires them to use AI directly’. Older students, from Primary 4, use only AI tools designed for education, under teacher supervision. National exams stay proctored, with AI prohibited (Ministry of Education Singapore, 2026a). A school there inherits its boundaries, while a school in England must draw its own.

So the school writes its own rules. The best trust policies I have read treat the policy as a working tool. HEARTS Academy Trust in England calls its policy ‘a living document’ and reviews it every year (HEARTS Academy Trust, 2025, p. 4). Digital champions in each school lead training and keep a register of approved tools, reviewed every term (pp. 7, 12). At first Microsoft Copilot and Canva AI were the only recognised approved generative AI tools, and unapproved tools count as ‘Shadow IT’ (pp. 8–9). Staff who want a new tool propose it through their champion. The champions then assess it against the trust’s safety and impact criteria and data protection standards (p. 13). This is one trust’s own policy document, so it shows what the trust intends, not how well the register works in daily use. The part I would copy first is the route for staff to propose a new tool. It gives teachers an approved way to ask before they start using something unapproved.

Vine Schools Trust, a Church of England trust, starts one step earlier. Its leaders define the problem first and only then assess a tool against it. Every tool, free ones included, must pass the same short evaluation of purpose, safety, data protection and compliance (GDPRiS, 2026, p. 1). Staff are encouraged to share failed experiments (p. 2). A data-protection company that sells to schools published the case study, so it describes practice and does not prove impact.

My doctoral research found that a ban can hide a practice rather than stop it. In Chapter 8, the lecturer in my study with the WhatsApp class group kept using it with students after a ban. That lecturer sent links through a class representative (Ilyas, 2026, p. 159, Participant L4). My thesis concludes that such shadow systems ‘must be formalised’, because their persistence shows where official provision is not enough (p. 238). That was a university, and the lesson travels to schools. An approved list works only if the approved tools do the job teachers need done.

Policy also has to carry the ethics that Chapter 8 set out. A systematic review of AI ethics in schools found only 25 relevant studies published between 2010 and 2023 (Gouseti et al., 2025, p. 161). Most empirical studies came from the United States and China (p. 167). The authors describe ‘the striking disjuncture’ between expectations of AI and the realities of everyday schooling (p. 175). Among their responses, the review reports a study supporting teacher co-design of AI ethics curricula (p. 171). I would read that as a reason to involve teachers when a school plans what students learn about AI ethics, while remembering that it rests on a single study.

Who holds the school to account

A policy needs someone outside the staffroom who asks whether it works. In England, that is rarely the inspectorate. Ofsted says it does ‘not look at AI as a stand-alone part of our inspections’, and it does not expect schools to use AI at all (Ofsted, 2025b). It admits it lacks the evidence ‘to define good use of AI’. Inspectors may instead ask leaders how their AI decisions serve children’s best interests. So I would write the reasons down when each decision is made.

That leaves governors and trustees. The Welsh inspectors recommend that schools involve ‘staff, governors, pupils and parents in shaping expectations and practice around the use of AI’ (Estyn, 2025, p. 9). In practice, only a minority of the Welsh schools it engaged with had updated their safeguarding and data protection policies for AI (p. 29). Vine gives its trustees a defined role, challenging purpose, safety and responsible use through the education committee (GDPRiS, 2026, p. 1). Little evidence exists on how governing boards actually approve AI tools, and I found none from Pakistan.

Data law sets some duties whatever the board decides. In Scotland, local authorities are the data controllers for AI tools in their schools (Scottish Government, 2026, p. 7). Australia’s national framework asks that tools be ‘tested before they are used’ and that teachers and leaders ‘retain control of decision making’ (National AI in Schools Taskforce, 2023, p. 7).

Unions form a third check. The National Education Union asks for ‘consultation with staff and their unions before AI is introduced’, with ongoing monitoring of benefits (National Education Union, n.d.-c). Scotland makes the same consultation part of its guardrails (Scottish Government, 2026, p. 10). Accountability, then, is spread thin across inspectors, boards, local authorities and unions. A school that wants shared practice has to build its own check, usually a short annual review that someone outside the leadership team reads.

Professional learning as a school system

Chapter 10 looked at how one teacher learns. Here the question is how a school organises learning so that it reaches classrooms. The evidence from before AI is disappointing, and it is strong. Across 104 experiments, teacher professional development raised student scores by about .05 standard deviations on average (Sims et al., 2021, p. 5). Coaching did better. In programmes with 100 or more teachers, though, its effect on achievement shrank to 0.10 standard deviations (Kraft et al., 2018, p. 572). Scale is where school systems usually struggle.

Colleagues seem to be one route from training to classroom practice. One study used PISA 2022 data from 16,072 STEM teachers, who teach science, technology, engineering and maths. It found that digital professional development predicted how much teachers used digital tools in class. About 11% of that link ran through professional learning communities, groups of teachers who learn together (Liu et al., 2024, p. 9). Teachers reported their own tool use, so this shows how often tools were used, not what students learned. On the adaptive platform described earlier, teachers who found the training helpful were the most active users, r = 0.246 (Cukurova et al., 2023, p. 8). Both findings are early evidence.

Estonia built its programme around this kind of structure. It ran seminars for school leadership teams and learning-circle facilitators, monthly webinars and ready-made plans for each circle (TI-Hüpe, 2026, pp. 11–12). Teachers in learning circles reported high motivation and relatively low stress (p. 26). In Wales, Estyn found staff confidence ‘closely linked to the quality and extent of professional learning available’ (Estyn, 2025, p. 24). Informal sharing stirred curiosity, it adds, but alone it did not improve staff confidence (p. 25).

My thesis made a similar recommendation for universities. It proposed a recognised roster of more confident teachers as mentors, with workload credit for mentoring, and peer-observation partnerships across competence levels (Ilyas, 2026, p. 239). That was written for a university, and the logic of paid time for peer support applies in schools.

What does this mean for a school timetable? Training that ends in a hall rarely changes much by itself. Learning that continues in a team meeting, with colleagues trying the same thing and comparing notes, has a better chance. The National Education Union adds a condition every head should hear. ‘Any reductions in workload that are achieved should not be replaced with new work in other areas’ (National Education Union, n.d.-c).

Buying AI tools, paid or free

Procurement is where good intentions meet money. Los Angeles Unified launched an AI chatbot called Ed in March 2024. By June it had been shelved, after about three months and nearly $3 million spent, when the company behind it laid off more than half its staff (Johnson, 2024). The contract had allowed up to $6 million over five years, and the pilot reached about 55,000 students at selected schools (Klein, 2024). One figure is money spent and the other is a ceiling, and the two should not be confused. The risk here was the supplier as well as the tool. Before signing, a school can ask how the service would continue, or how the school would leave, if the company failed.

San Diego shows a quieter route. An AI feature that suggested grades for writing arrived inside a larger Houghton Mifflin contract, approved among more than 70 items without discussion. Board members reportedly did not know it was there (Johnson, 2024). Both accounts are journalism, so the evidence is emerging. Bundled and free AI can skip the scrutiny that a named purchase would get.

Schools in England now have firmer ground to stand on. The government’s product safety standards say that ‘suppliers should not exaggerate the impact or capabilities of their tools’ (Department for Education, 2026a). Data-protection guidance says the school’s data protection officer and safeguarding lead must be consulted before buying (Department for Education, n.d.). A pilot evidence board ran for the government through the Chartered College of Teaching. Its reviewers could weigh several kinds of evidence ‘without relying on a single hierarchy’ (Chedzey et al., 2026, p. 4). The pilot was small by design (p. 39).

Schools rarely meet these standards on their own. A UK study of Google Classroom interviewed 47 professionals and traced children’s data as they used it. One former headteacher admitted, ‘Data protection, if I’m honest, isn’t usually at the forefront of our mind’ (Livingstone et al., 2024, p. 4). A commercial data protection officer who advises schools tried to work out how Google’s privacy policies fit together. ‘I read the privacy notice, and I lost the will to live’ (p. 4). In 2024, a link that a teacher posted in Google Classroom exposed a 16-year-old to 170 third-party tracking services (p. 5). The authors suggest that national negotiation with vendors is more practical than asking every school to assess the risks alone (p. 3). This is a problem of capacity, not of care. Until the national negotiation the authors suggest takes place, a school can at least keep its list of allowed tools short, so there are fewer policies to check.

Teachers are often absent from these decisions. A survey of leaders in 27 large urban US districts, funded by the Google Foundation, asked who takes part in buying AI. In 65% of districts, teachers took minimal or no part in AI procurement (Burch et al., 2026, p. 7). Paper protections were common. More than 80% included four or more privacy protections in their AI contracts (p. 7). Roughly three-quarters named equity in their criteria, yet fewer than one in five regularly prioritised AI spending for high-need students (p. 7). District leaders reported on their own systems, so this is emerging evidence.

Free offers deserve the same scrutiny. In November 2025, OpenAI launched ChatGPT for Teachers, free to US school educators until June 2027 according to the company (Capoot, 2025). A free licence still carries costs in data, training time and dependence on one supplier. Paid licences have their own costs, and these can grow. Korea’s auditors expected textbook subscription fees to rise sharply (Lim, 2025). A World Bank working paper on costing AI in low- and middle-income countries warns that many AI costs recur and accumulate beyond the initial purchase (Yarrow et al., 2026, p. 7). It also explains why the costs of pilots are poor guides to costs at scale (p. 12). Vine’s rule, that free tools pass the same evaluation as paid ones, is a sensible answer.

What evidence should a school ask for? Earlier edtech gives a modest benchmark. A meta-analysis of 18 studies in China found a small positive effect of 0.13 standard deviations, mostly from computer-assisted learning used as a supplement (Abbey et al., 2024, p. 1). That is a pre-AI effect. A comment in a learning-science journal notes that only 16% of 1,058 educators surveyed by Education Week called edtech very effective at accelerating learning (Kucirkova et al., 2023, p. 1). Marketing tends to promise far more than either figure.

Talking with parents

Parents often hear about AI from their children before they hear from the school. In Hong Kong, a survey of 215 parents at two kindergartens found 74.4% positive about AI in kindergarten (Su, 2025, pp. 283, 286). Yet 97.7% said their child had never interacted with an AI-powered robot (p. 286). Support, in other words, ran ahead of experience. Asked what made AI learning hard for young children, parents most often named limited comprehension (43%), then time (22%) and difficult content (19%) (p. 287). Family income was linked to whether a child had met an AI robot at all (p. 287). Su concludes that education authorities should do more to guide parents (p. 292).

Parents of teenagers feel less sure. Among 159 parents in 19 countries, 43% felt they lacked the knowledge to guide their child’s use of AI tools (Eira et al., 2025, pp. 927, 929). The study was partly funded by The Walt Disney Company (p. 931). The review of AI ethics in schools adds a sharper point. Schools should inform parents well enough for ‘confirmatory consent about specific uses’, beyond the general consent that is the current norm (Gouseti et al., 2025, p. 174). In short, parents of young children tend to support AI without much direct experience of it, while many parents of teenagers feel unsure how to guide their child. Both groups need clear information from the school about specific uses. Both studies are small surveys, and one was partly funded by a company, so I would not build a plan on their exact figures.

The practical advice from schools is consistent, though it rests on anecdote. Michael Rubin, principal of Uxbridge High School in Massachusetts, held a parent night on AI. He began with familiar AI such as the Waze navigation app, then explained the school’s rules (Klein, 2025). His advice to other heads was to get the school’s own policy in order first. ‘Two people can become 50 very, very quickly,’ he said of worried parents (Klein, 2025). Vine stages its communication in the same way. It focuses on safety and awareness now, with wider discussion once staff can explain the trust’s purpose and safeguards (GDPRiS, 2026, p. 2).

Estonia collected parents’ questions in its first year and built a website section and a YouTube discussion series around them (TI-Hüpe, 2026, p. 20). England’s guidance suggests schools ‘may also wish to consider how they engage with parents’ around generative AI (Department for Education, 2025b). Templates exist as well. TeachAI, an initiative led by Code.org, ETS, ISTE and Khan Academy among others, offers a sample parent letter covering privacy, human agency and age limits (TeachAI, 2025). Internet Matters closes its family guide with reassurance. ‘You don’t need to be an AI expert’ (Internet Matters, 2026, p. 8).

None of these sources tests whether a letter or an evening reduces complaints or builds trust. That gap is real. Until someone measures it, I would treat parent communication as part of the policy itself, timed after staff understand it and written in the languages families read.

In practice

These routines turn the argument into steps a school can take this term. They draw on the evidence above, and nobody has tested them as a package.

Start with one shared problem. A primary leadership team picks a problem staff already feel, such as the time spent writing reading texts at several levels. Volunteers try AI on that problem for half a term. Barry Island Primary began this way, with a small enquiry into differentiation and workload (Estyn, 2025, p. 23).

Put the trial in the timetable. A secondary school gives its volunteers one fortnightly meeting slot and protects it from cover. The group reports back at a staff meeting, including what failed. Estonia’s support worked best where schools already had time to experiment (TI-Hüpe, 2026, p. 39).

Name an owner with time. The head names a digital lead and gives that person an hour a week and a budget line. Distribution helps when it is real, and how leadership is shared matters most (Harris et al., 2022, p. 440).

Keep a short approved list. A trust keeps a register of approved tools, reviewed each term, with a simple route for staff to propose new ones. Free tools go through the same check as paid ones (HEARTS Academy Trust, 2025, pp. 12–13; GDPRiS, 2026, p. 1).

Ask suppliers four questions. Before any purchase or free sign-up, the business manager asks four things. What evidence supports the claims, where does students’ data go, what will the tool cost in year three, and how would the school leave? These follow England’s standards and the World Bank’s warning about recurrent costs (Department for Education, 2026a; Yarrow et al., 2026, p. 7).

Review the policy each term. The policy carries a date and a named owner, and staff can raise problems with it at any meeting. Ofsted’s early adopters updated theirs as often as monthly (Ofsted, 2025a).

Brief staff before parents. The school explains its policy to staff first. Then it holds a parent evening that starts with AI families already use. Uxbridge and Vine both kept to this order (Klein, 2025; GDPRiS, 2026, p. 2).

Measure more than logins. Alongside use, the school records teacher time, workload and what students produce. In Korea, a teacher survey explained what the login figures could not (Lim, 2025).

In a university department. Agree one department-wide AI policy instead of leaving each lecturer to decide alone, and review it every semester. Where the university restricts a platform, name an approved alternative.

When resources are thin

Most of the evidence above comes from England, Wales, Estonia and large American districts. Many schools in Pakistan and similar systems start from somewhere else. AI arrives as a ministry programme or an NGO gift. Nobody holds the title of IT lead, and the staff channel is a WhatsApp group on teachers’ own phones.

Pakistan’s National AI Policy 2025 commits to AI programmes in schools and to training 200,000 people a year (Ministry of Information Technology and Telecommunication, 2025, p. 8). It plans 10,000 trainers by 2027 (p. 9). These are national skill targets. In September 2026 Punjab approved a curriculum change that adds AI as a subject from Class I to Class XII, with teachers to be trained and laboratories to be built first (Lodhi, 2026). A subject on the timetable is not an acceptable-use policy. I have found no provincial policy on how teachers and students may use AI tools in Pakistani schools.

Sindh’s programme with UNICEF and Khan Academy Pakistan shows how a provincial gift lands. Of 3,503 participants who completed the online training, comprising 3,436 government school teachers and 67 officials or faculty, 3,181 passed, and 58% of those trained were women. WhatsApp groups carried peer troubleshooting. Certification required six months’ continued use of Khanmigo in class (The News, 2026). In surveys of 2,146 teachers before and after, the share of teachers using AI to revise lesson content rose from zero to 79%. Those are self-reports with no comparison group, and the figures come from the department and its partners. I know of no independent evaluation. Chapter 10 treated this as teacher training. Here it shows how a gift can choose a school’s tool before the school has a policy.

Khyber Pakhtunkhwa launched a Global Virtual School in August 2026. An AI teacher delivers the syllabus for grades 6 to 12 in Urdu, English and Pashto, starting with 175 schools and planning for 500 (Dawn, 2026b). Teaching in three languages is a real strength for families. No evaluation exists yet, and the only sources are news reports of the launch (Dawn, 2026b; Bloom Pakistan, 2026).

The access ceiling is low. In an online survey of 64 English teachers in Sindh, 85.9% reached the internet mainly on a personal smartphone. Some 29.7% had no internet at school (Ghumro et al., 2026, p. 319). The Swiss finding that infrastructure barely mattered came from a rich system, and its authors say so (Schmitz et al., 2023, p. 11). Here, electricity, connection and devices sit at the top of the list of infrastructural prerequisites. The same Sindh teachers rated data costs and load shedding among their biggest challenges, (Ghumro et al., 2026, p. 323). An online survey can only reach teachers who are already online, so the real picture may be harder than these figures suggest. For a leader in such a district, my reading is that an AI plan has to work on a teacher’s own phone, with costly data and an unreliable power supply.

My thesis found that teachers and middle leaders pay the cost of that gap. Chapter 4 gives the example of the zoologist in my study, who paid from a personal account (Ilyas, 2026, p. 158, Participant M06). The thesis also names an institutional paradox. Institutions ‘that most need technology-enabled efficiency gains are least able to provide the conditions for their development’ (p. 210). These are university findings. A government school faces the same arithmetic with less room.

The thesis turned this into recommendations for university management. It called infrastructure ‘a precondition, not a complement, to all other interventions’ (Ilyas, 2026, p. 238). It asked for backup power in every teaching building, stable Wi-Fi, departmental pools of loan equipment and institutional backup plans. ‘These are prerequisites, not enhancements’ (p. 238). A district office could ask the same of its schools before it asks teachers to adopt AI.

Money sets the frame. In low-income countries, teacher salaries often take up to 75% or more of education spending, which leaves little room for new technology (Yarrow et al., 2026, p. 2). The World Bank paper suggests an order for such systems. Teacher-facing tools are often more cost-effective than student-facing ones in the early stages (p. 19). They need fewer devices, can run on the basic smartphones many teachers already have and can work where students lack access (p. 19). Rural accounting teachers in South Africa kept a learning community going on WhatsApp. One warned that ‘data is also costly’ (Oduro et al., 2024, p. S7). This fits the Estonian finding that teacher-facing use spread first, and England’s guidance that such use brings fewer risks. For a school with few devices, I think starting with tools teachers use for planning is both cheaper and closer to what the evidence supports. Even a plan based on phones has a data cost, and unless the school budgets for it, teachers will pay it themselves.

In Gujranwala, where I worked as an assistant education officer, notices travelled faster through the headteachers’ WhatsApp group than through any letter. A circular sent at night was being discussed in staffrooms by morning. That group was the real staff channel, though nobody had designed it as one. Any AI practice in such a district will spread the same way, so the group deserves the same care as a policy.

So the thin-resource version of this chapter’s answer is short. Start with teacher-facing uses before student-facing ones. Use the channel staff already share, and agree in writing which tools it may carry. Ask every ministry or partner programme for an independent evaluation before anyone calls it a success.

This chapter closes Part III. A school that builds shared practice still has to decide what its students should be able to know and show without the machine. Chapter 12 takes that question to universities, where fluent answers unsettle what a degree can certify. Then Chapter 13 asks what kind of teacher the AI era needs, and why that answer belongs to teachers themselves.

In IFDC terms, the school as a whole controls two of the four levels: the basic conditions and the chances to learn (Ilyas et al., 2026b). When a headteacher protects time, keeps devices working and organises shared learning, every teacher’s classroom routines have something to stand on.

Questions for reflection

Take these questions to your next leadership team or staff meeting.

  1. In your own school, which use of AI has stayed in use the longest, and who or what has kept it going?

  2. Who owns your school’s AI policy, and when did staff last see it change?

  3. What did your last free tool cost in staff time, data and training?

  4. Which teachers had a say in the last technology your school bought?

  5. What would parents in your community need to hear first, and in which language?

Framework connections

This chapter uses two frameworks from implementation science, the study of how new practices take hold. Both come from health research. The Consolidated Framework for Implementation Research puts the people who receive a change at the centre, and includes factors that affect equity (Damschroder et al., 2022, p. 1). RE-AIM asks whether a programme reaches people, works, is taken up, is carried out as planned and lasts (Glasgow et al., 2019, p. 2). Lasting means that a programme continues after its first funding ends. Most AI pilots in this chapter have not yet passed that test. Chapter 10 placed each teacher’s growth in DigCompEdu and UNESCO’s framework. This chapter adds the conditions in the school that those frameworks take for granted.
