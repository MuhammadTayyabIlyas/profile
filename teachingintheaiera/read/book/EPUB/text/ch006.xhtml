Chapter 4. Planning with a Tireless Assistant

In the summer term of 2024, science teachers in 68 English secondary schools took part in an unusual experiment. Researchers split the schools at random. Teachers in one half planned their Year 7 and 8 science lessons (students aged 11 to 13) with ChatGPT and an online guide, while teachers in the other half planned without any generative AI. Each week, both groups recorded how long their planning took (Roy et al., 2024).

One experienced teacher, whom the report calls Teacher B, had a low-attaining class that the school’s ready-made lessons did not suit. She used ChatGPT for about nine in ten of her Year 7 and 8 lessons. Her prompts described the class and what the students had already learned, and she followed up with requests to refine what came back. She also asked colleagues to share prompts that worked, because ‘once you’ve got the prompt right, you will save time’. ChatGPT saved her some time, but she valued something else more. ‘Students are getting a much better deal, having spent the same amount of time’ (Roy et al., 2024, p. 75).

Two colleagues in the same trial tell different stories. Teacher A, an experienced science leader, used ChatGPT for about one lesson in ten, mostly to write questions and answers. It made her ‘more creative’, she said, but it did not save her time overall. Teacher C, a specialist in another subject who also teaches lower-secondary science, used it to write cover work for days when he expected to be away. He estimated that planning three cover lessons this way saved him ‘3 or 4 hours’ (Roy et al., 2024, pp. 75–76).

So one tool, in one trial, saved one teacher hours, another a little and a third nothing at all. What each teacher brought to it made the difference. Teacher B knew her class and spent effort describing it to the machine, and Teacher C knew exactly which task he wanted to hand over. That pattern runs through this chapter.

The chapter’s central question follows from it. How can AI support planning without weakening teachers’ judgement? My short answer is that AI can draft, adapt and reformat, and the best trials show that it saves real minutes each week. The design work that makes a plan fit a class still falls to the teacher, and somebody pays for that work in time and skill. Some teachers also reach the saving far more easily than others.

I call that design work teacher-design burden, and much of this chapter is about it. Everything here concerns teachers using AI for their own planning. That use is open to teachers in every phase, including primary teachers whose students are too young for most AI tools.

What the trials measured

Start with the strongest evidence. In the 2024 trial, teachers using ChatGPT spent 69% as much time planning as the comparison group, 56.2 minutes a week against 81.5. The evaluators gave that saving of 25.3 minutes a week a high security rating (Roy et al., 2024, p. 4). An expert panel, which did not know how the teachers had made each resource, found no evidence of a difference in quality between the two groups.

Use was more modest than the main result suggests. Most teachers used ChatGPT for one or two activities in a lesson, usually questions, quizzes or ideas for activities. They turned to it, and to the guide, less often as the term went on (Roy et al., 2024, p. 4). The 25.3 minutes seem to have come from small, repeated jobs, not from whole lessons handed over. For a teacher who is new to these tools, that suggests a sensible first step. Try the tool on one routine task, such as a quiz, before trusting it with more.

A second trial tested a tool built for English schools. Oak National Academy’s Aila walks teachers through planning a lesson that follows the national curriculum for England. In autumn 2025, researchers allocated 464 primary teachers in 108 schools at random. Teachers with Aila spent 76% as much time planning as the comparison group, 2.5 hours a week against 3.3. The saving came to 48.6 minutes a week, and the quality of their resources was no worse (Roy et al., 2026, p. 6).

Again, the details make the main result look smaller. Teachers used Aila for only 7% of their lessons on average, and their use fell over the ten weeks. Fewer than one in five felt it meaningfully influenced their teaching and planning, and many preferred other AI tools. Some found its outputs generic or inflexible. Aila, the evaluators note, deliberately asks for ‘more involvement’ from the teacher (Roy et al., 2026, pp. 6–7). The saving came from a tool that most teachers used for a small share of their lessons. So I read 48.6 minutes as what light use achieved in these schools, not as a guide to what heavier use would bring.

Early-career teachers, and those less confident in their subject or teaching knowledge, were more likely than similar colleagues to see their workload fall (Roy et al., 2026, p. 6). The comparison group could still use other AI tools, such as ChatGPT. Oak’s own newsletter, which some participants may have received, promised the tool could ‘save five hours a week’ (p. 13). The evaluators found no direct evidence that such emails shaped teachers’ diaries. Whatever the newsletter promised, the measured saving was under fifty minutes.

A third trial, a World Bank study part-funded by Microsoft’s AI Economy Institute, found no saving at all. In Lima, Peru, researchers gave sixth-grade teachers in half of 390 public schools a day of training, a Microsoft Copilot licence and eight months of light follow-up. Adoption rose sharply. At the end of the year, 607 of the trial’s 1,269 teachers reported how long seven core tasks had last taken them, from lesson planning to differentiating materials. On those reports, the trial found ‘precisely estimated null effects’ on time (Glave et al., 2026, pp. 2, 17).

What changed was skill. Teachers in the programme wrote better prompts. They also became 9.9 percentage points less likely to misjudge a weak prompt, and 6.7 points less likely to accept a poor AI answer as good. The gains in prompt quality were largest among teachers over 54, the median age (Glave et al., 2026, pp. 2, 11).

A fourth trial, in Germany, measured time and quality together, though its teachers worked in adult education. On four set tasks that took the comparison group about 90 minutes, teachers with ChatGPT finished about 34 minutes sooner. Those who also had AI training finished about 47 minutes sooner. Blind expert ratings of their work rose by 0.37 and 0.42 standard deviations, and the training changed the time but not the quality (Appel et al., 2026, p. 14). The paper is still a pre-proof, and none of the material reached a classroom.

Both English trials also show where AI helped. In the 2024 trial, teachers used ChatGPT most often to create questions or quizzes, and 36% of users did so every time or most times. Finding ideas for activities came next (Roy et al., 2024, p. 70). Teachers in interviews said ChatGPT wrote comprehension and recall questions faster than they could, and that most were broadly accurate. Cover work, as Teacher C found, suits it well too.

Less confident teachers may gain most. In 2024, the evaluators found signs that ChatGPT saved more time for teachers less confident in physics or in their science knowledge (Roy et al., 2024, p. 5). Some Aila users valued it for planning lessons on unfamiliar topics (Roy et al., 2026, p. 7). That is a real benefit, and it carries a risk. A teacher who cannot judge the content well has to check it all the more carefully.

Together, these trials give a clear but narrow picture. The evidence that AI can cut planning time is strong in England, where two well-run trials found savings of roughly 25 and 49 minutes a week. Elsewhere it is mixed. None of these trials measured what students learned from the lessons. The minutes are real, and they are far smaller than many of the claims teachers hear.

My reading of these trials is that teachers should treat AI as a modest helper, not a rescue. Twenty-five or forty-nine minutes a week matters to a tired teacher, but it will not fix an overloaded timetable. The bigger question, which no trial has yet answered, is what students learn from AI-assisted lessons. Until that evidence arrives, I would judge a planning tool by the quality of the lesson it helps to produce, not only by the minutes it saves.

Hours or minutes

Planning sits near the top of what teachers ask AI to do. In the OECD’s 2024 survey of lower-secondary teachers, around one in three across member systems had used AI in their work. Of those users, 64% had generated lesson plans or activities with it (OECD, 2025, p. 59). Because planning is one of the most common uses, checking AI-made plans is likely to be a regular part of the work for teachers who use AI.

Surveys of time saved tell a bigger story than the trials. In a US survey funded by the Walton Family Foundation, teachers who used AI weekly estimated that it saved them 5.9 hours a week. The report presents this as six weeks a school year (Gallup & Walton Family Foundation, 2025). In England, a Teacher Tapp poll in September 2026 found that 54% of classroom teachers who had used AI in the previous week had saved up to two hours. Another 27% said it had saved them no time at all, a rise of 11 points since April 2025 (Teacher Tapp, 2026b).

Why do surveys find hours where trials find minutes? Surveys ask people to estimate, while the two English trials asked teachers to record their planning every week against a comparison group. Estimates can drift with hope and with marketing. The OECD, following Selwyn and colleagues, raises a subtler point. Even careful records of tool use can miss the ‘hidden labour’ of reviewing and checking what AI produces, unless teachers make their revisions inside the tool (OECD, 2026, p. 57).

The gap between the two kinds of number is itself worth knowing. When a colleague, a vendor or a headline promises hours, ask how anyone measured them.

Where the time goes

A saving in minutes says nothing about where the effort went. The 2024 trial offers a rare look inside the work, because researchers studied eleven of the teachers’ ChatGPT conversations. All eleven left out students’ personal data. About half gave some teaching context, usually just the year group. Only two showed a teacher asking ChatGPT to improve what it had produced. Six used follow-up prompts to ask for related material instead, such as answers or a worksheet (Roy et al., 2024, p. 69).

The same trial shows why context matters. Its expert panel gave three lessons an average of three or less out of five for scientific accuracy, so the evaluators traced each one back to its ChatGPT conversation. Two contained no errors from ChatGPT at all. In the third, ChatGPT had written statements with deliberate errors for students to spot, and the slides never said so (Roy et al., 2024, p. 76). The teacher knew the purpose of those errors. A reader of the slides did not.

Smaller studies show what checking has to catch. Two researchers produced a sound first-grade science plan with ChatGPT in 30 minutes. Yet it recommended a children’s book that does not exist, ‘a fake book by a real author’ (Powell & Courchesne, 2024, p. 11). Of 2,230 activities in 310 civics plans from ChatGPT, Gemini and Copilot, only 10% called for higher-order thinking. Nearly half asked students simply to remember (Trust et al., 2025, p. 429). Another team ran the same lesson-plan prompts again and again. ‘Simply by chance’, some plans missed over a quarter of the criteria while others were more than 90% complete (Dornburg & Davin, 2025, p. 343).

All of this takes time that diaries may not capture. IFF Research and IOE, UCL’s Faculty of Education and Society (2025) studied teachers’ workload in England for the government. A few teachers told them they had spent as long editing AI outputs as making the material themselves (p. 55).

Records of tool use miss even more. In a pilot in Karnataka, India, teachers adapted their AI-drafted plans all the time. Yet only 85 of the 5,542 plans they created showed any edit on the platform. Most of the changes happened in their heads, the researchers found, partly for lack of time (Varuvel Dennison et al., 2026, pp. 16–17). This is why I trust platform records of editing only a little. For a school leader, low edit counts are not evidence that teachers accept AI plans without thought.

Saved time also moves around. In the 2024 trial, some teachers spent it on other planning or teaching tasks, and others cut their overall workload (Roy et al., 2024, p. 4). A review of 40 workload studies warns that removing one type of work, ‘for example, lesson planning’, does not cure a shortage of time (Creagh et al., 2025, p. 677). Its authors describe a gap between the teacher someone wants to be and the teacher they have time to be. The Education Policy Institute, in a report produced with the National Education Union, makes a similar point. Drawing on evidence to Parliament, it warns that time saved on planning ‘may just translate to further responsibilities’ (Reynolds, 2026, p. 4). Decide in advance what saved time is for. A department could agree, for example, to spend saved planning time on reading students’ work.

The evidence on this hidden work is emerging. Several studies describe it, and none has yet measured it. A teacher who saves twenty minutes on writing may spend fifteen of them checking, and a weekly total would show only the difference.

Teacher-design burden

Two answers from my interviews belong side by side.

‘Did you create any digital resource for your classroom presentations?’ I asked a geographer.

‘Yes, these days I mostly use AI tools in my classroom because they produce things very quickly and save time,’ he said. ‘If I have a 90-minute class, instead of spending time structuring everything in a Word file, I tell the AI to generate an outline, and I implement it from there’ (Ilyas, 2026, Participant H03).

I asked another lecturer what made preparing such resources hard.

‘Actually, time limitation is a major issue,’ she said. Then came the list. Several courses, each with assignments, presentations and quizzes. Research. Paperwork. Marks to upload. Power cuts on campus, and a generator at home that helps. ‘Realistically, I often have to take time away from my family to prepare these resources’ (Ilyas, 2026, Participant L3).

Same university, same tools on offer. One teacher had found minutes. The other was still paying with family time. Either way, knowledge of the class still had to come from the teacher.

One teacher in the 2024 trial described how their prompts had changed. ‘The prompt has gone from [short] to that long’, they said. It now carries ‘the year group, and their reading age level and what they should do’ (Roy et al., 2024, p. 69). That longer prompt carries knowledge the machine does not have. Someone has to supply it.

Before a tool can help a particular class learn, a teacher has to invest that kind of pedagogical effort. I call this teacher-design burden. It is the effort a teacher must put in before a tool helps learning, as distinct from simply having access to the tool. The term is mine, and none of the studies in this chapter uses it. It grows, though, from older work on teachers and technology.

Punya Mishra and Matthew Koehler describe teaching as ‘a complex cognitive skill occurring in an ill-structured, dynamic environment’ (Mishra & Koehler, 2006, p. 1020). Adding a new technology, they argue, ‘is not the same as adding another module to a course’. It raises questions about content and pedagogy ‘that can overwhelm even experienced instructors’ (p. 1030). Their framework, now usually called TPACK, describes the knowledge a teacher needs to fit a tool to a subject and to the learners in front of them. The tool cannot supply that knowledge for itself.

Janine Remillard’s review of research on how teachers use mathematics curricula offers a second root. Drawing on Brown’s work, she describes teachers who, by turns, ‘offloaded’ design onto the curriculum, ‘adapted’ its suggestions or ‘improvised’ from their own ideas (Remillard, 2005, p. 223). Brown called the skill behind these choices pedagogical design capacity. It is the ability ‘to perceive and mobilize existing resources in order to craft instructional contexts’ (Brown, 2002, as cited in Remillard, 2005, p. 228). An AI tool makes offloading easier than ever. Design capacity decides whether the offloading helps a class.

The burden shows up wherever researchers look closely. Aila’s evaluators found that teachers felt its outputs often needed adapting before they were ready for class (Roy et al., 2026, p. 6). In a survey by the NASUWT teachers’ union, 84% of members who used AI for planning said they used an AI tool they had adapted for their own purposes. The survey also asked whether these tools helped teachers adapt lessons for different learners. Of those who used them, 31% said a tool was a useful starting point but still left ‘a lot of work to adapt lessons’. Another 33% found its adaptations of limited use or impossible (NASUWT, 2025a, p. 11).

Tool makers face a trade-off here. Aila asks teachers for the year group, the purpose of the lesson and their students’ needs before it builds anything (Roy et al., 2026, p. 8). Its evaluators found that many teachers preferred other tools that were easier to use (p. 6). A tool that asks for design work feels slower, even when the design work is the point.

Even before AI, new materials cost planning time. In a national RAND survey, US teachers using a new instructional material spent about 30 more minutes a week planning than peers using none (Doan et al., 2025, p. 18). Skill reduces the burden without removing it. In the German trial, teachers with AI training wrote longer prompts, about 148 words against 116, and needed fewer of them (Appel et al., 2026, p. 18). Training in Lima made teachers sharper judges of AI output and saved them no time (Glave et al., 2026). Good prompting is itself design work.

Some school systems now teach that work directly. In New South Wales, a primary principal runs workshops for teachers across a wider group of schools in South-West Sydney. Teachers learn to give the tool their teaching context without personal details. They also learn to write explicit prompts, which ‘may take numerous iterations’, and to question the tool about its sources (Hoare & Smith-Ferguson, 2025, pp. 411–412). The account comes from the department that runs these schools, so I read it as a description of practice rather than evidence of results. Still, what the workshops teach matches what the trials suggest teachers need: class context, clear prompts and questions about sources.

Work outside education offers an analogy. In a widely cited experiment with 453 professionals, ChatGPT cut the time for writing tasks by 40% and raised quality by 18% (Noy & Zhang, 2023). The authors note that their tasks ‘did not require context-specific knowledge or precise factual accuracy’ (p. 190). A lesson plan needs both. That may be one reason why gains in planning look smaller than gains in generic writing.

My own doctoral research with university teachers in Pakistan shows the burden from another side. ‘The workload is large, so I can’t develop a detailed online resource that would eventually save time,’ one teacher, the botanist, explained (Ilyas, 2026, p. 168, Participant L5). My thesis calls this a cruel paradox. Teachers can see that effort now would save time later, yet the present workload blocks the effort. Another teacher said, ‘Multiple courses, research duties and paperwork leave little time for learning new technologies’ (Ilyas, 2026, p. 168, Participant L7). As the thesis concludes, tools that could save time are ‘least accessible to those who most need them’ (p. 210).

If the burden is real, schools should budget for it. The thesis argues that professional development should ‘recognise time as infrastructure, not personal inefficiency’, with shared planning time and recognition for the labour of redesign (Ilyas, 2026, p. 97). A tool that saves 25 minutes a week first costs the hours it takes to learn. Someone has to fund those hours, and too often the teacher’s evenings pay.

The plan is not the lesson

Planning is not paperwork, even when schools treat it that way. In the English workload study, teachers described planning as ‘creative, enjoyable work’ with an immediate benefit for students. On the whole, they did not want AI to replace it (IFF Research & IOE, UCL’s Faculty of Education and Society, 2025, pp. 24, 59). Part of the reason is that the thinking happens while a teacher plans.

Remillard’s review concludes that teaching is ‘a responsive and improvisational activity that cannot be scripted’ (Remillard, 2005, p. 234). A policy review of teacher development in low- and middle-income countries reaches a similar view. Structured lesson plans can help teachers try new techniques, it finds, but ‘overly scripted ones may be inappropriate to sustain pedagogical change across contexts’ (D’Angelo et al., 2022, p. 6). A generated plan is a script that someone else wrote, no matter how good it looks.

People who work with teachers have seen what can go missing. At a roundtable of sector experts, school trust leaders and academics, participants described teachers who felt they were losing expertise in their own subject when AI did the thinking about content. Some teachers lost spontaneity in lessons because they did not know an AI-made plan well enough. Participants saw the risk as greatest for early-career teachers, who ‘missed out on the learning and development of pedagogical concepts which underpin teaching’ (Reynolds, 2026, p. 11).

The same worry has reached staffrooms. Between December 2025 and April 2026, the share of teachers in a large English panel whose last lesson came from an AI-assisted plan rose from 14% to 20%. One teacher wrote to the panel about trainees in her school. She worried that they did not understand ‘how to plan, how topics fit together’, because ‘they aren’t designing or thinking through their own lessons’ (Teacher Tapp, 2026a). None of the research behind this chapter has yet measured how planning with AI shapes a teacher’s judgement over years.

Tool design can make matters worse. A 2025 risk assessment of AI teacher assistants found that many make it too easy to send content to a classroom without review. Their warnings about accuracy and bias are ‘often easy to miss’ (Common Sense Media, 2025, pp. 5, 17). The assessors judged novice teachers more likely to rely too heavily on generated content (p. 6).

Here two findings pull in different directions. In the Aila trial, teachers new to the profession were more likely to see their workload fall (Roy et al., 2026, p. 6). Yet the teachers who gain the most time may also have the most to learn from planning. Both can be true at once, because the trials counted minutes and never measured professional growth.

The teachers in these studies who used AI well treated its output as raw material. A mathematics teacher in Karnataka, India, put the reason plainly (Varuvel Dennison et al., 2026). ‘I would trust AI for geographical relevance, but not to deliver effective lessons in my classroom because it doesn’t know the background of my students’ (p. 20). In my thesis, teachers with higher digital competence used AI for drafts and outlines ‘while maintaining explicit verification routines’ (Ilyas, 2026, p. 212).

These accounts suggest a simple habit, the planning partner to the one in Chapter 3. Think first, then prompt. Before opening a tool, decide what students should learn, which mistake you expect and how you will know they have learned. Then ask the machine to help with the parts that need hands, not judgement.

Differentiation, SEND and paperwork

Differentiation is one of the heaviest planning loads, and one of the thinnest areas of evidence. In the same NASUWT survey, class teachers described the strain of planning for many different needs. ‘Differentiation ends up being blanket,’ wrote one teacher in Scotland, who doubted it was fit for purpose. Another, in England, said, ‘I make so many different scaffolds each lesson and am still being told it is not enough’ (NASUWT, 2025a, p. 10).

AI did not yet seem to carry much of that load. Only a third of those teachers (34%) used AI to help plan and prepare lessons, and only 7% did so regularly (NASUWT, 2025a, p. 10). The 2024 trial shows what careful use can look like. Teacher B used ChatGPT to adjust the difficulty of reading materials for her low-attaining class (Roy et al., 2024, p. 75). None of the research behind this chapter shows that AI-adapted materials help students with special educational needs learn more. So for differentiation I would use AI to save time on producing versions of a text. I would keep the decision about which student needs which version for myself.

Formal plans for individual students carry the highest stakes. Common Sense Media found generators for individual education plans (IEPs) and behaviour plans in three of the four teacher assistants it tested. With minimal input, one of them produced detailed plans with goals and accommodations. Real plans need observations, assessment data and records of past support, and most of that sits outside the tool. Only that product’s IEP generator reminded teachers to ‘review closely before implementation’ (Common Sense Media, 2025, p. 16). For any formal plan, I would treat AI wording as a draft at most, written only after people who know the student have gathered the observations and data.

One small experiment points to a safer use. Twenty-two novice special-education teachers wrote goals for five children with autism, working only from written statements of each child’s current performance. Those who drafted with ChatGPT wrote goals that trained raters scored higher, 9.56 against 6.78, and they took less time (Rakap, 2024, pp. 343–344). Its author concludes that ChatGPT should supplement professional judgement, not replace it (p. 346). The result looks promising but narrow, because the novices worked from written statements rather than from knowing the children, and the study counted each goal rather than each teacher, which can make a result look more certain than it is. The useful idea is the sequence: the professional description of the child comes first, and the tool only helps with wording.

Personal data deserves the same care. England’s guidance tells staff to use tools their school has approved, and to ask the data protection officer before entering any student’s personal data. In its own example, an administrator uses AI to draft an email to a parent about a child’s behaviour, then adds the student’s name and class (Department for Education, 2026b). All eleven conversations that researchers studied in the 2024 trial left students’ personal data out (Roy et al., 2024, p. 69). In practice, this means personal details go in after the AI has finished, as in the guidance’s own example, not before. The eleven trial conversations suggest the habit is already common, though eleven is too few to say how common.

Teacher-facing first

This chapter has stayed with teachers using AI for their own work, and that choice matters. England’s Department for Education sees ‘more immediate benefits and fewer risks from teacher-facing use of generative AI’ (Department for Education, 2025b). A primary teacher can plan with AI for a class of seven-year-olds who never touch the tool. Students’ own use is different. Some AI tools have age restrictions, and English guidance tells schools to review them before students use any tool (Department for Education, 2026b). Chapter 2 explains the age limits, and Chapter 5 turns to how students themselves use AI.

Teacher-facing use is not risk-free, as the invented book and the scripted plan show. It is the lighter route, though, and the one most schools can take first.

Who gets the saving

The saving does not reach everyone equally. In a RAND survey of US teachers in 2023–24, those in the lowest-poverty schools were more likely to use AI at all (Kaufman et al., 2025, p. 7). Among teachers who did use it for planning, though, 25% in the highest-poverty schools used it weekly, against 16% in the lowest-poverty schools (p. 8). Principals in the highest-poverty schools were about half as likely to report any AI guidance, 13% against 25% (p. 1). The concern is not only who uses AI but who uses it without support. A teacher in a poorer school who plans with AI every week, in a school with no guidance, does more of the checking alone.

Training is uneven everywhere. In the Gallup survey, 68% of US teachers had received no AI training from their school or district. More had taught themselves than had received training (Gallup & Walton Family Foundation, 2025). Teachers in schools with an AI policy estimated larger savings, 2.3 hours a week against 1.7, though a survey cannot show that the policy made the difference.

England shows a similar split. Private schools were three times as likely as state schools to have a clear school-wide staff strategy on AI, 27% against 9%. Teachers in schools with the most affluent intakes were more likely to have had formal training than those with the least affluent, 26% against 18% (Latham & Montacute, 2025, p. 1).

This pattern fits a finding of my thesis already quoted in this chapter (Ilyas, 2026, p. 210): the teachers who could gain most from a time-saving tool often have the least time, support and equipment to invest in it.

What to hand over, and what to keep

All this evidence points to a fair division of labour. AI can take the first draft, the question bank, the cover lesson, the reading passage at a different level and the email that needs a calmer tone. In good conditions, that saves a teacher somewhere between twenty-five and fifty minutes a week. The English trials support that much with confidence.

Near the end of one interview in my study, a lecturer told me how she had started using ChatGPT after a workshop. Her example was an exam paper.

‘I provided it with an outline, asked for 50 questions on that topic, five of which had to be application-based, keeping Bloom’s Taxonomy in mind,’ she said. ‘It gave me a large list, and I combined some to make my own paper’ (Ilyas, 2026, Participant L4).

Fifty questions in, one paper out. The machine drafted. She chose and combined, and the paper her students sat was hers.

Teachers keep the decisions that make a lesson belong to a class. What should these students learn next, and which mistake will they make? Which example will reach them, and how will the teacher know? Those questions make up teacher-design burden, and no tool in these studies answered them on a teacher’s behalf.

Schools decide whether that work falls on teachers’ evenings or happens in protected time. They also decide whether the saving goes back into teaching or into new tasks. AI can carry part of the load, and the judgement stays with the person who knows the class.

In practice

These routines turn the argument into ordinary planning habits. They draw on the research above, and nobody has yet tested them as programmes.

Think first, then prompt. A teacher of a Year 4 class (ages eight to nine), planning a fractions lesson, writes three lines before she opens any tool. She notes what students should be able to do by the end, the mistake she expects and how she will check. Only then does she ask for practice questions, and she drops any that do not test her three lines.

Context cards with no names. A secondary science teacher keeps a short card for each class, with the year group, typical reading age, prior learning and what worked last time. She pastes it into her prompts and leaves out names and anything else personal (Department for Education, 2026b). One trial teacher learned to do much the same (Roy et al., 2024, p. 69).

Check every source and one claim. Before a generated resource reaches students, the teacher checks every book, website and quotation it cites, and at least one claim, against a trusted source. One invented book in a first-grade plan shows why (Powell & Courchesne, 2024).

Shared prompt banks. A department keeps the prompts that worked, each with a note on the class and topic, so one teacher’s design work serves everyone. Teacher B in the 2024 trial asked colleagues for exactly this (Roy et al., 2024, p. 75). Leaders protect a little meeting time for it and count that time as planning (Ilyas, 2026, p. 97).

SEND (special educational needs and disabilities) paperwork with the child in view. A special educational needs coordinator lets an approved AI tool suggest wording for a student’s targets, without names or identifying details. The teachers who know the child then rewrite each target from their own observations. Nothing goes into a plan that someone who knows the child has not checked (Rakap, 2024; Common Sense Media, 2025).

One lesson from scratch. A mentor asks a trainee to plan one lesson a week without AI and talk it through, so the thinking still happens (Reynolds, 2026; Teacher Tapp, 2026a).

Two weeks of honest timing. Before deciding whether a tool saves time, a teacher keeps a simple diary for two weeks, as the teachers in the trials did. She records every minute of planning, including prompting, checking and editing, and compares a week with the tool against a week without it.

In a university course. Use AI to draft a large question bank, as the lecturer with the question bank did above, then choose and rewrite the questions yourself (Ilyas, 2026, Participant L4). Share the bank with colleagues who teach the same course, so that the design work is done once rather than many times.

When resources are thin

Every routine above assumes a working device, a connection and time to learn. Many teachers lack at least one of them. I call the conditions that must hold before a tool can work at all infrastructural prerequisites. The idea extends Peggy Ertmer’s first-order barriers, which include access to computers and software, time to plan and technical support (Ertmer, 1999, p. 48). Ertmer judged such barriers relatively easy to remove ‘once money is allocated’ (p. 50). Where the money never arrives, teachers often pay instead.

My thesis recorded this subsidy directly. ‘We arranged four or five LEDs on our own,’ said the botanist quoted earlier on workload (Ilyas, 2026, p. 158, Participant L5). The zoologist in my study said, ‘I paid from my personal account. The university still has not reimbursed me’ (Ilyas, 2026, p. 158, Participant M06). My thesis calls this ‘a financial subsidy from teachers to institutional failure’ (p. 204).

The national picture shows the scale. In 2021–22, only 70% of Pakistan’s schools had electricity (Pakistan Institute of Education, 2022, p. 13). My thesis argues that in such settings the work of making technology available at all, which it calls antecedent work, ‘may constitute the more demanding skill set’ (Ilyas, 2026, p. 204).

Planning help can still reach such teachers if it travels light. In Sierra Leone, only 34% of secondary schools had electricity in 2020 (Björkegren et al., 2025, p. 6). Over 17 months, 529 teachers there sent 40,350 questions to an AI chatbot on WhatsApp (p. 1). On average, a web page used 3,107 times more data than a chatbot reply. A WhatsApp question cost 98% less than loading one page, even counting the AI’s running costs. Most questions asked for facts or concepts, and 11% concerned lesson planning and assessment (p. 3). Use at night suggested that teachers planned with it. Forty per cent said they had shared their phone with colleagues so that others could use the chatbot too (p. 8). I find this a useful example of planning help that works with very limited infrastructure. The figures describe use and cost, not whether lessons or learning improved, and two of the authors work at Fab Inc, so I would weigh claims about its value with care. The phone sharing is a reminder that in such schools one device often serves several teachers.

In Karnataka, India, the education department requires teachers to submit lesson plans in a set five-stage format for its records. Before a pilot of Shiksha Copilot, an AI planning tool, teachers spent 45 to 90 minutes on each plan, and many wrote them by hand. Using the tool on their phones, most finished in under 15 minutes (Varuvel Dennison et al., 2026, p. 17). The saving came with design work attached. Curators had to edit 94.4% of the content blocks in the Kannada versions of the plans, because literal translation from English often made little sense (p. 16). The plans also had no pictures. Teachers kept using their own phones to show images, since their classrooms had no projectors (p. 20). With five of the nine authors at Microsoft Research India, I would want an independent study before treating the time figures as settled.

An English teacher with 18 years in the classroom described what the paperwork leaves out. ‘In reality, we write and submit the same lesson plans each year just for records. Actually, we teach much more than what’s written down’ (Varuvel Dennison et al., 2026, p. 17).

In my years as an assistant education officer in Gujranwala, I read many lesson-plan registers on school visits. The neatest registers did not always belong to the best lessons. A tool that fills a register in fifteen minutes is a real gift to a tired teacher. It is not, by itself, a better lesson.

Teachers in these places plan for failure as a matter of routine. The botanist, who helped arrange those screens, also sends videos to students on WhatsApp before class. ‘If the LED or tablet won’t connect, at least they have the video on their phones’ (Ilyas, 2026, p. 157, Participant L5). Another named the root problem as ‘the stability and speed of the internet’ (Ilyas, 2026, p. 156, Participant L2). A paper from the same study reads this practice as a shadow learning management system. Where formal platforms are absent, unreliable or prohibited, WhatsApp carries the core teaching work (Ilyas et al., 2026c, p. 343).

  • Teacher-facing planning is the lighter route in such schools. One teacher with one phone can draft a plan, translate a word list or prepare questions, and the students need no device at all. The design burden does not shrink, though. Someone still has to check the language, find the pictures and fit the plan to the class.

  • The routines above still work on a small budget. A teacher can write the three lines of ‘think first, then prompt’ on paper. She can draft a week’s questions on one phone while the connection holds, then copy them onto the board or print a single sheet. Teachers in Karnataka adapted their plans with ideas from cluster meetings and WhatsApp groups (Varuvel Dennison et al., 2026, p. 17). A shared prompt bank can live in the same place. Chapter 7 returns to these conditions in full.

In IFDC terms, planning with AI depends first on the basic conditions: a working device, a connection and protected time (Ilyas et al., 2026b). Only when those are in place can teachers make good choices about what to hand over and what to keep. When the basic conditions are missing, teachers pay for the time AI saves out of their own evenings and pockets.

Questions for reflection

Take these questions to your next department or phase meeting, or into a quiet moment after school.

  1. In your own school, which planning task would you refuse to hand to a machine, even one that did it well?

  2. When did a generated resource last take longer to fix than it saved?

  3. What would go on a context card for your most demanding class, and what must stay off it?

  4. If your team saved an hour a week, who would decide where that hour goes?

  5. How will the newest teachers in your school learn to plan if the first draft always comes from a machine?
