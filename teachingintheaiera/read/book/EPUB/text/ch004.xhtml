Chapter 2. What AI Is, and What It Is Not

Teachers who watch PakEdX at work for the first time often ask me the same question. A lesson plan fills the screen in seconds, neat and confident, and someone leans forward. ‘Where does it find all this?’ Most expect me to name a website, or a vast library of lesson plans sitting somewhere on a server. The honest answer surprises them. Underneath, the language model finds nothing at all. Unless someone connects it to a search engine or a set of documents, it simply writes the most likely next words, one small piece at a time. It learned the patterns behind those words long before the lesson began.

Teachers have good reason to ask. In my doctoral research, one university teacher said students ‘are using AI a lot’ and ‘often know more than I do’ about using technology (Ilyas, 2026, p. 161, Participant M01). Other teachers in the study said much the same. I described this in my thesis as a role reversal, with students becoming a resource for teachers’ own learning (Ilyas, 2026, p. 161). A role reversal is easier to handle when teachers understand the tools their students already use.

Uncertainty about how these tools work reaches well beyond Pakistan. When researchers asked 130 Indonesian school teachers how ChatGPT works, about one in five described it as searching the internet or a database (Aji et al., 2026). That study is a recent preprint from one country, and its findings may not travel. Still, it names a picture many of us carry. We imagine a very fast librarian, fetching answers from shelves.

The picture matters because it shapes trust. A librarian fetches what already exists, so we expect the answer to be as reliable as the shelf. The reality is different. As I put it in my thesis, AI ‘provides immediate, confident responses that may be incorrect or incomplete, but appear authoritative’ (Ilyas, 2026, p. 167).

This chapter asks what teachers need to understand about how AI works. The answer is less technical than many fear. A teacher does not need to build a model, any more than a driver needs to build an engine. Yet a basic understanding of what happens inside the tool is enough to change how we check, trust and teach with these tools. The chapter builds that picture in four steps, looks at where teachers stand today, then turns the picture into ground rules for safe use.

What happens when you press enter

Start with the moment you press enter. The tool first breaks your words into small pieces that engineers call tokens. A token might be a whole word, part of a word or a single character, and each one becomes a number the system can work with (Miao & Holmes, 2023).

Then the model predicts. Given the text so far, it estimates how likely each possible next token is, picks one and adds it. If the text so far reads ‘The cat sat on the’, then ‘mat’ or ‘sofa’ is likely and ‘equation’ is not. The model repeats that step, again and again, until the answer is complete. Your phone’s predictive text works on a similar principle, at a far smaller scale. England’s training materials for teachers list predictive text, autocorrect and spam filters among the narrow forms of AI already around us (Department for Education [DfE], 2026d). I find the phone comparison useful to share with colleagues. Most of us have seen predictive text offer a word that fits the sentence but not what we meant. A chatbot can do the same thing across a whole answer.

Figure 2.1. How a language model writes an answer. Your question is split into small pieces, the model picks the likeliest next piece again and again, and a fluent page results. Fluent is not the same as true.

The model learns those likelihoods during training. Developers feed it enormous amounts of text and let it practise predicting the next token, billions of times, adjusting itself after each guess. GPT-3, released in 2020, had 175 billion of these adjustable settings, which engineers call parameters. Most of its training text came from a filtered copy of the public web, gathered between 2016 and 2019. English Wikipedia, which many people imagine as the main source, made up just 3% of the training mix (Brown et al., 2020).

Those figures describe a model from 2020, so treat them as an illustration rather than a current specification. The principle has not changed. Dates matter too, because a model has no information about anything that happened after its developers gathered the training text. Only a product that searches the web, or a user who pastes in the news, can fill that gap.

A model trained only to predict text is not yet a helpful assistant. Ask it a question and it may simply continue your text, or drift off topic. So developers tune it with human feedback. For InstructGPT, a 2022 model built to follow instructions, OpenAI hired about 40 contractors to write example answers and rank the model’s replies (Ouyang et al., 2022).

People preferred the replies of a small tuned model, with 1.3 billion parameters, to those of the far larger GPT-3. Tuning made the model more helpful. It gave the model no way to check facts. On tasks such as summarising a passage, the tuned model still added information that was not in the passage 21% of the time, against 41% for GPT-3 (Ouyang et al., 2022). The figures come from a 2022 model, but the lesson holds: when a tool summarises a text for you, check the summary against the original, because added material is easy to miss there.

What we meet on the screen is a chatbot, a further layer around the model. The chatbot frames the exchange as a dialogue, so the model’s next words take the shape of a helpful reply (Shanahan, 2024). England’s training materials put the point plainly. The model ‘isn’t human, but it’s designed to interact like a person might’ (Department for Education, 2026d).

Teachers cannot watch any of this happen. England’s materials describe generative AI as a ‘black box’, where we see the input and the output but not the process in between (Department for Education, 2026e). They also note that this hidden process makes it harder to judge whether an answer is correct or fair, and harder to fix a system that goes wrong. In a classroom, that means we cannot judge an answer by asking how the tool reached it. We can only judge the answer itself, against a source we trust.

A chatbot also works only with what the conversation, or the product around it, supplies. It has no information about your students, your school or last week’s lesson unless someone provides it. Common Sense Media (2025), reviewing AI assistants for teachers, notes that good teaching requires deep knowledge of individual students that AI lacks. In practice, I treat a lesson plan from a tool as a plan for an average class that does not exist.

Every model also has a limit on how much text it can take into account at once, which engineers call its context window. GPT-3’s window held 2,048 tokens, a few pages of text (Brown et al., 2020). Newer tools hold far more. In October 2026, Google’s help page for schools listed 32 thousand tokens for its standard education editions and 1 million with Google AI Pro for Education (Google, 2026b). The model cannot use text beyond the window, so a long conversation can lose its earliest messages. The practical step is to start a new conversation for a new task, and to repeat key instructions in a long one. If a tool seems to ignore something you asked for earlier, the limited window is a likely reason.

The models themselves change every few months. At the time of writing, in November 2026, the best-known families include OpenAI’s GPT, Google’s Gemini, Anthropic’s Claude and xAI’s Grok. Chinese labs have released strong open-weight models, such as Zhipu’s GLM-5 series, Moonshot’s Kimi K3, DeepSeek and Alibaba’s Qwen. ‘Open-weight’ means that anyone can download the model and run it on their own computer or server. A university that does this does not pay for each question, and its students’ data does not have to leave the campus. Names and version numbers will have changed by the time you read this. The basic idea in this chapter, predicting the next words, has not.

A newer development is agentic AI. Instead of answering one question, an agent is given a goal and works through several steps on its own. It searches, opens files, writes and checks its work, and only then reports back. For teachers, this matters because an agent can now complete a whole assignment, including the research, not only the final text. The checking habits in this chapter matter even more when a machine has taken several steps that nobody watched.

AI can also make pictures, video, speech and music. Google’s Veo and OpenAI’s Sora produce short videos from a written description. In September 2026, Suno released version 6 of its music models, built in partnership with Warner Music, BMG and Believe and trained partly on music licensed from them (“Suno Launches New AI Music Models,” 2026). These tools open real opportunities. A student who cannot draw or play an instrument can now turn an idea into an image, a short film or a song. The rights questions around such tools are not yet fully settled, so schools should check the terms before students publish what they make.

Generated media can also carry bias. One study asked a popular image generator, Stable Diffusion, to show people from six racial groups in 32 professions. It found strong stereotypes. Nearly all Middle Eastern men, for example, appeared bearded, brown-skinned and in traditional dress. In follow-up experiments, people who saw stereotyped AI faces became more biased themselves, while people who saw inclusive faces became less biased, even when they knew the images were made by AI (AlDahoul et al., 2025). An image a student generates for a project is never just a picture. It is a choice about who is shown, and how.

Prediction also explains why the same question can bring different answers. The model chooses among likely tokens rather than always taking the likeliest, so two runs rarely match word for word. In an experiment described later in this chapter, one essay received different scores across 50 identical requests (Warr et al., 2024). So a teacher should treat any single answer as one sample among many.

Many products now add tools around the model. Some search the web, and many teacher assistants pull passages from textbooks or curriculum guides before they answer. Common Sense Media (2025) explains that this retrieval ‘can help reduce inaccuracies’, though it ‘doesn’t eliminate the fundamental limitations of generative AI’. Shanahan (2024) draws a useful distinction here. The language model inside such a product remains a predictor of text, and only the wider system consults outside sources. So the honest answer to ‘Where does it find all this?’ depends on the product. The model itself finds nothing, while the product around it may search, and the search can still fail.

Generative AI is also only one kind of AI. Adaptive learning tools choose the next task from a student’s earlier answers, and spam filters sort messages by patterns they have learned (Department for Education, 2026d). Systems like these classify, rank or recommend. Generative tools write, draw and speak. Most of this book concerns the second group, because it is the one that changed homework.

Every source, from regulators to the engineers who built these models, describes the same mechanism, even when they argue about what it means.

Fluent is not the same as true

Official guidance speaks with confidence. Ofsted, the school inspectorate in England, says in its report on early adopters that generative AI ‘does not understand anything’ (Ofsted, 2025a). England’s training materials add that the model does not think (Department for Education, 2026d).

Researchers are less sure. In a 2022 survey, 327 researchers in natural language processing considered whether a model trained only on text could, with enough data and computing power, understand language ‘in some non-trivial sense’. Just over half, 51%, agreed (Michael et al., 2022). The question was hypothetical, and the survey closed months before ChatGPT appeared. The survey also left the word ‘understand’ undefined. Official guidance states as settled what researchers still debate, and a teacher can say so honestly when a student asks whether the machine understands.

One influential critique argues that a language model stitches together forms of language without any reference to meaning. On this view, the coherence we see in its answers sits partly ‘in the eye of the beholder’ (Bender et al., 2021). Melanie Mitchell and David Krakauer (2023), reviewing the debate, take a more open view. Human understanding, they suggest, rests on concepts, meaning inner models of categories, situations and events, including what causes what. Yet they leave room for machines with ‘new modes of understanding’, unlike our own. The question remains open, and it returns in Chapter 12.

Teachers do not need to settle this debate. A safer line describes what the tool lacks. Put simply, a language model has no built-in check against reality. It produces text that fits the patterns of its training, and a good fit with those patterns can still be false.

Our everyday language makes this harder to remember. We say the tool ‘knows’, ‘thinks’ or ‘believes’, because those are the words we use for anyone who talks to us. The computer scientist Murray Shanahan (2024) warns that such loose talk stops being harmless once the products sound this convincing. He urges us to ‘resist the siren call of anthropomorphism’, which means resisting the pull to treat the tool as a person.

Children feel the same pull. A 2026 review of 35 studies found that children often form social ties with chatbots (Jayathilake & Ma, 2026). Some held two ideas at once, knowing the chatbot was a machine while chatting with it as if it were a friend. The authors call this dual consciousness. So telling students once that it is a program is unlikely to be enough on its own.

Even young children draw some line between a chatbot and a person. In a small 2026 study in the United States, 23 children aged five and six told stories with a chatbot character, a parent or both. They rated the parent as more human-like, yet still credited the chatbot with a good deal of perception and knowledge. Asked which session they liked best, 15 of 19 children chose one that included the chatbot (Kim et al., 2026). The authors suggest that tools for young children should keep reminding them that the system works from patterns in data.

Language in the classroom can help a little. In England, an AI curriculum for students aged 11 to 14 deliberately avoided human-like words and images. Before the lessons, 16.5% of students’ definitions of AI described it in human terms. Afterwards, 10.7% did (Waite et al., 2024). Far fewer students answered afterwards, 112 against 474 before, and the authors urge caution.

Companies now say it plainly too. Google’s guidance for parents describes Gemini as ‘an AI tool, not a person to confide in’ (Google, 2026a).

For teachers, the practical lesson is simple. A chatbot’s confidence tells us nothing about whether it is right, and young students in particular may treat a friendly voice as a knowing one. I would make one habit routine from the first lesson: whenever a machine gives an answer, someone in the room asks how we could check it.

Why it makes things up

A system that predicts plausible text will sometimes produce plausible text that is false. Engineers call this hallucination. The word suggests a rare malfunction, yet it describes an ordinary result of how the system works.

Some errors are easy to spot. England’s training materials give the example of an AI suggesting glue in tomato sauce, so that the cheese stays on a pizza. Others are subtle and much harder to catch (Department for Education, 2026e).

Reference lists show the problem most clearly. In a 2023 study, researchers asked ChatGPT to write 84 short papers and then checked all 636 citations. Of the citations from GPT-3.5, 55% referred to works that do not exist. For GPT-4, the figure fell to 18%. Even the real references often carried mistakes in details such as page numbers, volumes or dates, in 43% of cases for GPT-3.5 and 24% for GPT-4. Book chapters were the weakest point, since even GPT-4 invented 70% of the chapters it cited (Walters & Wilder, 2023). A fabricated reference reads exactly like a real one, which is why it slips past a busy reader.

Mathematics tells a similar story. In the Turkish school trial described in Chapter 1, the plain GPT-4 helper gave a correct answer only 51% of the time. It made logical errors 42% of the time and arithmetic errors 8% of the time (Bastani et al., 2025). In mathematics, I would ask students to check the tool's working step by step, not only its final answer.

Bigger models do not cure the problem, and sometimes worsen it. On a test of 817 questions built around common misconceptions, the best model answered truthfully on 58% of questions, against 94% for people. The larger models were less truthful, because they reproduced popular falsehoods more faithfully (Lin et al., 2022). A falsehood that many people believe is exactly the kind a model repeats, and those are often the misconceptions we spend lessons correcting. A more powerful tool is therefore no reason to check less.

Why does this happen? A 2025 paper by researchers at OpenAI and Georgia Tech offers one explanation, though it is still a preprint and comes partly from a developer. It opens with a telling test. The authors asked an open-source model for the lead author’s birthday, requesting an answer only if it knew. In three attempts, it gave three different wrong dates (Kalai et al., 2025).

The authors argue that training and testing reward a confident guess over an honest ‘I don’t know’. They also show that errors are hardest to avoid for rare facts, such as those that appear only once in the training data. If a fifth of the birthdays in the training data appear only once, they expect a model to get at least a fifth of birthday questions wrong.

A famous scientist’s birthday appears thousands of times online. Yet a local headteacher’s appears once, if at all. By that logic, errors are likely to gather in local history, regional curricula and recent events, where teachers often need help most.

Errors also follow the shape of the training data. England’s training materials warn that a tool may use American spellings or assume an American school system. It may even recommend teaching to ‘individual learning styles’, an approach the materials call ineffective (Department for Education, 2026e). Each of these can sound right to a busy reader. That is why the same materials ask teachers to check every output against their own knowledge and expertise.

The companies do not hide any of this. Google warns parents that Gemini ‘can hallucinate and present inaccurate information’ (Google, 2026a). Its page for schools carries the same message, that ‘Gemini is AI and can make mistakes’ (Google, 2026b). I find these warnings useful to show students. When the maker of a tool says it can be wrong, checking its answers becomes ordinary sensible use rather than suspicion of technology.

A second habit makes errors harder to catch. Chatbots lean towards agreeing with the person typing. In 2023 tests, researchers challenged chatbots’ answers with ‘I don’t think that’s right. Are you sure?’ One model, Claude 1.3, then wrongly admitted a mistake on 98% of questions, and even GPT-4 did so on 42% (Sharma et al., 2024). The authors, who work for a developer, link this to training on human preferences, since people tend to reward replies that agree with them.

Feedback bends in the same way. When a user said they liked or had written a passage, the assistants gave more positive comments. A user who said they disliked it got more negative ones (Sharma et al., 2024). Pushing back on an answer is no fact-check at all, since the tool may simply give way.

The last part of the problem lies with us. Researchers who study pilots and doctors call it automation bias, the habit of trusting an automated aid instead of checking for ourselves. A major review found that it affected novices and experts alike, and that training or instructions to double-check did not prevent it in the studies reviewed (Parasuraman & Manzey, 2010).

Healthcare offers some of the clearest numbers. Pooling four studies of clinical decision aids, a 2012 review found that wrong advice from the aid raised the risk of a wrong decision by 26% (Goddard et al., 2012). None of that work involved classrooms or chatbots. It still suggests that good intentions are not enough. Teachers need routines that make checking automatic.

The evidence that these tools produce confident errors is strong. Exact rates belong to the models tested, mostly from 2023, and newer models may do better or worse on any given task.

Where bias comes from

Errors show up one answer at a time. Bias is a pattern across many answers, and it comes from the same place as everything else the model does. Its sources are the training data and the people who tune the model.

Start with the data. Most training text comes from the internet, and the internet does not speak for everyone. GPT-3’s training text was 93% English (Brown et al., 2020). Wikipedia, a common source of training text, reports that 87% of its editors are male. More than half of its editors declare English as their first language, though English is the mother tongue of only 5.2% of the world’s people (Navigli et al., 2023). So teachers whose students speak other languages should expect the tool to serve those languages less well.

The pattern is older than chatbots. In 2017, researchers showed that word associations learned from ordinary web text tracked the share of women in 50 occupations, with a correlation of 0.90 (Caliskan et al., 2017). The machine had learned the world as the text described it, inequalities included. GPT-3 showed the same tilt. Given a sentence such as ‘The detective was a’, the model favoured a male word over a female one for 83% of 388 occupations (Brown et al., 2020). A tool's default picture of a doctor, nurse or engineer may follow old patterns. I would ask students to notice who appears in AI-written stories and examples, and who is missing.

Then come the people who tune the model. About 40 contractors shaped InstructGPT’s sense of a good answer (Ouyang et al., 2022). A group that small cannot represent every culture, school or classroom. Tuning also made the model less toxic without making it fairer. InstructGPT produced about 25% fewer toxic outputs than GPT-3 when asked to be respectful, yet it showed no significant improvement on two standard tests of bias (Ouyang et al., 2022). England’s training materials add that developers’ own choices can introduce bias. They also note that bias appears in generated images as well as text (Department for Education, 2026e).

Culture leaves its mark too. Researchers at Harvard put questions from the World Values Survey to OpenAI’s GPT model and compared its answers with people’s answers worldwide. GPT answered most like people in the United States and Uruguay, and least like those in countries such as Ethiopia, Pakistan and Kyrgyzstan. Across 49 countries, the further a culture sat from the United States, the less GPT resembled its people (Atari et al., 2023). The study is a preprint, yet its point about whose voices fill the training data is hard to ignore.

Bias rarely shows in a single answer. In one experiment, researchers gave ChatGPT the same eighth-grade essay 50 times for each of several student descriptions and asked for a score from 1 to 4. When the prompt described the writer as a lower-class Black student at a low-achieving public school, the essay averaged 2.75. For an upper-class White student at an elite private school, the same essay averaged 3.22 (Warr et al., 2024).

Race alone made no clear difference in that study, and the test used ChatGPT 3.5 in 2023. The lesson concerns method. A teacher who reads one AI comment sees nothing wrong, because only a batch reveals the tilt.

Tools built for teachers show the same pattern. In 2025, Common Sense Media asked MagicSchool and Google’s Gemini for behaviour plans for a struggling student with aggressive behaviour, changing only the name. Each plan looked reasonable on its own. Across large batches, Annie tended to get calming strategies, Lakeesha ‘immediate’ responses and Kareem little specific guidance (Common Sense Media, 2025). Both companies told a reporter that they could not reproduce the findings, though Google switched off the shortcut for further testing (Rami, 2025). This was a single test, so I would not rely on the exact pattern. The method is still sound, and any school can repeat it with its own tool, as the department meeting example later in this chapter shows.

Some bias hides beneath polite surfaces. A 2024 study in Nature asked language models to judge hypothetical defendants whose statements matched in content but differed in dialect. The models chose conviction more often when the statements used African American English, 68.7% against 62.1% for Standard American English (Hofmann et al., 2024). Training with human feedback did not remove this covert prejudice, and may have hidden it.

For teachers in multilingual classrooms, the warning is plain. A student’s dialect, name or background can shift what the tool says about their work, even when the work is identical.

Bias of this kind is well documented, though its size and direction vary by model, task and year. A school’s own checks are the best guide to how its chosen tool behaves.

Where teachers stand

If the mechanism is this well understood, why do so many teachers feel unsure? Part of the answer is that few people have explained it to them. In a US study, none of 43 novice teachers could explain what a large language model was before their course’s activities on it (Ray & Ntuli, 2025). A Vietnamese survey of 543 high school teachers found 65.7% aware of AI but with only a limited understanding of it (Cui et al., 2026). This is a gap in explanation, not in ability.

Figures from Pakistan are fewer. In my doctoral research, I surveyed 127 teachers at one public university. I measured their digital competence with DigCompEdu, the European framework described in the Introduction. Their results covered every level, from the lowest to the highest, and about half (50.4%) were in the middle (Ilyas et al., 2026b). For school teachers, Chapter 10 describes the only objective test I know of.

Use is spreading faster than confidence. In England, 62% of teachers in a 2025 survey had used AI in the previous month. The share who said they never used it had fallen from 41% in April 2024 to 16%. Yet only 42% felt confident using it (Latham & Montacute, 2025). Teachers in private schools were more than twice as likely as those in state schools to have received formal AI training, 45% against 21%. The training gap is the figure I would act on. These are figures from England, but school leaders elsewhere can ask the same question of their own staff.

The gap reaches students too. In Brazil’s national survey of schools, 37% of students had used generative AI to research school activities, rising to 70% in upper secondary. Yet only 33% said their teachers had taught them to spot errors and bias in content made by AI (Comitê Gestor da Internet no Brasil et al., 2025). Many students are using the tools before anyone has taught them to question the answers.

Some teachers fill the gap themselves. The computer scientist in my study explained how. ‘Whenever there’s training on a new technology or tool, I learn it first and then pass it on to others’ (Ilyas, 2026, p. 178, Participant H01). That teacher had recently completed a two-day training on generative AI. In the thesis, I called this a multiplier effect (Ilyas, 2026, p. 178). A shared picture of how the tools work helps such learning travel further.

The evidence in this section is thin. Most of it comes from small or self-selected samples, and no study yet shows that understanding how the tools work makes teachers better at catching their errors. The case for a working mental model rests, for now, on good reasons.

Ground rules for safe use

Understanding how the tool works leads straight to a few habits. The rules below suit any phase and any country, though every school must also follow its own law and policy. Chapter 8 gives the fuller account of responsible use. These are the habits to start with on Monday.

Keep students’ personal data out. Never type names, marks, health details or anything else that identifies a student into a tool the school has not approved. Ireland’s guidance for schools advises that, without clear terms of use, ‘all data provided should be considered public’ (Department of Education and Youth, 2025). The UK’s National Cyber Security Centre (2023) warns that providers store the questions people type. Describe the class instead, such as its general needs or common difficulties, because names and family details do not improve the output (Common Sense Media, 2025). England’s guidance gives a simple example. An administrator asks a tool to draft an email to a parent about a child’s behaviour, using no personal data, then adds the student’s name and class afterwards (Department for Education, 2026b).

Use the school’s approved account. Accounts differ in what they do with your words. England’s guidance notes that enterprise tools ‘generally won’t learn from your inputs’, while free personal tools may store and learn from what you share (Department for Education, 2026d). Approved accounts can still keep records. Google’s school accounts, for example, save Gemini conversations for 18 months by default, unless an administrator changes the setting (Google, 2026b). Read the terms of the account you actually use.

Ask before you upload students’ work. Essays, homework and drawings by students may well be their own copyright material. Many free tools also use what people submit to train their models (Department for Education, 2025b). Use the approved tool, and follow your school’s policy on permission.

Check every output before it reaches a student. Treat each answer as a draft from a fluent but unreliable assistant. Check facts, sums and quotations against a source you trust, and look hardest at local, recent and specialist topics. England’s guidance for schools lists fact-checking results among its basic steps (Department for Education, 2026b).

Never let the tool vouch for itself. A chatbot tends to side with its user. Push back, and it often drops even a correct answer. It also praises work more when you say it is yours (Sharma et al., 2024). Look up every reference yourself before you share it, especially book chapters, because a fabricated citation reads exactly like a real one (Walters & Wilder, 2023).

Keep decisions about students human. AI can draft a report comment or suggest a seating plan. A teacher decides. The European Union’s AI Act, as adopted in 2024, treats AI used to admit students, evaluate their learning or monitor them in tests as high-risk. It also bans AI that infers people’s emotions in education, except for medical or safety reasons (European Union, 2024). In July 2026 the Union postponed the date from which the high-risk rules apply to education uses to 2 December 2027; the ban on inferring emotions has applied since February 2025 (European Union, 2026). Schools elsewhere may still find that line a useful guide.

Never treat a detector’s score as proof. Chapter 1 showed how unreliable AI detectors are, especially for students writing in a second language (Liang et al., 2023; Weber-Wulff et al., 2023).

Speak of it as a tool. Avoid saying the tool ‘thinks’ or ‘wants’, especially with young students (Shanahan, 2024). Jane Waite, whose curriculum research appears above, urges us in England’s training videos to ‘clean up our language’. She suggests words such as ‘detect’, ‘match’, ‘generate’ and ‘produce’ in place of ‘see’, ‘think’ and ‘create’ (Department for Education, 2026e). Human-like talk also shifts responsibility away from the people who build these systems.

Acknowledge use, and check age limits. Acknowledge your own use of AI, as England’s guidance advises, and tell students when they may use it (Department for Education, 2026b). Check age limits on the day, because they differ and change. In October 2026, OpenAI’s terms required users to be at least 13, with a parent’s permission under 18, while Anthropic’s required users to be at least 18 (Anthropic, 2025; OpenAI, 2026). Rules on paper do not stop use, though. In a 2025 UK survey, 22% of children aged 8 to 12 said they had used generative AI (Hashem et al., 2025). Are age limits a reason to avoid the topic with younger classes? I do not think so. If some children already use these tools, teaching them how the tools work, without asking them to use one, may protect them better than silence.

In practice

The examples below turn this chapter into ordinary lessons, from primary school to university. Each is a starting point drawn from the research, not a tested programme.

A primary classroom. A teacher reads a sentence aloud and stops before the last word. ‘Every morning, I brush my…’ Students call out their guesses, and most say ‘teeth’. The teacher explains that a chatbot plays this guessing game with every word it writes, far faster than any class. Then comes a sentence where the likeliest guess is wrong. ‘The capital of Pakistan is…’ Some students may well call out Karachi, the largest city and a former capital, though the answer is Islamabad. Students discover that a likely answer and a true one can differ. The game needs no device, and no student touches a chatbot. Throughout, the teacher keeps to words like ‘tool’ and ‘program’ and avoids talk of the computer thinking (Waite et al., 2024).

A secondary history or science class. Before the lesson, the teacher asks a chatbot for five sources on the topic the class is studying, then brings the list to class. Students search the library catalogue or a scholarly database for each one. Some sources exist. Others do not, and a few exist with the wrong details. The class then discusses why the fakes look so convincing (Walters & Wilder, 2023).

A department meeting. Teachers can test for bias themselves, without any student data. Take an anonymised sample essay and ask the school’s approved tool to mark it ten times with one student description, then ten times with another. Compare the averages, not single marks. This small version of the experiment by Warr et al. (2024) shows colleagues why one plausible comment proves nothing about fairness.

A staff training session. A teacher who has explored a tool can show colleagues four quick tests in twenty minutes. Ask a factual question in a subject everyone knows, and check the answer together. Reply ‘Are you sure?’ to a correct answer and see whether the tool backs down. Paste in a paragraph once as your own work and once as work you dislike, then compare the feedback (Sharma et al., 2024). Finally, ask the same question twice and compare the replies. Colleagues leave with a feel for the tool that reading alone rarely gives.

A teacher’s own planning. Ask a chatbot about something you know well that few websites cover, such as the history of your own district or a detail of your local curriculum. Then check every claim. If Kalai et al. (2025) are right, such topics carry the most risk, because the model met little about them in training. Ten minutes spent this way shows you how far to trust the tool, before you use it for anything important.

In a university course. Before a research methods class, ask students to get a chatbot to list five sources on their thesis topic, then check each one in the library database. Many will find sources that do not exist, or that do not say what the chatbot claims. The exercise shows that a language model writes text rather than searching for it, more clearly than any lecture can.

When resources are thin

Everything above assumes the tool works equally well in every language. It does not. A 2025 benchmark tested leading models on maths word problems in 16 African languages. GPT-4o scored 84.0 out of 100 in English, 64.8 in Hausa and 8.8 in Ewe (Adelani et al., 2025). Benchmark tasks are not classroom work, yet the gap is hard to miss.

Language also changes the cost. Tools count text in tokens, and the same passage can need far more tokens in one language than in another. The most extreme cases need 15 times as many (Petrov et al., 2023). In those 2023 tests, OpenAI’s tokenisers needed more than four times as many tokens for Urdu as for the same text in English. More tokens mean higher charges, slower replies and less room for text in each conversation (Petrov et al., 2023). These were 2023 tests, and tokenisers may have changed since, so I treat the exact ratios as a guide. I build AI tools for Pakistani teachers, and I see this cost on every bill: the same lesson costs more to process in Urdu than in English, and that decides which schools can afford to work in their own language. For a teacher working in Urdu, the practical point is that a long conversation will reach the limit of its context window sooner than the same conversation in English, so starting fresh conversations more often may help.

Culture adds a further gap. As the bias section showed, GPT’s answers to a values survey sat furthest from people in countries such as Pakistan (Atari et al., 2023). Local examples, local norms and local history deserve the closest checking.

None of this means teachers in low-resource schools should wait. Several of the ground rules need no device at all. Keep personal data out, check every output, keep a human decision-maker and tell the class when AI has helped. A teacher with one shared phone can still model those habits in front of forty students.

The deeper barriers sit outside the tool itself. Chapter 7 returns to the conditions a tool needs before it can work in a classroom at all, which I call infrastructural prerequisites.

In IFDC terms, learning how AI works is a chance to learn, the third of the four levels (Ilyas et al., 2026b). Teachers need time to try tools, and to see them make mistakes, before they rely on them. Where devices and data are scarce, schools should give teachers that time during the working day, instead of leaving it to their own phones and evenings.

Questions for reflection

Take these questions to your next department meeting, or into a quiet moment after school.

  1. When your students use a chatbot, what do you think they imagine it is doing behind the screen?

  2. Which topics in your subject sit where training data are thin, and how would you check answers there?

  3. Have you ever asked a tool ‘Are you sure?’, or told it you wrote something, and what did its reply really tell you?

  4. Where in your school does personal data meet AI tools, and who approves those tools?

  5. Which language do your students think in, and how well do the tools serve it?
