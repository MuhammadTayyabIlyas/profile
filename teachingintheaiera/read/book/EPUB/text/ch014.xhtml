Chapter 12. Real Understanding in the AI Era

In my doctoral research with university teachers in Pakistan, a computer scientist described a habit that takes almost no equipment. Chapter 5 quoted the same teacher on homework. Here I want to ask what the habit certifies. For online quizzes, the computer scientist draws on a question bank that gives each student different values, ‘forcing each student to solve a unique problem’ (Ilyas, 2026, p. 186, Participant H01). Quick assessments go out as a link with two or three immediate questions (p. 187). After that comes the step that matters most here. ‘Then sometimes we ask them questions from their own submissions to ensure they actually understand what they wrote’ (Ilyas, 2026, p. 188, Participant H01).

Nothing in that account is new technology. A teacher reads a student’s work, points to a line and asks what it means. My thesis reads the practice as a check on authorship and comprehension at once (Ilyas, 2026, p. 188). I would put it a little more cautiously. A question can show whether a student understands a piece of work, but it cannot prove how the work was produced. That narrower claim is still a large one.

The computer scientist belongs to the group of teachers with high digital competence in my thesis (Ilyas, 2026, pp. 153, 171). Across the interviews, the thesis found a sharp divide in how teachers responded to AI in assessment. Teachers with lower competence saw assessment as a weak point, while those with higher competence saw it as a design problem to solve (p. 216). That teacher’s questions are on the design side, and they cost little more than time.

School students meet a version of this question before they arrive. Chapter 5 met students in Michigan’s Khanmigo pilots who ‘didn’t know what they didn’t know’ (Michigan Virtual, 2025). Students like these will soon apply to university. In the UK, the admissions service UCAS already warns applicants that any information in a personal statement ‘may need to be discussed or evidenced’ (UCAS, n.d.). Before a student reaches a lecture hall, the system expects a voice that can answer for its own words.

That expectation is the subject of this chapter. When machines produce fluent answers, what does it mean to know? For school teachers the question arrives as homework that reads well beside an explanation that does not. University teachers meet it further on, in what a degree can still certify.

I answer in three steps. First, a well-written piece of work does not, on its own, show what its author understands. Second, knowing still depends on trusting sources we cannot check ourselves, so the task is to trust the right sources. Third, assessment can still certify what students know, if a programme decides which claims a person must explain and defend in person, and pays for the time this takes.

Chapter 2 explained how language models produce text, and Chapter 5 introduced the theme of knowing. Then Chapter 6 asked what school assessments can still tell us. Most recently, Chapter 11 handed universities a question about what a degree can still certify. This chapter takes up that question, speaking mainly to university teachers. School teachers will also find it useful, because their students carry these habits into the lecture hall.

Fluent is not the same as known

The heading deliberately echoes Chapter 2’s ‘Fluent is not the same as true’. That chapter asked whether a fluent answer is correct. This one asks what a fluent answer shows about the person who hands it in. Chapter 5 set out the evidence that effort and the feeling of learning part company. Here I ask what that evidence means for a degree, so I recall it only briefly.

In a Harvard physics study, students in active-learning classes scored 0.46 standard deviations higher on a test yet reported a feeling of learning 0.56 lower (Deslauriers et al., 2019, p. 19253). People also overrate their grasp of everyday devices until they try to explain them (Rozenblit & Keil, 2002). In a trial with 117 university students, Fan and colleagues found that revising with ChatGPT improved essays most (Fan et al., 2025). Knowledge gain and transfer did not differ significantly between the four groups. That does not prove the students learned nothing, but it shows how easily a product and its author’s learning can come apart.

Confidence behaves in a similar way. In an online study, 246 US adults recruited through Prolific used ChatGPT on 20 logical reasoning problems from the Law School Admission Test (Fernandes et al., 2026, pp. 1, 5). Their scores rose by about three points against a norm group, yet they overestimated their own scores by about four. Participants with higher AI literacy judged their own performance less accurately, and a second study with 452 people replicated the pattern (p. 1). Chapter 5 gave the exact scores. Among 21 novice programmers, those who struggled with AI tools finished with what Prather and colleagues call ‘an illusion of competence’ (Prather et al., 2024, p. 469).

A smaller preprint brings the problem onto campus. University students, 102 in all, predicted how well they could explain four topics (Aslanov et al., 2025, p. 1). One group received explanations through a custom chatbot, another read the identical text without it, and a third had nothing. The chatbot’s answers were fixed in advance, so the study tests the frame of a conversation more than open use. Its users showed the largest gap between predicted and later self-rated understanding. Coders also judged that group’s explanations less accurate than those of the group who read the same text plainly. It is a preprint, which means other researchers have not yet checked it. Even so, when a student says a chatbot made a topic clear, the useful next step is to ask the student to explain it.

A leadership paper from the American Council on Education puts the teaching risk in one line. ‘Without underlying competence, students may produce fluent work without durable understanding’ (Okahana et al., 2026, p. 12).

Read together, these studies point the same way. In each of them, AI raised fluency or confidence more readily than measured knowing. Each study is small or short, and none follows students for long.

I saw a smaller version of this long before chatbots. When I taught in an elementary school, a student could recite a memorised answer without a single slip. If I changed one number in the question, the same student often stopped. The recited answer had been fluent, and the knowing behind it had been thin.

Knowing has more than one account

Philosophers have argued for centuries about what knowledge is, and this chapter will not settle that argument. A teacher still needs a working sense of what to look for. One familiar account treats knowing as holding a true belief for good reasons. Another stresses understanding, the ability to explain an idea, connect it to others and use it in a new case. Both ask something of the person, beyond possessing a correct sentence.

Chapter 2 left one question open and promised that it would return here. Do language models understand anything? I keep that question open on purpose. Each account in this section asks about a human knower, the student whose name sits on the work. None of them needs a verdict on the machine. Saying that a model predicts the next word does not settle the debate, and I do not use it to settle anything here. Whatever the machine has, the teacher still has to ask what the student knows.

A third account starts from relationships. Margaret Bearman and Rola Ajjawi describe a relational view in which ‘knowing is held between actors’, people, things and spaces together (Bearman & Ajjawi, 2023, p. 1162). They admit the tension this creates. Assessment often assumes that learning is an individual, context-free activity, while professional work binds people and tools together (p. 1162). A relational view does not treat work done with AI as empty by definition. It asks what the student brings to the arrangement.

A fourth account begins from dependence. Every one of us relies on other people and tools for most of what we know. Chapter 5 called one part of this cognitive offloading, using the world around us to reduce mental work (Risko & Gilbert, 2016). Offloading is not a failure in itself.

Bodong Chen draws a useful line. In a preprint, he uses the term epistemic substitution for a tool that does the very thinking that builds expertise (Chen, 2025, p. 2). A calculator can free a physicist to do physics. But a tool that does the reasoning a course was meant to teach takes away what the course promised. Chen suggests three questions about any tool (p. 1). Does it leave room for the student to use real skill? Does it help the student notice when an answer needs checking? And what habits does it build? By noticing, he means the ability ‘to recognize situations that warrant epistemic attention’ (p. 7). These are useful questions for any assignment brief.

I do not need to choose among these accounts to make this chapter’s practical claim. On each of them, a fluent product alone does not establish what its author understands. The belief account asks whether the person holds reasons. Understanding asks whether the person can explain and use the idea. The relational view asks what the person contributes, and the dependence view asks whether the right operations stayed with the learner. Each points a teacher towards the person behind the page.

A simple illustration shows how this works in a lecture room. Imagine a second-year engineering student who submits a correct calculation of the load on a beam. On the belief account, the teacher asks why the student trusts each step. Understanding asks the student to predict what changes if the beam is twice as long. The relational view asks which parts came from a tool, a textbook or a classmate, and what the student added. Finally, the dependence view asks whether the student still could have done the reasoning the module was built to teach. Four accounts lead to four questions, and all four can be asked in five minutes.

Trusting a source we cannot see inside

Most of what any graduate knows came from someone else. A history student who has never opened an archive still knows dates from a textbook and a lecture. Direct discovery cannot be the standard for every claim a student makes. The harder question is which kinds of dependence support knowledge.

Two philosophers, Jinhua He and Chen Yang, ask this question about language models. Their opening claim is bold. ‘Artificial testimony generated by large language models (LLMs) can be a source of knowledge’ (He & Yang, 2025, p. 6201). ‘Testimony’ here means learning something because someone tells you. Under the usual theories of testimony, they argue, a model does not count as a witness. One theory, for example, says that a witness gives a personal guarantee to the listener, and a model cannot do that (pp. 6206–6208). So they turn instead to the eighteenth-century philosopher Thomas Reid.

On their account, a belief based on what a model says is reasonable at first sight if the model consistently shows two ‘artificial propensities’, or habits (He & Yang, 2025, p. 6210). The first is truthfulness. The second is cautiousness, ‘a tendency to only mark truth as factual and flag non-truth as questionable’ (p. 6211). Cautiousness, they judge, is the harder of the two to achieve. They also warn that the more information these systems give, the less reliable they may become as sources of knowledge (p. 6212). A long, confident, detailed answer is not safer for being long.

This is a philosophical argument. He and Yang set conditions, and they do not show that any present system meets them. For a teacher, though, their conditions suggest questions a student can ask of any source. Does this source usually answer correctly in this subject? Will it say when the evidence is thin? Can I find an independent reason to accept this particular answer? These questions are my own translation of their conditions. They ask nothing of the machine’s inner life.

Students do not always ask them. In a small study at a Pakistani public university, three undergraduates described using ChatGPT in an English-medium speaking course (Siddiqui, 2025, pp. 123–124). The paper gives their words in English, translated from Urdu (p. 124). Asked how they knew the tool’s work was accurate, one student replied, ‘Because AI is a computer, it never makes such mistakes, neither grammatical mistakes nor anything’ (p. 125). Another valued that ‘it gives us an answer like a human’ (p. 126). Chapter 9 described similar trust among students at one Lahore school. This one focus group reports beliefs without testing accuracy, yet it still shows authority granted for fluency alone.

Teachers feel the same pull from the other side. The botanist in my study spoke of an authority crisis, as Chapter 1 describes (Ilyas, 2026, p. 167, Participant L5). My thesis found that lower-competence teachers tended towards surveillance and prohibition (p. 209). More confident colleagues became facilitators of critical thinking (p. 212).

What should students learn instead? Bearman and Ajjawi argue that students working with opaque systems ‘must learn when to trust and when to doubt and when doubt turns to mistrust’ (2023, p. 1164). They describe epistemic doubt as a state of uncertainty and discomfort that a student can hold while checking (p. 1169). The Russell Group’s principles put the responsibility in plain words. Accountability ‘for the accuracy of information generated’, once it moves into another context, ‘lies with the user’ (Russell Group, 2023, p. 2).

Trust has a second side, because it decides whose knowledge counts. In a preprint, three researchers, two of them linked to Google DeepMind, apply Miranda Fricker’s idea of epistemic injustice to generative AI (Kay et al., 2024). Epistemic injustice means treating some people’s knowledge as less believable because of who they are. The researchers note that language models are strongly centred on English, and that their quality varies across languages (Kay et al., 2024, p. 7). A source can sound authoritative while mainly reflecting the languages and institutions that dominate its training. For a student working in a second language, checking an answer and asking whether it is fair become related questions, though not the same one.

School teachers can start this work early. Chapter 9 sets out what students should learn about AI, including a check that keeps a clear-sounding answer apart from a well-supported one. I will not repeat it. For school leavers, one habit is worth building. Before accepting a fluent answer, ask how the source could know, and what would show it wrong. A student who arrives at university with that habit has already begun the work this chapter describes.

Expertise and the work of judging

If students will work with machine answers for the rest of their careers, someone has to judge those answers. In a conceptual paper, Bearman and colleagues call this capacity evaluative judgement. They write that educators and learners share a responsibility ‘to ensure that humans do not relinquish their roles as arbiters of quality’ (Bearman et al., 2024, p. 893). Their conclusion names the problem sharply. ‘AI has widened the gap between our capability to produce work, and our capability to evaluate the quality of that work’ (p. 903).

Their remedy is familiar to any teacher. Self-assessment, peer review, feedback, rubrics and exemplars can all build judgement, adapted for AI (Bearman et al., 2024, pp. 897, 899–901). Students can compare generated work with their own and check it against credible sources (p. 897). The authors also want students to judge the process behind an answer, so that no one stumbles on the right result through wrong assumptions or methods (p. 898). This is a conceptual paper, so it argues for these methods rather than testing them with AI. One simple change is to add an AI-generated answer to the examples students judge, and ask them to find a weak step in it.

Expertise may also erode quietly. Chapter 10 met Brooke Macnamara and colleagues’ warning about skill decay among teachers (Macnamara et al., 2024). Their argument reaches learners too. Because AI makes a task feel easier, learners may be less able to judge the true state of their skills (p. 5). The argument draws on autopilots in aviation and on medical AI, with no classroom trial behind it. I treat deskilling among students as a serious possibility that this chapter’s sources do not measure.

The computer scientist who checks submissions also puts foundations first, as Chapter 5 noted. He advises students to learn foundational concepts in class first, so that they ‘can better judge whether a website or video is credible’ (Ilyas, 2026, p. 185, Participant H01). That sequence captures the link between expertise and judgement. A student cannot doubt a fluent answer well without knowing enough to notice what is wrong with it.

Chapter 3 argued that relationships, judgement, care and example sit at the heart of teaching, and I leave that argument where it stands. This chapter asks something narrower. What must a graduate’s expertise still allow that person to explain, decide and defend without the machine?

Authorship, originality and ownership

University rules mostly frame the problem as originality. In a review of policy texts, Jiahui Luo examined the top 20 universities in the 2024 QS rankings (Luo, 2024, p. 655). Ten were in North America, five in Europe, three in Australia and two in Asia. That is a sample far from most Pakistani campuses. Of the 20, 19 had public policies on AI in assessment (p. 656). Across them, ‘the primary problem represented is that students may not submit original work for assessment’ (p. 651). AI is often cast as outside help, separate from the student’s own effort. Luo argues that ‘knowledge production in the digital age is an increasingly distributed and collaborative process’ and proposes judging originality by discipline and learning goal (p. 661). Rules about originality do not, on their own, ask whether a student understands the work. I think asking what counts as the student’s own contribution in each subject is a more useful question than whether AI was used at all.

A team at the University of Technology Sydney offers another frame. Mohsen Ebrahimzadeh, Antonette Shibani and Simon Buckingham Shum propose coauthorship integrity as a new source of validity evidence (Ebrahimzadeh et al., 2026). ‘Coauthorship Integrity is violated when students submit AI-generated content that they do not understand’ (p. 1). Their prototype, an AI Viva, builds comprehension questions of controllable type and difficulty from the student’s own submitted text (pp. 1, 5). So far it has had an early design check, with feedback from eight expert educators and no reported trial with students (p. 9).

Professional bodies draw the line in practical terms. The Engineering Council discourages using generative AI ‘to produce entire drafts or solutions from scratch’ (Engineering Council, 2025, p. 1). When AI writes a first draft, the guidance says, it ‘assumes the role of the initial author’. Any use ‘must include an element of human checking’ (p. 1). In medicine, interim guidance on intensive care training requires reflective reports to represent the doctor’s own clinical reasoning, judgement, experience and insight (Faculty of Intensive Care Medicine, 2025).

Three judgements sit side by side here, and they are easily confused. Originality asks where a product came from. Disclosure tells us what help was used. Ownership asks what the learner understands well enough to defend. A student can disclose honestly and still own little. Another can use AI heavily and own the argument completely. Coauthorship integrity sits usefully beside validity, which the next section takes up.

Validity, beyond catching cheats

Assessment theory already had words for this problem. Chapter 6 introduced Michael Kane’s view that what we check is not the score itself, but how we interpret and use it (Kane, 2013, p. 1). A mark is evidence for a claim about a learner. Kane adds that bigger claims need more evidence than smaller ones (p. 1). A degree makes some of the biggest claims in education, about a person’s ability across years and situations.

Chapter 6 also drew on Phillip Dawson and colleagues, for whom ‘validity is a claim not an absolute’ (Dawson et al., 2024, p. 1012). Their lead author declares funding from educational technology companies, including Turnitin and Inspera (p. 1013). For them, cheating matters because of what a grade certifies. It ‘makes it difficult to certify what a particular student has achieved’ (p. 1006). Honest work can break the same link, if the task never required the understanding that the grade reports.

Close reading did not rescue the link in two studies. Chapter 6 discussed the first, in which German pre-service teachers and experienced English-speaking teachers recruited online struggled to spot AI-written essays and were overconfident (Fleckenstein et al., 2024, p. 1). Its authors suggest oral examinations to check students’ understanding of their own written work (p. 8). The university case adds a second study. In one UK psychology degree, researchers slipped AI-written answers into real take-home examinations (Scarfe et al., 2024). Markers missed 94% of them, and the AI answers scored on average about half a grade boundary higher than real students. Only 16% of student submissions scored above the median AI grade on the same module. These are two studies, so I would not treat the exact figures as true everywhere. The direction is still clear. Careful reading of a written answer is not a reliable way to tell who wrote it or whether its author understood it. A teacher who depends on spotting AI writing is depending on a check that this evidence does not support.

The university question is sharper, because a degree certifies a person to employers and professions. Before choosing a method, a programme team can ask one question of each major assessment. What evidence would justify saying that this student understands this claim? Product, process, conversation and supervised performance each contribute something different. None settles the matter by carrying the label authentic.

Drafts raise a question of their own. Chapter 5 quoted the historian in my study, who asks students to show a draft before final submission (Ilyas, 2026, p. 188, Participant H02). Watching work develop is valuable teaching. A draft trail can be generated too, so it supports an inference only when someone talks with the student about the choices in it.

What a degree can still certify

Regulators now describe the problem in terms of certification. England’s regulator lists ‘the meaning and credibility of assessment and qualifications’ among the risks AI brings (Fleming, 2025). It asks providers how they will build the AI literacy employers want ‘without displacing opportunities to develop deep skills and acquire knowledge critical to a student’s discipline’. The worry is no longer only about individual misconduct.

Pakistan’s Higher Education Commission has gone further than most national bodies. Its framework on generative AI was notified for immediate effect on 4 September 2026 (Higher Education Commission Pakistan, 2026b). It aims to ensure that assessment ‘accurately reflects student learning and understanding’ (Higher Education Commission Pakistan, 2026a, p. 4). AI literacy, it says, is ‘essential for graduate competency’ (p. 4). As Chapter 6 noted, ‘Detection of AI use alone is insufficient to establish misconduct’ (p. 5). Chapter 8 argued that a student’s disclosure should not automatically trigger suspicion. The framework agrees for universities, since disclosure ‘should not be an adverse factor in evaluation’ (p. 5). In February 2026 the Commission also made a three-credit course on AI compulsory in every undergraduate and postgraduate programme, public and private, from the 2026 session (Dawn, 2026a).

Its definition of misuse is worth reading slowly. Misuse occurs when AI is used ‘to avoid learning in foundational contexts’, to misrepresent authorship or to break course rules (Higher Education Commission Pakistan, 2026a, p. 5). I read the first of those as a statement about knowing, more than about honesty. Institutions should review their major assessments for resilience to AI within 12 months of adopting the framework (pp. 9–10). The review should consider supervised work for foundational skills, oral examinations or presentations in key courses, authentic local tasks and portfolios. That same framework attaches a zero grade to unauthorised use (p. 12). An evidence rule and a sanction now sit in one document, and campuses will have to hold them together.

Australia’s regulator published a discussion paper that frames the task at programme level. Its September 2023 consultation draft calls for ‘security at meaningful points across a program’ (Lodge et al., 2023, p. 5). A programme would identify the moments where it must know what students can do without AI, and secure those. Jisc’s assessment flowchart asks a module team the same thing directly. Which intended learning outcomes must students demonstrate without AI assistance (Jisc National Centre for AI, 2024, p. 4)?

Policy texts do not prove practice. A study of 163 UK degree-awarding institutions found public AI policies at only 96 (Illingworth, 2026, p. 2). By keyword count, 83 of those 96 policies (86%) looked education-dominant (p. 3). A close reading of a sample changed the picture. The author concludes that ‘it is no longer right to claim that 86% of institutional AI policies are educational’ (p. 5). Many policies, the report argues, ‘promise critical thinking but deliver audit trails’ (p. 1). Before treating a policy as proof of good practice, I would read what it actually asks teachers and students to do. The same care applies to the HEC framework, whose effect on campuses is not yet known.

The sector is still writing its picture of the graduate. A QAA Collaborative Enhancement Project, led by the University of York, aims to redefine graduate attributes for the generative AI era (Quality Assurance Agency for Higher Education, 2025a). When its page was captured for this book, it listed ‘A universal Graduate Attributes framework’ as an intended output, not yet a finished one.

Professions are not waiting. The Engineering Council suggests testing an assessment against generative AI, to check that a student could not complete it without doing their own work (Engineering Council, 2025, p. 1). Doctors, the General Medical Council adds, remain responsible for decisions they take using AI and must work within their competence (General Medical Council, n.d.). These degrees lead to that responsibility, so in my view they must certify the competence behind it.

In the meantime, I would name three graduate outcomes that still need independent human demonstration. The first is explaining one’s own submitted work, line by line if asked. Next comes checking and owning an AI-assisted professional claim, as the engineering guidance already requires. Last is defending a claim under questioning, including saying where the evidence is thin.

Oral assessment on campus, and what it costs

The oldest answer to the knowing problem is a conversation. Two designs show what that takes on a modern campus, one small and one large.

The first comes from the University of Auckland, in a practitioner account the teachers wrote themselves. Shahper Richter and Patrick Dodd trialled interactive oral assessments in a postgraduate marketing course with 42 students (Richter & Dodd, 2025). Each student had a seven-minute conversation about coursework already submitted. The grading guide covered content and clear explanation. Where written grades had skewed high, the orals produced a more balanced spread across grade bands, though the account publishes no grade tables. One lecturer said, ‘The dialogue revealed what students actually understood, rather than what they could memorise or outsource.’ The authors built practice runs into tutorials, so nerves did not decide the result.

They are candid about cost. Examiners need training in asking probing yet fair questions, and scheduling at scale means coordinating rooms, examiners and recordings. For courses over 200 students, they run group sessions where each student answers individually, with several assessors working at once (Richter & Dodd, 2025).

An Irish pilot shows the preparation behind such designs. At Dublin City University, academics ran interactive orals with 322 students in seven modules across four faculties (Ward et al., 2024, p. 931). The pilot ran from September 2020 to May 2021, before ChatGPT was public (p. 930). An expert from Griffith University supported weekly meetings of a staff community of practice. Of the 140 students who answered a survey, 77% rated the orals good or very good at discouraging cheating (pp. 933–934). The survey table labels that item contract cheating (p. 935). That is a perception, not a measured fall in misconduct. Academics reported that the time needed to tool up was considerable (pp. 944–945). They also worried about fairness when students were assessed in pairs or groups, and rigorously designed rubrics helped them address it (p. 945). The pilot tells us more about the work of setting up orals than about AI. The preparation time and the need for careful rubrics are not caused by AI, so I expect them to apply today as well.

The second design works at scale. At the University of Melbourne, a data-processing subject enrols 400 to 500 undergraduates, who work in groups of three or four (University of Melbourne, n.d.). After each of two assignments, assessors have a week to mark the work and write questions for each student. Each student then has about five minutes of focused questioning. Two independent assessors run every session and average their marks. All assessors complete two hours of training before each round, and sessions are recorded. A marked gap between a student’s answers and the written report triggers a formal misconduct process.

The two educators behind it describe the build in a conference paper, a quality-assurance report that compares cohorts without controls (Samadi & Chee, 2024). In a small online course, one lead academic wrote individual questions for 27 students at about 4.4 minutes per question (p. 530). The large subject needed junior staff as examiners, who ‘had limited to no experience’ of oral assessment (p. 530). For the second round, students received a clearer rubric and a one-hour session with video examples (p. 531). Examiners attended a two-hour workshop, marked a sample presentation individually and then discussed their marks. The team also used a university-hosted AI tool to draft questions. Of the 16 examiners surveyed, 67% of those responding felt the AI-generated questions were too generic (p. 532). This report shows what the team did and what it cost, not whether the orals improved learning. The examiners’ view of the AI-drafted questions is also a reason to have a person write or edit the questions.

At Cornell, five oral exams replaced graded homework for about 75 engineering students, made possible by two graduate and five undergraduate teaching assistants (Schaffer & Adie, n.d.). Sydney examined 690 first-years in two weeks, while UWE Bristol’s five-minute code vivas suit 20 to 30 students (Teaching@Sydney, n.d.; Emberton, 2026). Each needed staff time that someone planned for.

What does the wider evidence say? A systematic review screened 2,657 records and kept 17 studies of oral assessment in higher education (Nallaya et al., 2024, p. 629). Validity, reliability and the capacity to reduce integrity breaches ‘were dependent on whether it has been designed, scaffolded, and implemented well’ (p. 629). All of the included studies predate ChatGPT, since they run from 2010 to 2021 (pp. 632, 634). This chapter’s sources include no trial linking an oral redesign to later unaided knowing, so the evidence for orals is emerging and conditional.

Orals raise fairness problems of their own. The London School of Economics warns that native and fluent English speakers ‘may be perceived as more knowledgeable than they are’ (London School of Economics, 2025, p. 4). Its guidance also names anxiety, accessibility, marker bias and time with large cohorts. An Australian study of orals and equity drew responses from only 172 of 1,136 students (Krautloher, 2024, p. 6). Its author admits that equity was not the project’s original focus (p. 13). In The Conversation, Nicole Brownlie reports a Norwegian secondary-school study where the same examiners asked some students fewer than ten questions and others nearly fifty (Brownlie, 2026). The evidence is limited, but it points to one risk. An oral can reward confident speakers of English instead of knowledge. For teachers in Pakistan this links to the language question later in this chapter, and it is one reason to agree the rubric and the prepared questions before the sessions begin.

In a conceptual paper for business schools, Graham Perkins draws the practical lesson. His key principle is that verbal assessment is ‘embedded within the design of the task rather than added post hoc’ (Perkins, 2026, p. 8). Scalability, he concedes, is ‘a significant challenge’ (p. 10). He suggests concentrating orals in capstones and major projects where individual understanding matters most (p. 10). Orals, he adds, are not a standalone solution (p. 13).

When the examiner is a machine

Some teachers now hand the conversation to software. At NYU Stern, Panos Ipeirotis and a colleague noticed that short written assignments read like polished consulting memos. In cold calls, many students could not explain basic choices in their own work (Ipeirotis, 2025). The blog puts it bluntly. ‘If you cannot defend your own work live, then the written artifact is not measuring what you think it is measuring.’

The team then ran oral exams through a voice agent built with the company ElevenLabs. It examined 36 students over nine days (Ipeirotis, 2025). Usage for all of them cost about US$15, on top of a US$99 monthly ElevenLabs subscription. In a survey, 70% of students agreed the format tested their actual understanding, which records how they perceived it. Only 13% preferred it to written exams, and 83% found it more stressful. Before discussion, two grading models disagreed sharply, averaging 17 and 13.4 out of 20.

I read this as a caution. An AI examiner can expose one outsourcing problem while creating another, because the questioner and the marker are now machines whose judgements need checking. The usage figure leaves out design, moderation, appeals and adjustments, which are the real costs of a fair oral. Before high-stakes use, a person still has to answer for bias, reliability and what the questions measure. The same caution applies to the AI Viva prototype described earlier, which its designers present as work in progress.

In practice

These routines turn the argument into steps a lecturer or programme team could take this term. They draw on the evidence above, but no study has tested them as a package. Each example is a hypothetical, and its timings are illustrations only.

Bring work you can explain. Imagine a sixth-form teacher whose class is drafting personal statements. The teacher could warn that anything they write may need to be discussed, then ask each student to explain one paragraph aloud to a partner. This stretches Chapter 5’s ‘Teach it back’ routine to the writing students carry to university. UCAS already gives applicants this warning (UCAS, n.d.).

Ask from the student’s own page. Chapter 5’s ‘Ask about their own work’ routine also suits a lecture course. Suppose a lecturer marking twenty reports picks three at random, as a check on the course, and asks each author two questions about a chosen paragraph. The questions come from the student’s text, as the computer scientist’s do (Ilyas, 2026, p. 188). Afterwards the lecturer would record only whether the student could explain the claim.

Name what must be shown without AI. A programme team could first list the outcomes that students must show with no AI help, using Jisc’s flowchart (Jisc National Centre for AI, 2024, p. 4). Then it would protect those key moments of assessment, so that the work there is clearly the student’s own, as TEQSA’s draft proposes (Lodge et al., 2023, p. 5). For the remaining tasks, I suggest allowing AI, as long as students say how they used it.

Keep the conversation short and planned. A five- or seven-minute oral on submitted work could use two or three prepared questions and one follow-up. That matches the lengths at Auckland and Melbourne (Richter & Dodd, 2025; University of Melbourne, n.d.). Students would get a practice round in tutorials first.

Calibrate before you judge. Before the real round, two staff could mark the same recorded sample and compare their judgements. Melbourne’s two-hour training and paired assessors show one version (Samadi & Chee, 2024, p. 531).

Separate language from knowing. When English fluency is not the outcome, the rubric could say so and the examiner could rephrase freely. LSE’s guidance asks staff to tell students that the assessment ‘will focus on demonstrating knowledge and learning, rather than confidence or language skills’ (London School of Economics, 2025, p. 7).

Budget the hours first. Before adding orals, a department head could count the staff hours, rooms and second markers needed, and agree their source. The American Council on Education report quoted earlier warns that redesign ‘should not become an unfunded expectation placed on individual instructors’ (Okahana et al., 2026, p. 18).

Teach trust and doubt. Students could compare their own draft with a generated one against the rubric, then justify which is better and why. Bearman and Ajjawi offer this as an illustrative task in a conceptual paper and report no evaluation of it (2023, pp. 1170–1171).

When resources are thin

Most designs above depend on staffing that many Pakistani departments do not have. My thesis reports an HEC survey, cited by the HEC chairman in February 2026, that found about 40% of faculty and administrative posts vacant across higher education institutions (Ilyas, 2026, p. 15). Remaining teachers absorb the extra teaching and administration. Few such departments could match Cornell’s seven assistants for 75 students.

Language adds a second layer. Most Pakistani universities teach mainly in English, especially in sciences and business, while public institutions also allow Urdu (Ilyas, 2026, p. 17). A document analysis and literature review on AI in Pakistan notes that many tools work best in English (Warsi & Siddiqui, 2026, p. 816). Their output may be weaker in Urdu, Sindhi, Pashto and other local languages. In an opinion piece for NORRAG, Saqib Raza argues that students from Urdu-medium, public or rural schooling face a ‘double burden’ (Raza, n.d.). They struggle with academic English and then meet tools shaped by English-dominant knowledge. This names a risk a teacher can watch for when deciding which tasks allow AI.

The same review observes that students ‘can produce polished text without demonstrating genuine understanding’ (Warsi & Siddiqui, 2026, p. 814). It names oral defence and process-based evaluation among the responses universities abroad are trying.

Teachers’ own readiness matters too. In my survey of 127 university teachers, assessment ranked second lowest of the competence areas, with 40% in the low band (Ilyas, 2026, pp. 146–147). Where confidence is low, the fallback is often surveillance. The botanist who spoke of an authority crisis monitors students’ phones and laptops in class, as Chapter 3 noted (Ilyas, 2026, p. 167, Participant L5). My thesis notes that surveillance prevents some behaviours without building discernment (pp. 167–168).

The Siddiqui study cannot guide thin settings. Its site was chosen for its advanced resources and its accessibility (Siddiqui, 2025, pp. 123–124). HEC now asks institutions to consider orals in key courses (Higher Education Commission Pakistan, 2026a, p. 10). A notified requirement is not evidence that campuses can run them.

Both terms from Chapter 4, teacher-design burden and infrastructural prerequisites, apply to oral assessment, even though an oral needs no software. None of the studies in this chapter uses them.

For a short oral, the infrastructural prerequisites are a quiet room, a slot in the timetable, a second assessor where possible and an agreed language of assessment. Recording adds devices and secure storage. The design burden comes first, in writing questions, agreeing a rubric, practising with students and planning appeals. Samadi and Chee’s 4.4 minutes per question for one small cohort gives a sense of scale. Simple arithmetic adds the rest. A class of 120 students at five minutes each is ten hours of talking, before any question writing, moderation or rest between sessions.

Under those limits, I would propose selective short orals as a pilot to test. A department picks one or two assessments a year where individual understanding matters most, such as a final project. Every student in that assessment gets a five-minute conversation about their own submission, with a practice run beforehand. Where English is not the outcome, students choose English or Urdu, as the teachers in my own interviews did (Ilyas, 2026, p. 129). The department counts the hours honestly and evaluates the pilot before extending it.

Some of this costs nothing beyond attention. The computer scientist’s habit of asking about a student’s own submission fits inside an ordinary tutorial or office hour. A teacher who monitors phones spends the same minutes watching, and learns less about what students know. Where there is no money for a new oral programme, a few well-chosen questions in class are still a better use of the time than surveillance.

One limit needs saying plainly. A sample of students who explain their work well checks the quality of a course. It does not certify each graduate, because the unsampled students have shown nothing. Selective orals must cover every student at the chosen points if a grade is to rest on them.

The conversation at the centre of this chapter always ends with a person. QAA’s framework for generative AI in assessment says that ‘human takes the final responsibility’ (Quality Assurance Agency for Higher Education, 2025b, p. 3). Chapter 13 asks what kind of teacher the AI era needs. Part of the answer lies in a simple test from this chapter. Who remains answerable for a claim, once a machine has helped to make it?

IFDC came from my research with university teachers, so it fits this chapter closely (Ilyas et al., 2026b). Universities with few resources can still help teachers feel safe enough to redesign their assessments. Clear policy and shared examples cost little.

Questions for reflection

Take these questions to your next programme meeting, or to a quiet hour after marking.

  1. In the course you teach now, which piece of assessed work would tell you most if a student had to talk you through it?

  2. When a student’s work is fluent, what tells you whether the student understands it?

  3. Which sources do your students trust without checking, and what would teach them when to doubt?

  4. What would a five-minute oral cost your department in rooms, staff and timetable hours?

  5. In which language can your students best show what they know, and does your assessment allow it?

Framework connections

My doctoral research found that assessment was the second weakest area of digital competence for Pakistani university teachers (Ilyas, 2026, pp. 146–147). DigCompEdu gives assessment its own area. It asks teachers to use different kinds of assessment and to read the evidence of what learners can do (Redecker, 2017, p. 21). This chapter suggests why that area now matters more. Underneath it lies validity, the question of whether the evidence supports what we claim about a learner (Kane, 2013, p. 1).

Several organisations now point the same way. UNESCO asks teachers to examine the risk of AI ‘usurping human accountability’ in decisions about students’ results (Miao & Cukurova, 2024, p. 36). The QAA sees AI literacy as a likely key skill for graduates (Quality Assurance Agency for Higher Education, 2023, p. 2). Jisc asks course teams which outcomes students must show without AI (Jisc National Centre for AI, 2024, p. 4). Pakistan’s HEC calls AI literacy essential for graduates and asks for assessment that reflects real understanding (Higher Education Commission Pakistan, 2026a, p. 4). But none of them has yet agreed what a degree should certify in the AI era.
