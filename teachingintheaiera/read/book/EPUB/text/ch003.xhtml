Chapter 1. The Day the Homework Changed

When I taught in an elementary school in Pakistan, the threat to homework had a familiar face. It was the guidebook, a slim key that shops near almost every school gate sold for a few rupees, with a ready answer for every exercise in the textbook. A student who owned one could finish a week of homework in an evening.

We learned to spot it quickly. The same sentence would appear in a dozen notebooks, word for word, carrying the same odd phrase from the same page. Now and then a child copied the answer to the wrong question. Catching the shortcut took a glance and a little patience.

Years later, I sat with university teachers in Pakistan for my doctoral research and heard that old worry in a new form. One teacher described students who ‘use ChatGPT or similar tools to submit work’. The teacher’s answer was to mark more stringently and check references for authenticity, so that students ‘learn they must actually do their own work’ (Ilyas, 2026, Participant H02). Another teacher captured the deeper problem in a single line. ‘ChatGPT can instantly provide answers’, they said, which ‘creates an authority crisis for teachers’ (Ilyas, 2026, Participant L5).

The temptation these teachers described was old. Students have always looked for shortcuts, and teachers have always looked for the thinking behind the work. What changed was the shortcut. It became free, private, instant and fluent, and it no longer repeated itself from notebook to notebook.

This chapter asks a simple question with a complicated answer. What has AI changed in classrooms, and what has stayed the same? My answer, drawing on the best evidence available in 2026, is that AI changed what students can hand in far more than it changed what learning requires. The rest of the chapter tests that claim.

A shock with a date on it

Most changes in education arrive slowly, through committees, pilots and new textbooks. This one arrived on an ordinary day in late 2022, when ChatGPT opened to the public. Within weeks, any student with a phone and a connection could ask for an essay, a summary or a worked solution and receive one in seconds.

Teachers moved quickly too, faster than their schools. In England, a large teacher panel tracked the change month by month. The share of teachers who had used generative AI for school work rose from 17% in April 2023 to 42% by November (Open Innovation Team & Department for Education, 2024). Most of that use happened behind the scenes, since only 7% had used it in a lesson. In the United States, teacher use more than doubled, from 25% in 2023–24 to 53% in spring 2025, while 54% of students reported using AI for school (Doss et al., 2025). My reading of these figures is that the first change happened at the teacher’s desk, not in front of the class. With only 7% using AI in a lesson, most students had not yet seen their teachers use it openly. That matters, because students learn how to use a tool partly by watching an adult use it well.

Beyond these two countries, the pattern holds. The OECD’s 2024 survey of lower-secondary teachers found that around one in three had used AI in the previous year (OECD, 2025). Its range was striking, from under 20% in France and Japan to roughly three quarters in Singapore and the United Arab Emirates. The OECD’s 2026 outlook on digital education reports that 37% of teachers use generative AI for work, and that one in ten say their school prohibits AI in teaching (OECD, 2026, pp. 3, 19).

A word of caution belongs here. Surveys define use differently, so their numbers rarely agree. Two UK surveys of students put AI use for schoolwork at 14% and 67% (Open Innovation Team & Department for Education, 2024). The direction, though, is beyond dispute. Use spread within about two years, which is very fast for anything in education.

Policy could not keep up. When UNESCO published the first global guidance on generative AI in education in 2023, only China had rules written specifically for the technology (Miao & Holmes, 2023). The guidance suggested a minimum age of 13 for classroom use, while admitting that even this might be too young. By 2025, only 45% of American principals reported any policy or guidance on AI, and just 34% of teachers reported policies on AI and academic integrity (Doss et al., 2025).

Frameworks followed the guidance. UNESCO set out AI competencies for teachers and, separately, twelve competencies for students in 2024 (Miao & Cukurova, 2024; Miao et al., 2024). The OECD and the European Commission then built an AI literacy framework for schools that will feed into the PISA 2029 assessment (OECD/European Union, 2026). Within four years of ChatGPT’s release, AI literacy had moved from a novelty to an expected outcome of schooling.

Reading these frameworks side by side, I notice how fast the expectations grew and how slowly the support followed. Teachers were asked to teach AI literacy within a few years of the tool’s release, often before anyone had trained them to use it. Frameworks tell us where to go. They do not give a teacher the time, the device or the confidence to get there. That gap between what is expected and what is provided runs through the rest of this book.

Students felt the gap. More than 80% of American students said their teachers had never explicitly taught them how to use AI for schoolwork (Doss et al., 2025). Half worried that someone might falsely accuse them of cheating. Educators and experts in England widely judged the early school bans short-sighted (Open Innovation Team & Department for Education, 2024). Many students seem to be left to work out acceptable use of AI by themselves. A teacher does not need to wait for a school policy to show a class one acceptable and one unacceptable use of AI on a real task.

Many teachers held back, and their reasons deserve a hearing. Among lower-secondary teachers who had not used AI, three in four said they lacked the knowledge or skills to teach with it (OECD, 2025). About a third felt overwhelmed, and about half did not think teaching should involve AI at all. In the United States, a quarter of teachers judged that AI tools did more harm than good in schools, and 35% were not sure (Lin, 2024). Uncertainty, more than enthusiasm or alarm, marked the first year.

The change also reached beyond homework. In the United Kingdom, 64% of children aged 9 to 17 had used an AI chatbot (Internet Matters, 2025). One in eight of those users said they talked to it because they had no one else to speak with. Chapter 8 returns to what that means for students’ wellbeing. When a student mentions a chatbot, a teacher should not assume the conversation was only about homework.

Why did this wave feel so different from earlier ones? Part of the answer lies in what the technology touched. Previous tools mostly changed how teachers taught. This one changed what students could produce on their own, at home and out of sight. UNESCO’s 2023 report saw the problem early. If written assignments no longer show what a student has mastered, it warned, assessment methods will need to develop (UNESCO, 2023).

That is the real shock behind the news stories. Homework, essays and take-home problems have long let teachers see how students think. Generative AI did not take that view away completely, but it made it much less clear.

What actually changed

Four things changed in a short time. Help became cheap, detection became unreliable, authority shifted, and the link between a finished task and real learning grew weaker. A fifth change sat on the teacher’s side of the desk, and it was more hopeful.

Help became cheap

Students have always sought help with homework, from parents, older siblings, tutors and guidebooks. Generative AI made that help free, constant and private. A chatbot does not get tired, and it never asks why a student needs the answer.

The evidence on cheating tells a more surprising story than the news stories did. At three American high schools, the share of students admitting any cheating stayed level or fell after ChatGPT arrived (Lee et al., 2024). A larger 2024 survey of 4,354 students in six high schools found that 72% admitted some form of cheating (Chen et al., 2026). AI seems to have changed the tools of dishonesty more than its scale. In that survey, about a quarter reported unauthorised use of AI or digital aids. Yet the most common use of chatbots was to explain a concept, reported by 82% of student users (Chen et al., 2026).

Students also drew their own lines. Most of them, 83%, said AI should never write a whole assignment (Chen et al., 2026). American teenagers in a national survey said much the same. Only 18% of those who had heard of ChatGPT thought it acceptable for writing essays, while 54% thought it fine for researching new topics (Sidoti et al., 2025). The evidence here is emerging rather than settled, because it rests on self-report in one country. Still, it complicates the picture of a generation outsourcing everything.

For teachers, this is good news with a warning attached. Most students do not want a machine to do all their work, and many use it to understand rather than to copy. That gives us something to build on: a shared sense of where the line sits. The warning is that students draw the line in different places, and they draw it alone. A class that talks openly about acceptable use will draw it more consistently than thirty students guessing in private.

Detection became a trap

Faced with fluent text, many schools reached for AI detectors. The research gives teachers good reason for caution. A systematic test of 14 detection tools found them neither accurate nor reliable, with no tool reaching 80% accuracy (Weber-Wulff et al., 2023). Accuracy fell further when students edited or paraphrased the text.

The tools also carry a hidden bias. In one study, seven detectors wrongly flagged essays by non-native English writers as machine-written in 61% of cases on average, while judging essays by American eighth-graders almost perfectly (Liang et al., 2023). Simple, careful prose looked suspicious to the software. For teachers in multilingual classrooms, where many students write in a second or third language, that finding should stop any accusation that rests on a detector score alone. Evidence on unreliability is strong, though researchers tested 2023 tools, mostly on adult writing.

My advice is to treat a suspicion as a reason to talk with the student, not as proof. Ask the student to explain a paragraph or talk through their sources, as the examples later in this chapter suggest.

Authority shifted

A quieter change touched the teacher’s standing as a source of knowledge. The botanist in my study, who spoke of an authority crisis, also described students already confused when weak content sat at the top of search results (Ilyas, 2026, Participant L5). A chatbot sharpened that problem. It answers instantly and confidently, even when it is wrong, and a student cannot easily tell the difference.

Other university teachers in my study responded in different ways. One discouraged students from ‘relying too much on AI’, warning that plagiarism issues could arise (Ilyas, 2026, Participant M01). Another steered students towards ChatGPT as a starting point, accepting that it makes errors but hoping they would move ‘gradually’ from its basic answers towards original sources (Ilyas, 2026, Participant M08). Both positions are reasonable, and together they show teachers renegotiating a relationship with knowledge that once felt settled.

Performance stopped meaning learning

The most important change is also the least visible. A finished task used to imply some learning, however imperfect. Generative AI loosened that link.

The clearest evidence comes from a large randomised trial in a Turkish high school (Bastani et al., 2025). Nearly 1,000 students practised mathematics with one of three supports, namely a standard GPT-4 chat interface, a tutor version with teacher-designed hints and safeguards, or no AI at all. With the standard chatbot, practice scores rose by 48%. When the researchers removed the chatbot for the exam, those same students scored 17% lower than classmates who never had it. The tutor version raised practice scores by 127% and largely removed the harm to exam results.

One detail from that trial deserves a teacher’s attention. Students who leaned on the chatbot did not perceive any loss in their learning (Bastani et al., 2025). They felt they had learned. Many had simply copied. A student's sense of having learned is not a safe guide, so neither the student nor the teacher can rely on it. A short check without the tool, such as a few questions answered from memory, shows what a practice score cannot.

This finding is strong for its setting, a single school and subject, and it fits a wider pattern. A smaller study with university students, still a preprint, found that using an AI to replace one’s own thinking harmed learning, while using it to explain or check work helped (Lehmann et al., 2025). The OECD’s review of the evidence reached a similar view in 2026. Heavy dependence on AI, it found, reduces how actively students monitor their own thinking and opens a gap between how well they perform and how much they learn (OECD, 2026).

Well-designed tutors have also outperformed good teaching in at least one trial. University physics students using a purpose-built AI tutor learned significantly more, in less time, than they did in an active-learning class (Kestin et al., 2025). The tutor followed established teaching principles, guiding students step by step instead of handing over answers. That study covered one elite university and two lessons, so it shows what is possible rather than what is typical.

Design changes the outcome. The same technology helped learning once its designers turned it into a tutor that gives hints, withholds full solutions and expects students to try first. That pattern will return throughout this book.

A teacher who decides when students may see a hint, and when they must try first, is doing the same design work that turned a harmful chatbot into a helpful tutor. That design work does not need a research team. It needs a clear rule about the order of work, and the confidence to keep to it.

The teacher’s desk got lighter

Teachers have used the same tools to prepare lessons, and here the news is better. Among teachers who used AI in the OECD survey, 64% used it to generate lesson plans and 68% to learn about or summarise a topic (OECD, 2025). The university teachers in my own study described similar habits. Rather than structuring everything in a Word file before a 90-minute class, the geographer asks an AI for an outline and adds, ‘I implement it from there’ (Ilyas, 2026, Participant H03).

Some uses went beyond saving time. The same teacher used an AI tool to draft marking criteria for presentations, then shared them with students so they would know ‘exactly where they might lose marks’ (Ilyas, 2026, Participant H03). The computer scientist in my study valued how it could produce ‘examples, slides, or even assignment questions upon request’ (Ilyas, 2026, Participant H01). Teachers kept their doubts too. Across OECD systems, seven in ten teachers believed AI could let students pass off other people’s work as their own (OECD, 2025).

A carefully designed trial in England put numbers on the benefit. Science teachers who used ChatGPT with a short guide spent about 56 minutes a week on lesson preparation, against 81.5 minutes for colleagues without it (Roy et al., 2024). An expert panel, who did not know how teachers had made each resource, found no difference in quality. The evidence here is strong, though it measured teacher time rather than student learning.

In government schools in Karnataka, India, a tool that helped teachers adapt lesson plans saved them about two hours a week and reduced their reported stress (Varuvel Dennison et al., 2026). Teachers there were clear about its limits. The hours they gained did not solve staff shortages or the paperwork that still consumed their days.

So the list of changes is real. Help is cheap. Detection is weak. Authority is in question. Performance and learning have drifted apart. Planning can be faster. Each change sounds dramatic. Yet each one also leaves something essential exactly where it was.

We have been here before

Teachers who have worked for a while may greet promises of revolution with a tired smile. They have heard them before. Larry Cuban, a historian of classroom technology, describes a cycle that keeps repeating (Cuban & Jandrić, 2015). Reformers make extravagant claims for a new machine. Studies then find that teachers use it rarely, or in familiar ways. Disappointment follows, and teachers get the blame.

The pattern began long before computers. In 1926, Sidney Pressey built a machine that set multiple-choice questions and gave students instant feedback. He argued that it would teach as well as test, and that it might spare teachers the labour of marking (Holmes & Tuomi, 2022). Almost a century later, that same promise to spare teachers their marking appears in the case for AI.

Computers arrived with even louder claims. Cuban’s study of schools in Silicon Valley found the machines oversold and underused (Cuban, 2001). Years later he admitted that his earlier forecast of very low use had proved too gloomy. Use did grow, but mostly in traditional, teacher-centred ways (Cuban & Jandrić, 2015).

Cuban borrows an idea from Donald Schön that explains much of this. Schools show a kind of dynamic conservatism, absorbing each new tool into routines they already have (Cuban & Jandrić, 2015). The tool changes. Routines often do not.

Cuban adds a warning that matters for this book. Theories of technology adoption often treat teachers who say no as a problem to fix. Teachers may have good reasons to refuse, and those reasons deserve a hearing rather than blame (Cuban & Jandrić, 2015).

What might those good reasons be? From my years in schools and my research with university teachers, I would name three. Some teachers refuse because the tool does not fit their subject, like the historian in Chapter 3 who prefers real objects to slides. Some refuse because the conditions are missing: no power, no device, no time to learn. Others have simply seen earlier promises fail. Each reason calls for a different response, whether better tools, better conditions or better evidence. Blame fits none of them.

UNESCO’s global review of technology in education reached a blunt verdict in 2023. Digital technology, it concluded, has changed education but not transformed it (UNESCO, 2023). The change has been incremental and uneven, larger in some places than in others. Outside the most technologically advanced countries, schools rarely use computers in classrooms at scale.

Calculators offer the closest precedent for the homework question. They too sparked debates about what counts as cheating (Gogh & Kovari, 2025). A meta-analysis of 54 studies found that calculator use did not hinder the development of mathematical skills, and students who used calculators held better attitudes towards mathematics (Ellington, 2003). The strongest results appeared when calculators were part of both teaching and testing. That finding carries a lesson for AI. Tools and assessment need to agree about what counts as a student’s own work.

Each school system still decides when a calculator may stand in for a student’s own arithmetic. In my experience, most students in Punjab only start using one at about 13. Even then, the exam hall keeps it out. Punjab’s revived Grade 8 public exam drew nearly a million students in March 2026. Calculators, phones and smartwatches stayed at the door, even though AI helped mark the answer scripts for the first time (“Grade 8 Board-Style Exams Conclude,” 2026; Khattak, 2026). The research supports part of Punjab’s caution. An early meta-analysis found that calculators in Grade 4 lessons weakened computation on calculator-free tests (Hembree & Dessart, 1986, as cited in Ellington, 2003). By Grade 8, though, Ellington (2003) recommended calculators in tests of problem solving, so Punjab’s ban may cost its students practice their peers elsewhere already enjoy. ChatGPT reopens the same question for reading, writing and reasoning.

Even automated feedback on writing is older than ChatGPT. In a randomised trial across 178 public schools in Brazil, a machine-learning system that scored practice essays for the national exam improved students’ writing (Ferman et al., 2020). Teachers did not hand their work to the machine. Their students discussed more essays with them, and the researchers read this as teachers shifting towards individual, non-routine teaching. The study, still a working paper, suggests that teachers adapt to new tools rather than disappear behind them.

A major review in October 2022 delivered a sober verdict on the evidence. Robust, independent evidence of the kind expected in medical research, it concluded, remained elusive for AI in education (Holmes & Tuomi, 2022). Even Cognitive Tutor, one of the best-known tutoring systems, had shown mixed results for algebra and no clear effect on general mathematics. I treat the Turkish and physics trials as good reasons to try something carefully, not as proof that it will work in every classroom.

So is this just another turn of the old wheel? Partly. The hype, the uneven use and the thin evidence all look familiar. Yet three differences stand out, and they explain the title of this chapter.

The first is the route in. Earlier technologies entered through the school gate, after institutions chose and installed them. Generative AI walked in through students’ pockets.

The second is what the tool produces. A calculator does arithmetic. Today’s chatbots can write the essay, explain the method and draft the reflection, in a student’s own style if the student asks. It produces the very things schools use as evidence of learning.

The third is speed. Within about two and a half years, a majority of school-age young people in the United States and the United Kingdom were using AI tools (Doss et al., 2025; Internet Matters, 2025). That pace left no time for the usual cycle of pilots and evaluation.

These differences do not make the old lessons useless. They make them urgent.

What has stayed the same

If the list of changes is real, so is the list of things that have not moved at all. The first is how people learn.

Learning still requires effort. Classroom research shows that students remember more when they work to recall what they have learned than when they simply reread it. A review of 50 classroom experiments found that this kind of retrieval practice improved learning across subjects and ages, with medium or large benefits in most cases (Agarwal et al., 2021). No chatbot can do that recalling on a student’s behalf. When it does the work, the student loses the very effort that builds memory. Most of this research comes from wealthy Western countries, with only 6% of the experiments taking place elsewhere, so it is strong but not yet universal.

Feedback still matters, and the right kind matters most. A meta-analysis of 435 studies found that feedback has a medium effect on learning overall (Wisniewski et al., 2020). Feedback rich in information about the task, the process and the student’s own self-regulation had far larger effects than praise or simple corrections. AI can now draft feedback in seconds. Whether that feedback helps still depends on whether it tells a student something useful, and whether the student acts on it.

Relationships still shape learning. A meta-analysis covering nearly 250,000 students from preschool to secondary school found that warm teacher–student relationships go with higher engagement and achievement (Roorda et al., 2017). The OECD’s 2026 review reached a parallel conclusion about AI. Motivation, relationships and social and emotional learning remain human responsibilities (OECD, 2026).

Families and students seem to know this. In deliberative research with parents and students in England, participants felt more comfortable with teachers using AI than with students using it directly, and they wanted human oversight (Thinks Insight & Strategy, 2024).

Homework itself has not changed its purpose, even if its form must. Yet its evidence base was never as strong as its place in school life suggests. The Education Endowment Foundation rates its average impact as moderate, about five months of extra progress, but on evidence of low security that predates generative AI (Education Endowment Foundation, 2021). Quality matters more than quantity. Homework that connects to classroom learning, with a clear purpose and feedback, works best. A recent systematic review compared primary students who did homework with those who did not (Guo et al., 2024). Homework helped most with arithmetic calculation, less with problem solving and not at all with understanding concepts, though only three studies tested concepts. Most of its studies were old and of low quality.

That gap in the evidence is worth naming plainly. Nobody yet has strong research on homework in the age of AI. Teachers are redesigning it on the basis of principles, not proof, and they deserve to know that.

The teacher’s judgement still sits at the centre. Every framework that has appeared since 2022 places it there, from UNESCO’s competency framework for teachers onwards (Miao & Cukurova, 2024). The university teachers in my research showed what that judgement looks like in practice. The computer scientist welcomed AI for organising material but added that ChatGPT ‘may not provide the latest info or specific local examples’ (Ilyas, 2026, Participant H01). Knowing the local, the particular and the current remains the teacher’s work.

My doctoral research also found a split that matters for every school. Teachers with lower digital competence tended to experience AI as a threat and responded with surveillance and prohibition. Those with higher competence used it, verified its output and taught their students to question it (Ilyas, 2026). The technology was the same in both groups. What differed was the teacher’s confidence and skill.

Inequality, finally, has not moved. AI arrived in classrooms that were already unequal. In the United States, teachers in higher-poverty schools were less likely to use AI, and their principals were about half as likely to report guidance on it (Kaufman et al., 2025). A small survey in Pakistan found that 73% of teachers feared a lack of devices would leave some students behind (Raza & Farooq, 2025). The old divides decide who benefits from the new tool.

So the claim with which this chapter began holds up. AI changed what students can hand in, how quickly teachers can plan and how hard it is to see the thinking behind the work. It did not change what learning requires. Effort, feedback, relationships and judgement still do most of the work.

That conclusion raises the next question. To decide where AI helps and where it harms, teachers need a working picture of what this technology actually is. Chapter 2 provides it.

In practice

Ideas only help when they reach a real timetable. The examples below show how teachers in different phases have begun to respond. Treat them as starting points rather than recipes.

A primary classroom. For younger students, most sensible AI use happens on the teacher’s side of the desk. UNESCO’s guidance suggests 13 as the minimum age for classroom use of generative AI (Miao & Holmes, 2023). A teacher of eight- and nine-year-olds might ask an AI to draft one short reading passage at three levels of difficulty, then check every sentence before printing. Homework stays human. Students read aloud to someone at home, and the next morning the class answers a few quick questions on the story from memory. That small act of recall builds learning in a way no chatbot can do for them (Agarwal et al., 2021).

A secondary English class. A teacher who once set a take-home essay might divide the work differently. Students may use AI openly to explore ideas or explain a difficult concept, which is the use most students already consider fair (Chen et al., 2026). The drafting then happens in class, by hand or on school devices without AI access. Each student adds a short note on how the essay developed and explains one paragraph aloud to the teacher.

A mathematics department. Twenty identical practice problems make the easiest homework for a chatbot. A department might set fewer problems and ask students to explain their reasoning, find the error in a worked example or justify their choice of method. Such tasks are harder to hand off, though this rests on practitioners’ judgement rather than tested evidence (Gogh & Kovari, 2025). The calculator research adds one more principle. Tools and tests should agree about what counts as the student’s own work (Ellington, 2003).

A teacher’s own planning. The English trial suggests a modest, realistic pattern. Teachers typically used ChatGPT for one or two activities, such as quiz questions or a fresh idea for an activity (Roy et al., 2024). They spent the time they gained on other planning, on teaching or simply on a lighter week. That is a sensible goal. The aim is a teacher with more time for students, never a lesson that a machine wrote.

A whole staff. Students notice when the rules change from one classroom to the next. In a US survey, 77% of parents said that whether AI use counts as cheating depends on the situation (Doss et al., 2025). Staff can remove some of that confusion by agreeing which homework tasks allow AI, which do not, and how students should say when they have used it. A short statement that staff revisit each term protects students from guessing and teachers from arguing case by case.

A homework redesign. Each week, keep one task that only makes sense with the student’s own material: a short interview with a family member, a photograph of a local example, or a reflection on that day’s class discussion. AI can help with the writing, but it cannot supply the material.

In a university course. A lecturer who sets a take-home essay can ask every student for a short note on how they used AI, if at all, and then talk for ten minutes with a few students chosen at random about their sources. A historian in my study already checks references in this way (Ilyas, 2026, Participant H02).

When resources are thin

Much of the research above comes from well-connected schools. Many readers of this book teach elsewhere, in classrooms with one shared phone, a weak signal or no reliable electricity. For them, the question of what AI changed takes a different shape.

Access comes first. UNESCO’s 2023 report found that only 40% of primary schools worldwide had internet access (UNESCO, 2023). In 2024, about 38% of the world’s people lived where mobile internet reached them but did not use it (Shanahan & Bahia, 2025). That usage gap covered half of South Asia and almost two thirds of Sub-Saharan Africa, including about 130 million people in Pakistan alone (Shanahan & Bahia, 2025, p. 14). Tools that assume a constant connection will miss many of the students who most need help.

The learning crisis itself also predates AI. In 2019, an estimated 86% of ten-year-olds in Sub-Saharan Africa could not read and understand a simple text (Asghar & Dintilhac, 2025). No chatbot created that problem, and none will solve it alone. In interviews for a World Bank case study, nearly every Nigerian stakeholder mentioned unreliable electricity as a barrier (Srinivasan & Twinomugisha, 2025).

Yet the early evidence from low-resource settings is genuinely encouraging, as long as teachers stay in charge. In Benin City, Nigeria, secondary students worked in pairs with an AI chatbot during twelve after-school sessions over six weeks, with trained teachers guiding them. Their scores rose by about a third of a standard deviation, despite power cuts and internet failures during the programme (De Simone et al., 2025). Girls gained the most, which made up for their weaker start. Students who started stronger or came from better-off homes also gained more, a reminder that even a helpful tool can widen some gaps. In Ghana, a maths tutor that ran through WhatsApp on basic phones raised students’ scores at a marginal cost of about $5 each (Henkel et al., 2024). One is a working paper and the other a short, preliminary conference paper, so the evidence is promising rather than proven.

The Nigerian study shares the design lesson of the Turkish trial. The AI did not work alone. That part needs no special tool. A teacher with one shared device can still keep students working together and stay close enough to guide them.

Three practical lessons follow.

  • One phone is enough to start. A single teacher’s phone can still bring AI into a lesson, if the teacher asks the questions and the class judges the answers together.

  • Homework need not depend on a device. Setting it that way protects students who lack a quiet space or a connection at home (Education Endowment Foundation, 2021).

  • Teachers’ own planning is often the best place to start. In Karnataka, India, most teachers reached a lesson-planning assistant through their own smartphones (Varuvel Dennison et al., 2026).

Researchers working in these settings have begun to sketch what suitable tools could look like. A World Bank case study urges governments to fund ultra-lightweight models under 100 megabytes and voice-first tools that work over basic 2G networks (Srinivasan & Twinomugisha, 2025). Asghar and Dintilhac (2025), writing for UNESCO’s monitoring report, point to offline, on-device tutors, voice models for African languages and practice that reaches students through print, SMS and WhatsApp. These are proposals rather than proven solutions, but they point in a sensible direction.

The deeper lesson is an old one. New technology tends to help most where conditions are already good, and the most disadvantaged often cannot benefit (UNESCO, 2023). A teacher who knows that can work to make sure the new tool narrows gaps rather than widening them.

My research suggests a simple way to read this section. In my Infrastructure-First Digital Competence (IFDC) framework, a teacher’s skill with technology rests on four levels, like the floors of a building (Ilyas et al., 2026b). At the bottom are basic conditions such as power, devices and a connection (structural provisions). Next comes feeling safe to try and to make mistakes (psychological safety). Then come chances to learn (developmental opportunities). Only at the top does a teacher choose well when to use AI and when not to (strategic practice). The routines above belong at the top. They work only when the floors beneath them are in place.

Questions for reflection

Take these questions to a department meeting, or sit with them alone over a cup of tea.

  1. Look at the homework you set this week: which task could a chatbot finish for your students in a minute, and what would they lose if it did?

  2. When you last suspected that AI had written a piece of work, what evidence did you actually have?

  3. Which earlier classroom technology did your school welcome with high hopes, and what did it really change?

  4. Where could AI give you back planning time without taking the judgement out of your hands?

  5. Which of your students would miss out if homework assumed a device and a connection at home?

Framework connections

This chapter builds the habit of reflection that every later chapter depends on. In DigCompEdu, it connects most closely with reflective practice and with designing assessment (Redecker, 2017). In UNESCO’s framework for teachers, it belongs to the human-centred mindset, at the first level, acquire (Miao & Cukurova, 2024). The table below shows which part of each framework this chapter connects to, including my own Infrastructure-First Digital Competence (IFDC) framework (Ilyas et al., 2026b). The framework map at the back of the book gathers these links for every chapter.

Framework Where this chapter sits What it asks of you
DigCompEdu 1.3 Reflective practice; 4.1 Assessment strategies You ask what evidence you really have about student work, and redesign homework so it needs the student’s own material.
UNESCO AI competency framework Human agency (Human-centred mindset, Acquire level) You keep decisions about homework, marking and AI use in human hands, starting with a staff statement agreed each term.
IFDC (Ilyas et al., 2026b) Strategic practice (skilled choices about tools) The chapter’s routines sit at this top level. They work only when power, safety and time to learn are already in place.

UNESCO does not name this chapter’s topic. The placing is my own.
