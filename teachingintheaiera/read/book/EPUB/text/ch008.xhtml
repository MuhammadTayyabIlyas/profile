Chapter 6. Fair Assessment in the AI Era

In a senior physical education course in Toronto, students used AI to help organise a warm-up plan. Their teacher then required something the plan alone could not supply. Students had to ‘lead and explain the routine in person’ (C21 Canada, 2025, p. 5). The written work remained, alongside a performance and an explanation.

This school account comes from a report on teachers exploring an AI Assessment Scale. Leon Furze, one of its developers, helped train them. The report describes practice and teachers’ reflections, without a controlled test of learning. Some redesigned tasks never reached full implementation, and individual coaching took considerable time (C21 Canada, 2025, pp. 4–5).

Still, the physical education example makes the assessment question unusually clear. A student could arrive with a polished plan and then struggle to explain or lead it. Equally, someone might use help with planning and still demonstrate the intended skill. The teacher needed evidence beyond the document to distinguish those possibilities.

What can our assessments still tell us?

Chapter 5 planted the gap between a fluent answer and knowing. Here that gap becomes a practical problem for the teacher holding a student’s work. The product may deserve praise, yet leave the student’s understanding uncertain. We need to decide what evidence would make that understanding visible.

‘How do you think these tools help accurately gauge a student’s performance?’ I asked a zoologist.

She did not start with tools.

‘Look, for me, quizzes are the most important. Students these days rely on ChatGPT and so on for assignments, so there’s a lot of copying. That’s why I assign fewer marks to assignments and more to quizzes. Even so, I do a short viva on the assignment to ensure the student has actually read something themselves’ (Ilyas, 2026, Participant M06).

A short viva means a few questions about the student’s own work. The answers show whether the student really knows what the paper says.

A geographer made the opposite move and opened the marking up. He used an AI tool to draft a rubric for presentations, then showed it to students ‘so they’d know, for instance, how many marks go for eye contact, body language, data usage’ (Ilyas, 2026, Participant H03).

One teacher added a conversation about the work. The other showed students exactly what would earn marks. Both were trying to find out what a piece of work can still tell a teacher.

An essay still has a place. Students need to develop sustained arguments, organise evidence and find words for difficult ideas. Generative AI changes what we can infer from an essay completed beyond our view. It also changes the conditions under which we should ask students to write one.

The response begins with validity. That means asking whether the evidence supports the claim we want to make about a learner. Integrity matters because dishonest help can break that link. Yet a student can follow every rule and still complete an assessment that tells us little.

Our task has two sides. We need to notice when submitted work no longer supports a learning claim. Schools also need fair ways to find out what a student can do, without turning ordinary teaching into continuous investigation.

The evidence offers firmer warnings than settled replacements. Detector tests expose serious limits, and school studies question AI’s readiness to mark essays alone. Evidence for redesigned assessment remains emerging, with much coming from higher education. Throughout this chapter, proposed classroom examples illustrate decisions teachers could try. They are not reports of lessons that happened.

What the mark allows us to claim

Michael Kane’s work on validity came before ChatGPT. Writing from Educational Testing Service, he argues that we validate the ways scores are interpreted and used (Kane, 2013, p. 1). A test score does not mean the same thing in every case. Its evidence supports certain claims under certain conditions.

For example, a correct answer might show that a student can recognise a fraction in a familiar diagram. A claim that the student understands fractions in many settings needs more support. An unfamiliar diagram, a practical comparison and an explanation might each add to it. The stronger claim needs stronger evidence.

Now consider an essay written at home. A high mark might support a claim about the quality of the submitted text. Claiming that its author can build that argument independently adds an assumption about how the text came into being. AI can make that assumption unsafe, even when the writing meets every criterion.

This does not make all help unacceptable. Dictionaries, discussion, teacher guidance and revision already shape written work. The question is about the skill we mean to assess and the help the task allows. If building sentences matters, a tool that rewrites sentences changes the evidence. Where making sense of historical sources matters, help with spelling may not affect the intended claim.

Assessment design starts by making those distinctions clear. A teacher might want to know whether students can recall an idea, apply it, compare explanations or judge evidence. Each aim needs different observations. Asking for an attractive poster does not by itself show any of them.

Dawson and colleagues make a similar argument about universities (Dawson et al., 2024). Their paper argues that validity matters more than ever-longer lists of banned behaviour. Validity here means whether the evidence supports what we claim about a student. Dawson declares that he has had funding from Turnitin and Inspera. Neither company funded this article. The argument is about what assessment evidence can show. It is not about whether a particular detection product works.

For schools, the distinction helps separate two decisions. One is about learning and asks what we can say now about the student’s understanding. The other is about conduct. It asks whether the evidence supports a finding that someone broke a rule. Uncertainty about learning does not prove misconduct. A school may need fresh assessment evidence while an allegation is still unproven.

I would keep those decisions separate in school records. One entry could explain the learning evidence we still need. A different record would hold any conduct finding and its grounds. Combining them risks turning an unanswered subject question into a judgement about character.

The scale of misuse also needs care. Ofqual recorded 100 cases of plagiarism involving AI in England’s summer 2025 examinations. These were 2.0% of recorded cases of student malpractice (Ofqual, 2025, p. 7). They describe cases that were found and that led to penalties. The figures cannot tell us how much AI use without permission took place in ordinary homework.

Chapter 5 has already shown how students say they use AI for schoolwork. Those accounts and official examination figures answer different questions. A teacher who says they noticed misuse is also not describing a proven case. Setting these side by side as rival rates would confuse the issue.

Even perfect honesty would leave some weak assessments as they are. A student may memorise a model answer and repeat it without understanding its reasoning. Another may understand an idea but struggle to express it in the required language. Both cases remind us that validity is about more than who wrote the work.

Black and Wiliam’s account of formative assessment supplies the next step (Black & Wiliam, 2009). Evidence becomes formative when people interpret and use it to decide what learning needs next. Collecting more work alone does not accomplish that. Teachers need an observation that changes a decision.

For instance, a short explanation may reveal that a student confuses the size of a denominator with the size of a fraction. The next lesson can address that mistake. A complete folder of polished worksheets may reveal less. Quantity cannot substitute for a clear question about learning.

There is a modest answer to the chapter’s central question already. Assessments can still tell us useful things when their conditions, evidence and claims fit together. AI makes that fit harder to assume. Teachers have to make it visible.

Why a detector cannot settle it

A detector appears to offer a quicker route. Submit the essay, read the score and decide whether its author used AI. Unfortunately, that chain contains several different claims. A tool classifies features of text. Teachers then interpret the result as evidence about a person’s actions.

One warning comes from school writing. Fleckenstein and colleagues asked teachers to judge argumentative essays from a school context (Fleckenstein et al., 2024). Trainee teachers spotted 45.1% of AI texts correctly. Experienced teachers spotted 37.8%, and both groups were too confident (pp. 3, 5). Some AI writing was missed because it looked weak.

Parts of the study’s reporting do not agree, which limits how far we can read its details. Its tables and main finding still challenge the belief that teachers can spot AI by reading. Teachers should take an unexpected change in writing seriously. Noticing it is the start of an enquiry; it cannot show how the change happened.

Some software did much better under narrow conditions. Walters tested 16 detectors on AI papers that had not been changed. He also tested older papers by university students (Walters, 2023). Copyleaks and Turnitin sorted every paper correctly in that test. Originality.ai reached 98% across the 126 papers (pp. 8–9).

This university comparison deserves a place beside the failures, but it has limits. None of the papers tried to disguise AI writing. The human papers came from an earlier period, and Walters notes that Turnitin’s systems may already have seen them. He names unedited text as a major limitation (Walters, 2023, pp. 6, 14). Success in that test does not show that detectors are reliable on real school work, which is often mixed and edited.

Perkins and colleagues tested the harder question (Perkins et al., 2024b). They asked generative tools to change generated text, for example by paraphrasing it and changing its style. Detectors did worse after these changes. Their combined score dropped from 39.5% to 22.14% (pp. 14–15).

Those percentages describe the researchers’ combined score. They are not a simple share of students identified correctly. The sample was small and did not represent a school population. Its practical warning is still clear. A detector’s result depends on what someone does to the text before it is handed in.

The lecturer in my study with the question bank, from Chapter 4, told me a story about her own student.

‘I once sent some AI-generated content to my MPhil/PhD student, and she returned it in just fifteen minutes “AI-free,” using some software that conceals AI origins,’ she said. ‘We had no idea’ (Ilyas, 2026, Participant L4).

The tool had hidden the signs that a machine wrote it. Fifteen minutes. One student. Perkins and colleagues found the same weakness in a controlled test. Here it was in an ordinary week, at a Pakistani university. If hiding AI writing takes minutes, a teacher cannot treat the absence of a flag as evidence that the student wrote the text.

Errors also go in the other direction. A false positive labels human writing as generated. A false negative misses generated writing. Improving one kind of result does not mean the other improves. Teachers need both error rates. Both should be measured under conditions like those of their students’ work.

Bias adds another difficulty. Liang and colleagues tested detectors and found that they often misclassified writing by non-native English writers (Liang et al., 2023). Their materials included TOEFL essays, which cannot give a school-wide error rate. Turnitin disputed the wider claim with its own test of longer writing (Adamson, 2023).

The disagreement is contested evidence, with different texts, lengths and tools in play. Turnitin sells the detector it evaluated, and its tool did not form part of Liang’s test. Neither study shows fairness for every bilingual student or language. A school should resist turning unfamiliar phrasing into an accusation.

One university case shows what a wrong judgement can cost. A history student at a US university was accused of cheating. His professor had used detection software. The student, William Quarterman, supplied his document history, and the university cleared him (Jimenez, 2023). This is one reported case. It is not an estimate of how often detectors fail.

Its value lies in the sequence. An apparent problem in the answers led to software, then an allegation, then further evidence. A drafting record helped challenge the initial conclusion. Schools should make room for explanations before a tool’s label hardens into a decision.

Watermarking offers a different technical approach. A provider builds a statistical signal into generated text, then checks for that signal later. Google DeepMind funded and conducted work on SynthID-Text, with patent interests disclosed (Dathathri et al., 2024). The work shows technical promise, with limitations for some kinds of text.

A watermark cannot identify output from a model that never used it. Nor does identifying a source show that the student broke the task’s rules. Permitted help might carry the same signal as prohibited substitution. The assessment conditions still determine what the evidence means.

Ofqual’s advice treats a tool’s output as one part of an investigation, along with its false positives and false negatives (Ofqual, 2026, p. 8). Pakistan’s Higher Education Commission also says a detector result alone is not enough proof of misconduct (Higher Education Commission Pakistan, 2026a, p. 5). That Pakistani framework is for universities and colleges. It is not a school board rule.

My cautious default is not to use detector scores to award marks or to prove misconduct. If a school already receives them, staff should treat them as uncertain information. Such scores need evidence from another source. A clean report cannot prove understanding either. Nobody should need to write in a more complex style just to avoid suspicion.

Across these tests, the evidence of detector limits is strong. Current error rates on school work are still uncertain. The practical alternative is a calm discussion based on the work. Ask the student to explain a relevant choice. Ask, too, for any account they can give of how the work took shape. Teachers can compare that with ordinary classroom evidence. Trouble explaining may show a learning need, anxiety or a misreading of the task. It does not by itself prove deception.

Chapter 8 returns to responsible use and fair procedures. Its discussion can build on the boundary set out here. A detector classifies text imperfectly. The school remains responsible for any decision about a student.

Change the task and its conditions

Clear rules help students make honest choices. On their own, they cannot make an unsupervised task show independent understanding. The difference matters when schools adopt colour codes or scales for the AI use they allow. Telling students the rules is one job. Designing the assessment is another.

The AI Assessment Scale gives graded descriptions of the use allowed (Perkins et al., 2024a). Its authors built it for higher education. They also discussed how schools might use it. Teachers can separate tasks that exclude AI from tasks that allow it for preparation, editing or fuller use. The scale helps explain what is expected before work begins.

A label still leaves an evidence problem. If a student may use AI to plan but must write independently, the final text may not reveal whether that boundary held. Corbin and colleagues analyse this problem in higher education (Corbin et al., 2025). They distinguish changes in instructions from changes in assessment structure.

Their paper sets out an argument rather than testing it, and co-author Danny Liu also helped to write guidance on the two-lane approach it discusses. So it makes a case for redesign without proving that a particular model works. The main idea still applies widely. When teachers change where students work, what students must show and when teachers watch them, they change the evidence they can collect.

A pilot of the AI Assessment Scale needs similar restraint. Its developers evaluated implementation in higher education in Vietnam, without a control group (Furze et al., 2024). Recorded misconduct had already fallen before the scale began. The institution also changed how staff handled some AI use. So I would not adopt the scale expecting it to reduce misconduct by itself. Its clearer value, on this evidence, is that it helps students know what help a task allows before they start.

The Toronto school report gives us something more modest and more useful for classroom planning. Teachers reconsidered what counted as students’ own thinking, and some paired products with explanations or performances (C21 Canada, 2025). Their experiences suggest possibilities. They are not a tested school programme.

One approach combines protected demonstrations with tasks where tools remain available. A student might use sources, discussion and approved AI support during a project. At selected points, the teacher checks an essential part under conditions that limit outside help. The two forms answer related but distinct questions.

For example, a science project could assess how students compare explanations using evidence. A short classroom task could then check whether each student can interpret a new graph independently. The project shows how they work with resources. Classroom checks supply a clearer view of individual understanding.

When reporting achievement, I would describe those two claims separately. A single total can hide the gap between understanding a topic alone and doing well with a lot of support. Where a school needs one grade, teachers should still know which evidence supports each criterion.

Protecting work does not have to mean a return to long handwritten examinations for every task. England’s Curriculum and Assessment Review favours examinations. It also keeps teacher-assessed writing in primary school (Department for Education, 2025a, pp. 130, 136). These are policy choices about certain qualifications and stages of school. They do not settle the best format for every classroom decision.

Higher education guidance from the Quality Assurance Agency warns that a wholesale return to handwriting could reverse gains in accessibility (Quality Assurance Agency for Higher Education, 2023). A school account makes the concern concrete. Townsend Harris students questioned the speed demands of moving summer writing into handwritten class assessment (The Classic Summer Staff, 2025). This is one school’s students describing one change, so I treat it as a concern to check, not as a measure of how often handwriting harms access. It is still a reason to ask students how a new format affects them before it counts towards a grade.

The question for validity is which ability we mean to judge. Handwriting speed may matter in one assessment and get in the way in another. A student who uses an agreed writing aid may need it during protected work too. Limits on outside help should still keep proper access arrangements in place.

Authentic projects also need careful thought. A local topic, real audience or practical problem can give work purpose. AI can still produce a plausible proposal about it. Calling a task authentic does not guarantee that its author understands the proposal.

Before generative AI, Gulikers and colleagues built a framework for judging how authentic a task is (Gulikers et al., 2004). Their work draws on vocational nursing students and teachers, outside the school setting. It looks at the task, the context, the social setting, the product and the criteria together. It is a set of ideas to help design, with a small exploratory study behind it.

For a school project, the framework encourages a wider view than choosing a realistic title. Students could gather observations, explain a decision, respond to a changed constraint and judge the final result. Those actions give teachers several opportunities to see understanding. The local setting serves learning when students must think within it.

Rules should then follow the purpose. A task that tests how students build sentences on their own needs certain permissions. A task that tests how they criticise generated arguments needs different ones. Teachers can explain the reason in ordinary language. Students should know what help they may use and what they must show by themselves.

Drafts, checkpoints and short oral checks

Chapter 5 introduced ‘Ask about their own work’ and ‘Drafts in view’. My own doctoral research with university teachers in Pakistan recorded similar practices (Ilyas, 2026, p. 188). Those people were university teachers, and their accounts did not measure school outcomes. Here the task is to turn those routines into an assessment design that teachers can defend.

The aim is to observe a few key decisions. An initial idea can show what a student thinks the problem involves. A later choice can show how they use evidence. Explaining a revision can show whether it changed their understanding. Keeping every keystroke would add records but might not improve any of those observations.

I would ask for the smallest process record that can answer the assessment question. For a short argument, that might mean an initial claim, an evidence choice and a reason for one revision. Longer projects may need more. The record should help students think and teachers decide.

College Board’s school-level AP guidance shows one formal use of checkpoints (College Board, n.d.). Teachers meet students during the development of Capstone work and confirm its authenticity. This is an assessment requirement. The guidance does not test whether checkpoints improve learning. Schools can learn from its planned contact without copying its consequences into unrelated tasks.

A checkpoint has more value when it can change the teaching. If several students cannot explain why a source supports a claim, the teacher can model that link again. Work then continues with better guidance. The record captures a learning decision, alongside any value it has for assurance.

Version history can support this work, as the Quarterman case illustrated in higher education. Its absence cannot prove wrongdoing. Students may draft on paper, work offline or share a device. Even a long sequence of edits cannot, by itself, show understanding. The teacher still needs to connect records with the learning claim.

Oral work adds another kind of evidence. A student can explain why they chose an example, compare two answers or apply an idea to a changed problem. The answer may show understanding that writing hides. On the other hand, confident speech can hide weak subject knowledge if questions never ask for evidence.

Guidance for higher education recommends structured oral assessment. It warns that unstructured versions raise concerns about stress and reliability (Quality Assurance Agency for Higher Education, 2023). School teachers should bring that caution to short classroom checks. Each question needs a clear purpose. The demands on students should be roughly the same. A friendly talk still needs fair marking criteria.

For example, every student discussing an argument could identify its main claim and justify one piece of evidence. Follow-up questions could then explore the student’s actual answer. The teacher would judge the reasoning against shared criteria. Quick speech, accent and eye contact would carry no marks unless they belonged to the intended skill.

I would let students practise the format before it affects a grade that counts. Teachers should explain how pauses, questions to clarify and a request to repeat a question will work. A student who needs another way to respond should have a similar route to show the same understanding.

In my Pakistani university study, the geographer from the start of this chapter used AI to generate presentation criteria and shared them with students (Ilyas, 2026, p. 173, Participant H03). The account shows an effort to make expectations visible. It does not establish that every suggested criterion served the intended learning. Teachers still need to examine what a rubric rewards.

That examination matters when a tool suggests presentation polish alongside subject understanding. A student might read a confident script without understanding its evidence. Another might explain an idea hesitantly and accurately. Assessment design should make room to recognise those different performances.

The thesis’s later analysis contrasts teachers who treated assessment as a vulnerability with those who approached it as a design challenge (Ilyas, 2026, p. 216). That is my synthesis of university teachers’ accounts. It offers a useful orientation, without showing that any one redesigned method prevents misuse.

The next difficulty comes when pieces of evidence disagree. A strong project and a weak explanation may mean that help went beyond the limits of the task. They may also show uneven understanding across the topic. A teacher needs questions that tell those two cases apart. Repeating the same hard question more loudly will add little.

For example, a student might explain a source accurately but fail to connect it with the conclusion. That response gives the teacher something specific to assess. The student recognises information but needs help with reasoning. Calling the whole project understood or misunderstood would hide this difference. A more precise claim can guide a more useful next lesson.

I would plan how to respond to disagreement before setting an assessment that counts. The plan could name the fresh evidence that would make the learning claim clearer. It could also name who would review a disputed judgement. Students should know about that chance before they need it. Surprise questions after the work is handed in can make an ordinary check feel like an accusation.

Enough evidence may come from several brief encounters. A first attempt, a project decision and a later attempt done alone can support each other. They may also disagree. That disagreement gives the teacher a reason to look further into learning before making a broad claim.

Help with marking, care with judgement

AI also enters assessment from the teacher’s side. It can draft criteria, suggest comments and propose marks. These tasks differ in their consequences. A flawed comment may confuse a student, while a flawed grade may affect progression. Calling both assistance should not hide the difference.

School evidence on essay marking calls for caution. Tate and colleagues studied US secondary writing (Tate et al., 2024). Their main analyses used GPT-3.5. Exact agreement with human scores ranged from about 19% to 42% across samples (p. 9).

Human scorers agreed exactly on about 49% in the available comparison (Tate et al., 2024, p. 9). This also reminds us that teachers need moderation. Disagreement among humans does not make any machine result acceptable. The question remains whether a particular scoring process supports the intended decision. The gap between machine and human agreement matters most when a mark affects a student’s next step, and less when a mark only guides practice.

In one sample, AI gave English learners scores within a narrower range than human scorers used (Tate et al., 2024, pp. 5–6). This finding concerns that sample and model. It cannot describe every system’s treatment of multilingual students. Still, a reasonable average mark can conceal consequential differences for individual writers. If a school tries AI marking, I would check the marks of English learners and other multilingual writers one by one.

Seßler and colleagues studied German school essays and compared the results with teachers’ marks (Seßler et al., 2025). Their set held only 20 essays. Several models marked more generously than teachers and varied less (p. 468). This early evidence gives us a reason to look at each disagreement before we adopt automated grades.

Consistency answers yet another question. A tool might give the same mark every time while using the wrong standard. Asking it to grade twice would then repeat the same problem. Teachers need evidence that it agrees with criteria they can defend, as well as checks that its marks repeat.

A department considering AI marking also needs a shared human starting point. Teachers can read the same responses, explain their scores and discuss the criterion that produced a disagreement. Otherwise, a model may appear inconsistent simply because colleagues apply different standards. Equally, agreement with a shared mistake would offer false reassurance.

I would keep a small set of agreed examples showing different kinds of understanding. Include a response with sound reasoning and weak expression, alongside polished writing with an unsupported claim. Teachers could use these when checking a tool or welcoming a new colleague. The examples should represent the decisions the school actually needs to make.

Short-answer work offers more encouraging, still limited evidence. A preprint from Henkel and colleagues studied Ghanaian students’ reading responses (Henkel et al., n.d.). GPT-4 approached expert agreement overall, yet performed poorly on partially correct answers. Two things limit how far I would trust this result. It has not yet been checked through peer review, and the team disclosed an ongoing research partnership with Rising Academies, a school operator. Neither makes the finding wrong, but both are reasons to test it on your own students’ answers before relying on it.

Those partial answers often tell a teacher what to teach next. A student may identify the right event but misunderstand its cause. Another may grasp the idea while using an unexpected phrase. A broad correct-or-incorrect result can lose that distinction. The Ghanaian researchers also explain that schools would need support to implement such marking (Henkel et al., n.d., p. 22).

For regulated qualifications in England, Ofqual does not allow AI to be the only marker (Ofqual, 2024, p. 4). This rule from the regulator gives a clear limit in that setting. A teacher’s final judgement needs substance too. Approving a suggested mark without reading the work gives little protection.

I would begin any classroom marking trial with low-stakes work and a narrow question. Teachers could compare their own judgements with suggestions before students receive them. The comparison should include weak work, strong work and answers that show partial understanding. A tool that needs extensive correction may add work, despite producing marks quickly.

Feedback has a different evidence base. In a German school experiment, Meyer and colleagues tested GPT-3.5 feedback on students’ English writing (Meyer et al., 2024). Students receiving it improved their revisions slightly more than students receiving no feedback. The reported effect was small, at d = 0.19 (p. 5).

Its comparison did not include teacher feedback. Performance on a new writing task showed no statistically clear advantage (Meyer et al., 2024, pp. 5, 7). The study supports a narrow claim about revision after feedback. It does not show that generated comments replace teachers or produce lasting improvement.

A practical feedback decision asks what the student will do next. Pages of suggestions may offer less help than one clear question about an unsupported claim. Teachers can use AI to draft that question, then check it against the actual work. Students still need time to respond.

Suppose a draft lacks a reason connecting evidence to its conclusion. A teacher might ask a tool for several ways to explain that gap, using an invented example. She could select one and adapt its language. The student would then revise the connection and explain the change. No claim about learning would rest on the tool’s eloquence.

Feedback also creates evidence about the learner’s judgement. A student who accepts every suggestion may produce a better paragraph while learning little about why it works. Another might reject a suggestion for a sound reason. Teachers can ask for that reason and assess it against the subject criteria. Accepting the machine’s advice should never become the hidden objective.

Tai and colleagues describe evaluative judgement in a conceptual paper from higher education (Tai et al., 2018). Their account was written before ChatGPT. It is about judging the quality of one’s own work and of other people’s work. It gives feedback a useful purpose beyond correction. Students slowly learn to see what makes an answer convincing.

For example, a teacher could show two possible comments on the same passage. Students would decide which comment deals with the real weakness and explain their choice. The activity would assess reading and judgement, with no need for personal accounts. Teachers would still check the comments before the lesson. Students would judge the generated material, alongside other classroom examples.

In Chapter 4, I called this teacher-design burden. It is the effort a teacher must put in before a tool helps learning, as distinct from simply having access to the tool. None of this chapter’s studies uses the term. Here it includes choosing criteria, checking suggestions and planning the response to feedback.

The burden also belongs to redesign. A short oral check takes preparation and class time. Sharing criteria takes discussion, and comparing judgements requires colleagues’ attention. The Toronto report’s accounts of demanding coaching make that work visible (C21 Canada, 2025, p. 5). The evidence in this chapter does not show how much teacher time AI marking saves.

Schools should judge a marking trial by the whole cycle. Count preparation, reading, checking, correction and the time students need to act. Then ask whether teachers can make better decisions about learning. Faster output earns its place only when that wider account makes sense.

In practice

These worked illustrations apply the distinctions above. Teachers can adapt them.

Primary fractions with evidence in the room. Imagine a teacher who wants to know whether students can explain equal parts. She begins with paper shapes that students divide and compare. Children then mark a shape showing a half and explain their choice. The teacher listens for the idea of equal size.

For the assessment, she changes the shape and includes an unequal division. Students choose a representation and explain why another fails. One child might point while speaking, and another might draw an explanation. The teacher judges understanding of equal parts across these responses. Neat colouring contributes no evidence to that particular claim.

A worksheet completed at home could still provide practice. The teacher would check its learning in class, as Chapter 5 proposed. If she used AI to suggest diagrams or questions, she would inspect every example before use. Students would need no chatbot account.

Primary writing with the purpose clear. Consider a class writing instructions for planting seeds. The learning intention concerns sequence and enough detail for someone else to act. Students first arrange pictures, talk through the order and write a short set of instructions. A partner follows them and identifies a missing step.

The teacher could collect the initial sequence and the revised instructions, using Chapter 5’s ‘Drafts in view’. Her assessment would focus on how the revision helps a reader. A short discussion about the changed step could strengthen that judgement. Spelling support would follow the class’s agreed arrangements. If spelling itself became the assessment focus, the permitted help would change.

Secondary history: an open project with a protected check. Imagine students weighing different explanations for a historical decision. The teacher gives everyone the same small set of sources. Students build an argument and keep one short note explaining why they chose each source. Approved tools might help with preparation, under clear rules.

Before marking independent understanding, the teacher gives a new extract in class. Each student explains whether it strengthens or weakens the argument. The teacher then compares the explanation with the submitted project. Agreement supports a broader claim than either piece could support alone. A mismatch prompts a further learning check.

The rubric marks the reading of evidence apart from the way the final argument is presented. Teachers would discuss sample answers together before giving marks that count. The task would still let a student disagree with a model answer. Reasons and evidence would decide how good the disagreement is.

Secondary science with a decision to defend. A class could investigate ways to reduce water use at school. Students would work from teacher-approved observations or a shared dataset. Their proposals would identify an action and explain why the evidence supports it. A generated proposal could enter the lesson as material for criticism.

Each student would then respond to a changed constraint, such as a smaller budget. The teacher would assess how they adjust their reasoning. An attractive proposal would remain part of the evidence, alongside the decision and explanation. Group work would include a short individual response so that participation alone did not stand for understanding.

Secondary mathematics with partial understanding visible. Consider a teacher assessing proportional reasoning. Students might choose between two methods for comparing prices and explain which one fits the problem. One method could give the correct answer by accident, because of the particular numbers. The teacher would then change those numbers and ask students to think again.

A correct total would remain useful evidence. Explaining why a method works would support a broader claim. The teacher could mark the calculation and reasoning separately, recognising a sound approach despite an arithmetic slip. An AI suggestion might help prepare contrasting methods. She would check both carefully and retain the worked reasoning herself.

If students had practised with help at home, the classroom task would give a fresh view of independent application. They could then compare their approach with the earlier support. The assessment would serve learning by showing where a familiar procedure stops working. It would also avoid treating every wrong answer as the same kind of difficulty.

Feedback with a next step. A secondary English teacher could read an argument and identify its main learning need before consulting an approved tool. She might ask for possible comments on an invented paragraph with the same problem. After checking them, she would give students one manageable revision task.

In the next lesson, students would explain what they changed and why. Their responses would guide the teacher’s next decision. She would check the revised passage herself before recording progress. A comment that sounds helpful would earn no credit as evidence of improvement until the work changes.

Handle a concern without a verdict. Suppose a student submits work that differs sharply from their usual writing. The teacher could describe the difference calmly and ask about the task’s development. Any permitted help, access arrangement or drafting evidence would form part of the discussion. The student would have room to explain.

A short, similar task in the subject might show what the student understands now. Its result would guide teaching. The school would handle any conduct concern through its own separate process. Teachers would not claim that hesitation proves AI use. Where the evidence is still uncertain, the record should keep that uncertainty.

In a university course. Pair a written assignment with a short viva of three questions about the student’s own work, and give the viva real weight in the mark. The zoologist earlier in this chapter moved marks to quizzes and short vivas for exactly this reason (Ilyas, 2026, Participant M06). Another lecturer in my study goes further and asks students to perform what they have read, an approach he calls ‘text to stage’ (Ilyas, 2026, Participant M11).

When resources are thin

Plans to change assessment can quietly assume that every student drafts online and keeps a version history. They also assume that each student owns a working device. Many ordinary classrooms do not fit. These are the infrastructural prerequisites described in Chapter 4.

The prerequisites differ between methods. A cloud history needs reliable access and a suitable account. On-screen marking needs functioning equipment and technical support. A brief oral check needs teacher time, an accessible response format and a class that can work while the teacher listens. Paper reduces some demands while leaving others intact.

In my doctoral research, a university teacher in Pakistan described a computer used for entering examination grades (Ilyas, 2026, p. 163, Participant M02). The account concerns university record keeping. It does not describe school assessment design. Still, it cautions against assuming that a digital system already exists because teachers must submit marks.

School evidence from Pakistan raises a deeper issue. Rind and Mari studied how external examinations shaped English teaching in Sindh (Rind & Mari, 2019). Their pre-AI research described students memorising notes and teachers keeping examination demands closely in view. Moving an answer into a supervised room would leave that learning problem unresolved.

The study also shows why teachers cannot freely redesign assessment on their own. Classroom work must fit the examination board’s expectations, which students must meet in the end. A teacher can add explanation and varied practice during lessons. Changing the final examination needs action beyond the classroom. School reform should take both levels into account.

Large classes limit contact too. Ahmad and colleagues ran a descriptive survey in Nankana Sahib. It reported that teachers found it hard to assess students in very crowded secondary classrooms (Ahmad et al., 2018, p. 212). Parts of its tables and text disagree, so I leave the detailed percentages aside. The account still supports taking teachers’ capacity seriously.

In such conditions, I would begin with brief evidence from everyone and closer attention to a rotating group. All students could answer a short question on paper. While the class tackles the next task, the teacher could discuss selected responses. Across lessons, the rotation would give each student planned contact.

That proposal needs honest limits. A rotating conversation cannot confirm that every student understood every lesson. Teachers would use written answers to spot patterns and to choose where more checking is needed. A grade that matters a lot for one student may need a fuller observation. Schools should not promise more certainty than the timetable can give.

  • One shared device could still support preparation. A teacher might download or draft questions while connected, check them and copy the selected task onto the board. Students could respond in notebooks, with no online submission. A paper folder could hold an initial idea, a revised explanation and the teacher’s brief observation.

  • With no connection, the same sequence survives. Students explain evidence, respond to changed questions and demonstrate practical skills. Colleagues can compare a few anonymised scripts during a meeting. The method relies on clear intentions and shared judgement. AI may help prepare materials, but its absence need not remove the assessment principle.

Ghana’s short-answer preprint brings another low-resource perspective. Its researchers warn that individual schools would struggle to implement LLM marking without support (Henkel et al., n.d., p. 22). A promising benchmark does not provide the staff, funding or technical arrangements for everyday use. Adoption needs a system around the model.

India’s examination arrangements make some prerequisites explicit. CBSE’s circular on on-screen marking requires reliable connectivity and uninterrupted power at marking centres (Central Board of Secondary Education, 2026b). The circular concerns displaying and marking scripts on computers. It does not say that AI awards the marks or that students take the examination online.

A shared device also raises a fairness question inside an open task. Some students may have private access to a stronger model after school. Others may rely entirely on the example the teacher supplies. Asking everyone to produce a polished digital project would then combine learning with unequal resources. The teacher needs to know which differences the grade should represent.

I would offer a common set of materials whenever access could shape the result. Students could critique the same generated answer or develop proposals from the same observations. Additional tools might remain optional where they do not change the intended claim. If tool use itself forms the assessment, the school must provide a fair opportunity to learn and demonstrate it.

Those distinctions matter when schools spend money. Scanning papers may improve how scripts reach markers while leaving the questions unchanged. Buying a detector may produce reports while leaving understanding uncertain. A school needs to name the assessment problem before it buys equipment.

My first priority would be question design and manageable chances to explain. Teachers could change a familiar example, ask for a reason or compare two possible answers. Such changes still need skill and time. Their advantage is that students can take part without owning devices.

Records of assessment can carry that precision into talks with families. A teacher might explain that a student writes well with support but still needs practice in choosing evidence alone. Both statements give credit for real achievement. Neither needs anyone to pretend that one performance tells the whole story. A useful report makes the next step in learning easy to understand.

I would also review the arrangement after a short period of classroom use. Ask which students could show their understanding, which needed another route and which checks changed teaching decisions. Remove a record that nobody uses. Keep the contact that reveals something worth acting on. Redesign should remain open to revision itself.

Where this chapter leaves us

The theme of knowing now takes a sharper form. A fluent product alone cannot tell us who understands its argument, can use its ideas or can judge its errors. Teachers need evidence that connects those abilities to the learner. The evidence may include writing, talk, practical work and supported use of tools.

Chapter 12 will ask what knowing itself means when machines produce fluent answers. Before then, assessment raises a question about fairness. Who gets the support, time and access to demonstrate what they know? That is the question for Chapter 7: who gains from AI, and who gets left behind?

IFDC also includes a level about feeling safe to try (Ilyas et al., 2026b). In my study, teachers with lower digital competence described assessment as a weak point, while those with higher competence treated it as a design problem to solve (Ilyas, 2026, p. 216). Schools can help teachers move from the first view to the second. They can give teachers time and permission to redesign tasks, not only rules to enforce.

Questions for reflection

Take these questions to a phase or subject meeting, with one recent assessment in front of you.

  1. What claim about understanding does its mark allow you to make, and which assumption about help supports that claim?

  2. Which brief classroom demonstration could strengthen the evidence without consuming another lesson?

  3. How would your response differ between uncertain understanding and proven misconduct?

  4. Could a student using an agreed access arrangement demonstrate the same skill through your proposed check?

  5. If you tried AI marking, what evidence would show whether its suggestions improved your decisions about learning?
