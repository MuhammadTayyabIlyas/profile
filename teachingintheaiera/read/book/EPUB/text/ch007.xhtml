Chapter 5. Learning When Answers Are Free

Near the end of our interview, I asked the lecturer in my study who, as Chapter 4 describes, drafted her exam with ChatGPT, what future teachers should learn. Her answer began at home.

‘Take ChatGPT as an example: my son searches for something on it, and I tell him to type it out so he can learn spelling’ (Ilyas, 2026, Participant L4).

Type it out. Three words that hold this chapter’s worry. Some effort is not a cost on the way to learning. It is the learning.

A lecturer in education reached the same point in her own classes. After trying ChatGPT herself, she found it ‘great for generating ideas’, yet students who leaned on it wrote work that ‘lacks logical flow’. So she told them the tools could spark new views, as long as they did not depend on them completely (Ilyas, 2026, Participant M03).

Neither teacher banned the tool. Both tried to protect the part of the work that does the teaching. Researchers in the United States have tested what happens when the tool itself tries to protect that work.

Between 2024 and 2026, students who were behind in maths in 18 middle schools in Hamilton County, Tennessee, tried something new. The district already ran daily catch-up sessions for them, in small groups of 25 to 40 minutes. In each school, researchers picked one or two year groups at random to spend that time on Khan Academy with its AI tutor, Khanmigo (Oreopoulos & Low, 2026, p. 3). Khan Academy designed Khanmigo to coach rather than tell. It withholds answers, asks guiding questions and, in the researchers’ words, ‘sits one click away’ as students work through each problem.

The researchers published short exchanges from the tutor’s logs, paraphrased to protect the students (p. 21). In one, a student types ‘whats the answer’. Khanmigo replies with a question about the rectangle’s rows and columns. The student writes back, ‘just give me it I already got it wrong so theres no point doing it again’. In another exchange, a student asks whether the answer is 60. Khanmigo confirms it and asks how the student worked it out. The student explains that the other angles were all bigger than 90, ‘so those couldnt be it so it had to be 60’.

That second kind of exchange was rare. Nearly every student, 96%, sent Khanmigo at least one message. Yet the median student sent no message on two-thirds of the days they practised. The median student also messaged the tutor in only 17% of the exercise sessions in which they had made a mistake (Oreopoulos & Low, 2026, p. 4). Just 14.5% of their messages during maths exercises contained a maths question or a step of reasoning. The researchers add a fair caveat. A short numerical reply is what a Socratic tutor asks for, so some brief messages show compliance more than avoidance (p. 22). Students still rarely raised their own confusion. What matters for a teacher is how seldom students turned to it after a mistake, which is the moment the tutor was built for.

The programme did some good. Students practised about 30 extra minutes a week, and their maths scores rose by roughly 0.06 to 0.08 standard deviations over a school year. Those gains resemble what the same platform produces without an AI tutor. At about $15 a student a year, the programme was also cheap (p. 5). ‘Nothing in the achievement data requires the AI tutor to explain it,’ the authors conclude (p. 4). I take the gain to have come from the extra practice time, not from the tutor. Protected practice on the platform may give similar results, and an AI tutor adds little if students do not talk to it.

What stays with me is the student who typed ‘just give me it’. A tool can be built to hold back answers, but it cannot make a student want to think. That still takes a teacher, a reason to care about the problem and a classroom where being stuck is normal.

So a tutor that held answers back went largely unasked. The tools students reach for most often do the opposite. In a 2026 survey of American teenagers, 63% of those who used AI for schoolwork said they got answers from it (Zimmermann et al., 2026, p. 3). For any student with a phone and a connection, answers now cost nothing.

This chapter asks a question that every teacher now faces. How do we protect thinking when answers cost nothing?

First, doing a task with AI differs from learning it. Practice scores can rise while what a student can do alone stays flat or falls. Second, tools designed to hold back answers can help, but only when students engage with them, and people build that engagement. Third, students judge their own learning poorly, especially when an answer came easily. So teachers need ways to check what students know, as well as what they hand in.

That third point opens a thread that runs through the rest of the book. I call it knowing, the difference between having an answer and being able to explain, use and defend it without the machine. Chapter 6 takes the thread into assessment, and Chapter 12 gives it full attention. Most of the evidence here comes from secondary schools and universities, and from maths, programming and writing. I say where a finding comes from adults or rests on a single study.

Doing is not learning

Psychologists separated performance from learning long before chatbots arrived. Performance is what a learner does during practice. Learning is the lasting change that shows up later, on another day or in a new task. After reviewing decades of studies, Nicholas Soderstrom and Robert Bjork concluded that the two often come apart (Soderstrom & Bjork, 2015). ‘Performance during acquisition is a highly imperfect index of long-term learning,’ they write (p. 188). The conditions that produce the most errors in practice ‘are often the very conditions that produce the most learning’ (p. 176).

A classic experiment shows the gap. Henry Roediger and Jeffrey Karpicke had students read a prose passage, then either reread it or write down all they could recall, with no feedback (Roediger & Karpicke, 2006). Five minutes later, rereading looked better, with 81% of ideas recalled against 75%. Two days later, the tested students recalled 68% against 54%, and after a week 56% against 42% (p. 251). Rereading also made students more confident that they would remember, ‘even though repeated-study subjects actually showed much poorer retention on delayed tests’ (p. 254).

Producing an answer, even a small one, also helps memory. A meta-analysis of 86 studies found that people remember material they generate better than material they only read, by about 0.40 standard deviations (Bertsch et al., 2007, p. 201). On tests given more than a day later, the advantage grew to 0.64 (p. 206).

Elizabeth and Robert Bjork draw the practical lesson, and they address learners directly. Looking up an answer you could have worked out yourself, they warn, means that ‘you rob yourself of a powerful learning opportunity’ (Bjork & Bjork, 2011, p. 61). The Bjorks call helpful struggles of this kind desirable difficulties. Their examples include spacing study sessions, mixing topics and using tests as study events (p. 58). Difficulty helps only when learners can meet it. Learners who lack the background knowledge or skills to respond meet ‘undesirable difficulties’ instead (p. 58).

That warning sits at the centre of a long argument about guidance. Paul Kirschner, John Sweller and Richard Clark argued that minimal guidance fails novices, because learning means changing what long-term memory holds (Kirschner et al., 2006). ‘If nothing has changed in long-term memory, nothing has been learned,’ they wrote (p. 77). Cindy Hmelo-Silver and her colleagues replied that good problem-based and inquiry learning includes heavy scaffolding (Hmelo-Silver et al., 2007). Teachers guide students through ‘coaching, task structuring, and hints, without explicitly giving students the final answers’ (p. 100).

Manu Kapur’s work on productive failure adds a third view. In one study, 75 ninth-grade students in India learned about standard deviation, either by struggling with a problem before instruction or by receiving instruction first (Kapur, 2014, p. 1010). The students who struggled first understood the concept better and transferred it better, with equal skill at the procedure (p. 1013). A later meta-analysis of 166 comparisons found a moderate advantage for problem solving before instruction, g = 0.36 (Sinha & Kapur, 2021, p. 761). The advantage grew when the design followed the principles of productive failure closely. This supports letting students try a problem before we teach the method, as long as the problem is designed for it. A hard task set with no plan behind it is not the same thing.

None of these studies tested AI. Together, though, they make a strong case, built over decades. Effort spent retrieving and producing answers builds memory, and struggle helps when someone has designed it with care. An answer handed over before the learner has tried skips that effort. Whether that matters depends on what the task was for.

What offloading costs

Handing thinking to a tool is old, and often wise. Psychologists call it cognitive offloading, using a physical action, such as writing a note, to cut the mental demands of a task (Risko & Gilbert, 2016, p. 676). A shopping list, a diary and a calculator all offload. Offloading, Evan Risko and Sam Gilbert note, helps people ‘achieve cognitive feats that would not otherwise be possible’ (p. 676).

The cost tends to show up later. In one study they review, drivers who followed turn-by-turn directions drove a set route better than drivers with no aid. Those drivers remembered less of the scenery, though, and drove the route worse a second time without help (p. 683). People also offload when they have no need to. In one memory task, participants wrote items down about 40% of the time even when they had to remember only two, and they wrongly judged that this helped (p. 681). The second finding concerns me most for classrooms. Students with a chatbot open may also use it when they do not need it, so I think the teacher, not each student alone, should sometimes decide when the tool stays closed.

Chapter 1 showed that each school system still decides when a calculator may stand in for a student’s own arithmetic. Timing matters there too. An early meta-analysis found that calculators in Grade 4 lessons weakened computation on calculator-free tests (Hembree & Dessart, 1986, as cited in Ellington, 2003). In the later school years, Ellington recommended them for tests of problem solving.

So the useful question is what a task is for. If the point is the product, such as a letter to parents translated into Urdu or a timetable for a school trip, offloading may be sensible. When the point is a change in the student, offloading defeats it. Most school tasks exist for the second reason. That is why free answers bite harder in classrooms than in offices.

None of this makes every answer harmful. John Sweller argued that some forms of problem solving ‘interfere with learning’, because a novice’s search for a solution uses up the working memory that learning needs (Sweller, 1988, p. 257). For novices, many studies found that ‘properly designed worked examples are often a better instructional alternative than conventional problem-solving techniques’ (Kalyuga et al., 2003, p. 26). A worked example is an answer with its reasoning shown, offered so that a student can study it. Trouble starts when an answer replaces the thinking that a task exists to produce, and nobody notices.

What happens when students get the answer

The clearest school test of the worry comes from Turkey. Hamsa Bastani and colleagues worked with nearly 1,000 high school students in about fifty maths classes, over four sessions of 90 minutes (Bastani et al., 2025). In each session, a teacher first reviewed a topic at the board. Students then practised problems, and finally sat a closed-book exam on similar problems.

Some classes practised with a plain GPT-4 chatbot, which the researchers called GPT Base. Others used GPT Tutor, which held teacher-designed hints for each problem and instructions not to give the answer away. A control group had only textbooks and notes.

With AI at hand, practice scores rose sharply, by 48% with GPT Base and by 127% with GPT Tutor. On the closed-book exam, the GPT Base group scored 17% below the control group. The GPT Tutor group scored about the same as the control group (Bastani et al., 2025). According to the researchers, ‘students are simply copying answers from GPT Base’ (Bastani et al., 2024, p. 14). The plain chatbot was not even reliable. It gave a correct answer only 51% of the time (Bastani et al., 2025).

This is one study, in one country, with an exam taken in the same session as the practice. The study shows that a plain chatbot can lower what students manage alone soon afterwards. It also shows that a design built to withhold answers can prevent that loss.

A study of young programmers shows the same gap without the harm. Majeed Kazemitabaar and colleagues taught Python to 69 novices aged 10 to 17, half of them with an AI code generator (Kazemitabaar et al., 2023). During training, the AI group’s code scored 80.1% for correctness, against 44.4% for the others (p. 9). Learners submitted the AI’s code without any change on 49% of tasks (p. 10). The next day, working without AI, the two groups scored about the same, 61.3% against 62.9% (p. 11). A week later the AI group did slightly better, though the difference fell short of statistical significance. These learners also had to modify code after each task, and the authors suspect they learned while doing so (p. 13). A teacher could try the same idea: when students use AI output, ask them to adapt it to a new case before it counts.

In two laboratory experiments, participants learned basic Python for 45 minutes, some with access to an AI chatbot. Access made no difference to overall learning (Lehmann et al., 2025, p. 1). The same paper reports that AI helped students who already knew more and harmed those who knew less (p. 2). It is a preprint, and its tests came straight after the lesson. An average of no difference can hide some students who gained and others who lost.

Other studies point the same way on depth. In one, 91 university students researched nanoparticles in sunscreen with either ChatGPT or Google (Stadler et al., 2024). The ChatGPT group reported less mental effort and wrote weaker justifications for their recommendations (p. 1). In another study of 117 university students, those who revised essays with ChatGPT improved their essays most but gained no more knowledge than other groups (Fan et al., 2025). The caution here is about what we mark. If we look only at the product, AI can raise the quality of work while knowledge does not grow.

An MIT preprint on essay writing adds a caution of a different kind. Among 54 adults, those who wrote with a chatbot ‘fell behind in their ability to quote from the essays they wrote just minutes prior’ (Kosmyna et al., 2025, p. 2). Only 18 people completed its final session. A commentary estimates that a design like this needs about 159 participants (Stanković et al., 2026, p. 2). The study measured brain activity and recall of one’s own essay, and it did not test learning.

Evidence from Pakistan is thinner still. In a three-wave survey of 494 university students in Pakistan, those who used ChatGPT more reported more procrastination and more memory loss (Abbas et al., 2024, p. 15). The authors say they ‘could not completely rule out the possibility of reciprocal relationships’ (p. 18). Surveys like this record what students report, and they cannot show what students learned.

Reviews of the field urge the same care. A Stanford team found over 800 papers on AI in schooling, of which only 20 offered strong causal evidence (Fesler et al., 2026, p. 2). Their count ran to October 2025 and found no high-quality causal study of American school students. The Tennessee trial has since begun to fill that gap. Gains from AI tools, they note, ‘do not always persist when students are later asked to perform independently’ (p. 18). The OECD puts the point plainly. ‘Successfully performing an educational task with GenAI does not automatically lead to learning’ (OECD, 2026, p. 32).

So the honest claim is narrow. With a plain chatbot, practice scores can rise while what students can do alone stays flat or falls. Most of these tests came minutes or a day after practice, and none followed students for a term. The evidence of harm is emerging, and much of it rests on a single school study.

When the tutor holds back

Bastani’s team also tested a remedy. GPT Tutor received problem-specific information from teachers, and its prompt told it to give hints without revealing the answer (Bastani et al., 2025). ‘You should in no circumstances provide the student with the full solution,’ the prompt read (Bastani et al., 2024, p. 22). That design removed the harm, though it produced no gain on the exam.

Other designed tutors did better. At Harvard, physics instructors built an AI tutor on research about how students learn. They wrote detailed step-by-step solutions into its prompts so that its explanations would be accurate (Kestin et al., 2025, p. 5). In a randomised trial with 194 students, the median learning gain with the AI tutor was more than double the gain from an active-learning class (p. 2). The median student spent 49 minutes on the AI lesson. Its authors still warn that instructors ‘should avoid using AI in situations where students are likely to use it as a crutch to circumvent critical thinking’ (p. 6).

Not every designed tutor helps. In a randomised trial at a large US public university, 2,379 students in the courses of 30 instructors were offered a tutor built into their course. Those offered it finished with grades 0.37 standard deviations lower in matched sections of the same course, and they took part far less in the course’s online platform. The losses were larger for first-generation students (Liu et al., 2026). The paper is a working paper and it measures course grades rather than an independent test, so I treat it as a warning, not as a settled finding. An offer of help is not the same as a design that requires students to think.

Help from AI can work as well as a human tutor’s, if someone checks it. In one online study, 274 adults solved algebra or statistics problems with worked solutions from ChatGPT, help from human tutors, or none (Pardos & Bhandari, 2024). Learning gains were 17.00 percentage points with ChatGPT’s solutions, 11.62 with the human tutors’ help and 1.85 with none (p. 11). The two kinds of help did not differ significantly. Before the researchers checked it, though, ChatGPT’s help failed quality checks on 32% of problems (p. 1). This was an online study with adults, so I would not apply it directly to a school class. If a teacher hands out AI-written solutions, the teacher needs to check them before the students see them.

Some designed tutors now reach poorer schools. In Ghana, students in grades 3 to 8 used Rori, a maths tutor on WhatsApp (Henkel et al., 2024, p. 1). They had access to a phone for one hour a week. When a student struggles, Rori gives a hint first and then a worked solution (p. 2). Over eight months, the tutor group’s growth in maths beat the control group’s by an effect size of 0.36 (pp. 3, 6). The authors work for the organisation that built Rori, and 160 of the 637 students who sat the first test missed the last one (p. 5). They also caution that Rori may be effective only with ‘relatively easier math topics’ (p. 8).

In Benin City, Nigeria, first-year senior secondary students used GPT-4 in an after-school English programme for six weeks (De Simone et al., 2025, p. 2). Teachers guided them through twelve 90-minute sessions in computer labs. Their scores on the final assessment rose by 0.31 standard deviations, and by 0.24 on its English section (p. 2). The team ‘carefully crafted’ lesson guides ‘to position the LLM as a tutor, focusing on facilitating learning rather than simply providing direct answers’ (p. 7). Teachers took a three-day training programme first. I notice how much human support surrounded the tool: trained teachers, set sessions and written lesson guides. It does not show that students would gain the same from GPT-4 on their own.

A trial in five UK secondary schools kept a human in the loop. Google and the maths platform Eedi tested an AI model that drafted tutoring messages, which expert tutors checked before sending (LearnLM Team, Google, & Eedi, 2025, p. 1). The tutors approved 76.4% of its drafts with no edit or a tiny one. Students it supported solved 66.2% of new problems on later topics, against 60.7% for students with human tutors alone. That gap’s uncertainty range included zero, so chance could explain it (p. 5). The trial was exploratory, with 165 students in Years 9 and 10 (ages thirteen to fifteen), and its authors work for Google and Eedi.

One set of experiments with adults complicates the story about effort. Benjamin Lira and colleagues had over 2,000 people practise writing cover letters, some with an AI writing tool (Lira et al., 2026). The AI group put in less effort, yet wrote better letters afterwards without AI, by d = 0.38 (p. 3). That advantage held a day later (p. 4). In a later experiment, people who only studied an AI-revised letter improved as much as those who practised with the tool (p. 6). The authors argue that AI helped by giving learners strong, personal examples to learn from (p. 7). They also note a limit. In maths or programming, ‘the final answer ... does not often reveal the process that produced it’ (p. 7). The paper is a preprint, and one author works at Microsoft Research.

Even real gains can fade. In India, a computer-assisted maths programme raised scores by 0.35 and then 0.47 standard deviations over two years (Banerjee et al., 2007, p. 1252). One year after it ended, the gain had shrunk to about 0.10 (p. 1238). A Stanford brief concludes that AI-led tutoring software ‘does not yet meet the established evidence base or definition of high-impact tutoring’ (Turano et al., 2026, p. 1).

Every success in this section came with design work done before a student logged in. Teachers supplied hints, instructors wrote solutions, a team wrote lesson guides and researchers screened the AI’s help. This is the teacher-design burden from Chapter 4. With learning tools, much of that burden falls on whoever builds the tutor, and in many schools that is the teacher. The evidence for designed tutors is promising and still emerging. Most of the studies are small or short, and several come from authors linked to the tool’s maker.

The feeling of knowing

Students cannot easily tell when learning is happening. In a Harvard physics study without any AI, students learned more in active-learning classes than in lectures (Deslauriers et al., 2019). They scored 0.46 standard deviations higher on a test, yet reported a feeling of learning 0.56 standard deviations lower (p. 19253). The effort that taught them also made them feel they had learned less.

Psychology finds the same mismatch elsewhere. In one experiment on learning painters’ styles, 78% of participants did better with spaced study, but 78% judged massed study as good or better (Kornell & Bjork, 2008, p. 588). The authors traced the error to ‘a sense of fluency’ as examples of one style came one after another (p. 591). People also believe they understand everyday devices until they try to explain how they work. Rozenblit and Keil called this ‘a genuine miscalibration in people’s sense of how well they understand the workings of the world around them’ (Rozenblit & Keil, 2002, p. 535).

AI tools can make the mismatch worse. In the Turkish study, students who used the plain chatbot did not think they had learned less. Those with GPT Tutor believed they had done significantly better on the exam, though they had not (Bastani et al., 2025). In another study, 246 adults solved logic problems from a law school admissions test with ChatGPT’s help (Fernandes et al., 2026). They scored 12.98 out of 20 on average but estimated their score at 16.50 (p. 4). Among novice programmers, students who struggled with AI tools ‘thought they performed better than they did, and finished with an illusion of competence’ (Prather et al., 2024, p. 469).

Scientists face a version of the same risk. Lisa Messeri and Molly Crockett warn that AI could bring a phase of research ‘in which we produce more but understand less’ (Messeri & Crockett, 2024, p. 49). Schools face it with every homework task.

The illusion is not universal. Lira’s adult writers, who learned more with AI, reported learning about as much as those who practised without it (Lira et al., 2026, p. 4). The illusion seems to grow when the tool supplies the answer and the learner supplies little.

Many students sense the problem themselves. In a 2026 survey of American teenagers, 39% said they felt they were missing out on learning when they used AI to complete assignments (Zimmermann et al., 2026, p. 7). Two-thirds said AI helped them understand their schoolwork, ‘not just finish it faster’ (p. 7). One British undergraduate wrote simply, ‘I’m not using my brain at all’ (Stephenson & Armstrong, 2026, p. 22). An American student told a reporter that he often wonders whether he would have learned more by taking the time to learn, ‘instead of just finding it out’ (Walsh, 2025). These are survey answers and single quotes, so they show what students feel, not what they learned. Many students already notice the cost, which gives a teacher a starting point for an honest conversation with the class about AI use.

Teachers notice a related problem. In a Khanmigo pilot in Michigan, one teacher found that students ‘struggled to ask Khanmigo the correct question’. ‘They didn’t know what they didn’t know,’ the teacher said (Michigan Virtual, 2025). A geometry teacher in Indiana made the same point to a journalist. ‘If students don’t engage with the material enough to know what they’re looking for, then an AI like Khanmigo doesn’t necessarily help’ (Barnum, 2026).

This is the gap I called knowing at the start of the chapter. A student who knows can explain a step, spot a wrong answer and use the idea on a new problem with the machine closed. One who only holds the answer can do none of these. Good teachers have long checked for knowing, and free answers make that check more urgent. Chapter 6 asks what our assessments can still tell us about it, and Chapter 12 asks what knowing means when machines produce fluent answers.

Access is not engagement

Return to Tennessee. That trial removed nearly every barrier that usually keeps students away from learning software. The tutor was free, sessions were compulsory, a teacher was present, and Khanmigo even suggested questions to ask (Oreopoulos & Low, 2026, p. 4). Students still rarely asked it for help. ‘Seeking help with one’s own confusion remained a choice, and most students declined it most of the time,’ the authors write (p. 4). Between autumn 2025 and spring 2026, active students’ weekly practice rose from 42 to 48 minutes, while their weekly chats with the tutor stayed flat at about 2.6 (p. 19). When a student did start a conversation after a mistake, it ran to a median of three messages. The authors wonder whether ‘human attention is the key ingredient’ for personalised learning (p. 34).

Low use is common. A Stanford brief describes a study of 181,000 students on a maths platform. Only 5% reached the recommended 30 minutes a week, and 41% never logged on (Turano et al., 2026, p. 5). Teacher, school and district factors explained 57% of the differences in use. The figures come from a 2022 study of Khan Academy’s MAP Accelerator platform by the company’s own researchers, which the brief summarises (p. 6), so they describe one vendor’s product.

Tutoring research shows why regular time matters. Programmes that met four or five days a week averaged 0.35 standard deviations, against 0.10 for those meeting once or twice (Nickow et al., 2024, p. 86). In Michigan’s two Khanmigo pilots, with 43 teachers and nearly 1,800 students, teachers found the tool ‘worked better for older or more self-motivated students’ (Michigan Virtual, 2025).

India offers the strongest contrast. Every school in a trial among residential government middle schools in Uttar Pradesh had access to Khan Academy. Randomly chosen schools also received a lab-in-charge whose sole job was to make practice happen (Oreopoulos et al., 2026, pp. 5, 8). These staff secured connections, protected practice time and supervised students at work. Weekly practice rose from 7.2 to 47.4 minutes, and maths scores rose by almost half a standard deviation over 31 weeks. The platform had no AI tutor in this trial. People and protected time did the work. One of the authors works for Khan Academy.

An older study makes the same point about teachers. With an experienced teacher, one geometry tutoring program raised scores by 0.96 standard deviations. In the hands of two teachers new to it, the effect was −0.23 (Kulik & Fletcher, 2016, p. 66). The new teachers treated the software as a replacement for themselves and graded papers while students worked. Their experienced colleague used the time to give students more individual help. This finding rests on a single evaluation from 1993, but the lesson seems sensible to me. When students work on a learning tool, I would use that time to move around the room and help individuals, not to mark. The tool gives the teacher time; it does not replace the teacher.

In Nigeria, average attendance was 72%, and gains rose with each extra day a student attended (De Simone et al., 2025, p. 3). Khan Academy has drawn its own lesson. It has built Khanmigo directly into practice problems because, a spokesperson said, ‘students were not seeking out Khanmigo’s help as much as we had hoped’ (Barnum, 2026). Sal Khan, the founder of Khan Academy, now says that ‘our biggest lever is really investing in the human systems’.

A tool that holds answers back helps only the students who engage with it. Engagement depends on people. Teachers model good questions, schools protect time, and routines make asking for help normal. Without them, even a well-designed tutor sits one click away and unused.

Settings that hold back answers

Some governments now ask for friction by design. England’s Department for Education expects AI products for learners ‘not to provide final answers, full solutions, or complete worked examples by default’ (Department for Education, 2026a). Products should ‘only show a full solution after a genuine learner attempt’. They should ‘create friction, or require teacher approval’ before students switch to modes where full answers come easily. Suppliers should also ‘track and report when learners offload thinking to the system’. The standards give examples, from clicking to reveal a full solution to pasting text into an answer box in place of a student’s own response. They allow exceptions only in specific cases, such as reviewing prior knowledge. These are expectations for suppliers, and nobody has yet tested whether they protect learning.

Several AI companies now offer study or learning modes that ask questions before they give answers. Independent checks so far give a mixed picture. Common Sense Media found that Gemini’s Guided Learning mode ‘shows promise but has significant limitations’ (Common Sense Media, 2026, p. 2). Students ‘can easily switch back to regular mode at any time’, and the tool ‘will complete homework assignments and essays’ (p. 2).

Friction has a cost of its own. The journalist who interviewed the Indiana teacher reports that her students found Khanmigo frustrating, because it sometimes made mistakes yet would not give away the answer (Barnum, 2026). By his account, she no longer uses it and sees more students using AI just to find answers. A tool that withholds answers can lose its students to tools that do not.

Most students never meet a learning mode at all. In a December 2025 survey of American students, chatbots were the most common kind of AI tool, and 53% used ChatGPT (Schwartz & Diliberti, 2026, p. 5). General chatbots give answers by default.

Any default protects thinking only while it stays on. A company sets it, and a student can often switch it off with one click. The classroom has its own default, which is the order of work a teacher sets. Teachers cannot control every app a student opens at home. They can decide what students do before any tool appears in a lesson, and what students must show without it afterwards.

Homework and the check of knowing

Homework is where free answers matter most, because students do it alone. In a RAND panel of American students, the share using AI for homework help rose from 48% in May 2025 to 62% in December 2025 (Schwartz & Diliberti, 2026, p. 1). Students draw their own line. Among those who used AI to get homework answers, 45% called it cheating. Of those who used it for better explanations of assignments, only 5% did (p. 9). Two-thirds agreed that the more students use AI for schoolwork, the more it will harm their critical thinking (p. 1).

Heavy reliance is still a minority habit. One in ten American teenagers told Pew that they do all or most of their schoolwork with a chatbot’s help (McClain et al., 2026, p. 6). Another 45% had not used a chatbot for schoolwork at all.

What students do with an answer varies. Of American teens who use AI, 35% said they get an answer and fix or improve it from what they know, and 31% rewrite it to sound like them. A quarter use the answer as it is (Zimmermann et al., 2026, p. 3). Rules lag behind practice. Only 44% of the teens surveyed said a teacher had ever discussed when they should and should not use AI for schoolwork (p. 8). In the RAND panel, about a third of students said their school had a schoolwide rule (Schwartz & Diliberti, 2026, p. 9).

England’s guidance suggests that schools ‘may wish to review homework policies, and other types of unsupervised study’ (Department for Education, 2025b). The research in this chapter points to what such a review should protect. Homework that students can hand to a machine no longer shows a teacher what they know. It also stops giving them the retrieval practice that builds memory. Dunlosky and colleagues rated practice testing as one of only two study methods of high utility among those they reviewed (Dunlosky et al., 2013, p. 5).

The university teachers in my thesis met this problem early, and some checked for knowing directly. ‘Then sometimes we ask them questions from their own submissions to ensure they actually understand what they wrote,’ said the computer scientist in my study (Ilyas, 2026, p. 188, Participant H01). The historian asks to see work in progress. ‘I usually tell students not to submit the final version right away; they should come show me a draft first’ (Ilyas, 2026, p. 188, Participant H02). The historian also wants students to ‘exercise their own thinking and research skills’ and not ‘rely blindly’ on tools such as ChatGPT (Ilyas, 2026, p. 174, Participant H02). The computer scientist puts foundations before tools. ‘We advise students to learn the foundational concepts from us or in class first,’ that teacher explained (Ilyas, 2026, p. 185, Participant H01).

Not every teacher felt so sure. As Chapter 1 describes, the botanist in my study saw an ‘authority crisis’ for teachers (Ilyas, 2026, p. 167, Participant L5). In my thesis, teachers with lower digital competence tended to answer AI with ‘surveillance and prohibition’ (Ilyas, 2026, p. 212). Teachers with higher competence ‘repositioned their role from information-provider to critical-thinking facilitator’ (p. 212). These are teachers’ own accounts from universities in Pakistan, and the thesis did not measure what their students learned.

Younger students raise a different case. UNESCO’s guidance says that the minimum age for independent conversations with generative AI platforms ‘should be 13 years of age’ (Miao & Holmes, 2023, p. 21). For primary teachers, the issue in this chapter is less about chatbots in lessons. It concerns homework done with help at home, and the habits students carry into secondary school.

The practical response is to move part of the check into class. A short quiz without devices, a question about one line of a student’s own essay or a draft discussed before the final version all show what a student knows. Homework can then return to its older job, which is practice, with understanding checked later in person.

What to protect

Taken together, the evidence suggests a simple order of work. The student tries first, even badly. Help comes next, as a hint from a tool, a teacher or a classmate. Later, the student shows without help what they can do. That order keeps the retrieval and generation that build memory, and it still lets students gain from good explanations and examples. The OECD reaches a similar view. ‘Acquiring and demonstrating foundational knowledge and skills in key subjects without the use of general-purpose GenAI remains key,’ it advises (OECD, 2026, p. 32).

Figure 5.1. The order of work that protects thinking when answers are free: the student tries first, help comes next, and later the student shows without help what they can do.

Three things in that order deserve protection. The attempt makes a student generate something, which the research on generation and productive failure rewards. Retrieval without help, days later, is what turns practice into memory. Explanation, aloud or on paper, is where a teacher sees knowing and a student discovers its gaps.

The order does not depend on any particular tool. A teacher can apply it to a chatbot, a tutor, the answer pages of a textbook or an older sibling. What it does depend on is design and time. Someone has to plan the attempt, choose the hint and make room for the check, and schools decide whether teachers get the time to do it.

That time is scarce. ‘The workload is large, so I can’t develop a detailed online resource that would eventually save time,’ the botanist said (Ilyas, 2026, p. 168, Participant L5). My thesis calls this a ‘cruel paradox’ (p. 168).

In practice

These routines turn the argument into classroom habits. They draw on the research above, though nobody has tested them as a package.

Attempt first, then ask. Before students open any AI tool, they write down a first step, a guess or a plan, even a wrong one. For example, a Year 9 maths teacher might ask for two attempted questions on paper before anyone uses the tutor for a hint. England’s standards ask the same of products (Department for Education, 2026a).

Hints, not answers. A teacher sets up the class’s AI tool to give one hint at a time and to answer a question with a question. GPT Tutor in Turkey and Rori in Ghana both worked this way (Bastani et al., 2025; Henkel et al., 2024). The teacher tries the setup as a student first, including a blunt request for the answer.

A quiz with the machine closed. Once a week, students answer five short questions from memory, with no devices and no marks recorded. The quiz shows students and teacher what has stuck. Deslauriers and colleagues advise testing ‘as early as possible so students can gauge their actual learning’ (Deslauriers et al., 2019, p. 19256).

Ask about their own work. The teacher picks one sentence or step from each submission and asks the student to explain it, in a minute or two at the desk. The computer scientist did this to make sure students ‘actually understand what they wrote’ (Ilyas, 2026, p. 188, Participant H01).

Drafts in view. Students show a draft before the final version, and the teacher comments on the thinking as well as the text (Ilyas, 2026, p. 188, Participant H02). Work that grows in view is harder to hand to a machine and easier to coach.

Teach it back. Students explain a step or an idea to a partner without notes, then swap roles. Trying to explain exposes the gaps that a feeling of understanding hides (Rozenblit & Keil, 2002).

Model the good question. Many students do not know what to ask (Michigan Virtual, 2025). A teacher works through a problem with a tutor in front of the class, thinking aloud about each question and hint. In the Tennessee trial, few messages contained a maths question or a step of reasoning (Oreopoulos & Low, 2026, p. 22). Ready-made prompts made it easier to ask something useful. Clicks on suggested prompts such as ‘Help me check my answer’ made up 24.2% of messages (pp. 19, 21), so a class list of good questions may do similar work.

Agree the line together. A class agrees which uses of AI help learning and which replace it, and the list goes on the wall. Students already separate explanations from answers (Schwartz & Diliberti, 2026, p. 9). A shared agreement gives that instinct a form.

Protect the practice time. If students use a learning tool, the timetable gives it a fixed slot and an adult supervises (Oreopoulos et al., 2026). Use depends heavily on what teachers and schools do (Turano et al., 2026, p. 5).

Name the feeling. Teachers tell students, in plain words, that effortful learning often feels worse even while it works better (Deslauriers et al., 2019). Students who know this are less likely to read struggle as failure.

In a university course. Allow AI for brainstorming, but ask students to hand in their own outline first, written in class or uploaded before the AI-assisted draft. Compare the two in a short tutorial. A lecturer in education in my study found AI ‘great for generating ideas’, but warned that work leaning on it ‘lacks logical flow’ (Ilyas, 2026, Participant M03).

When resources are thin

The designed tutors in this chapter all assumed a device and a connection. In Nigeria, the programme picked its schools for their computer labs (De Simone et al., 2025, p. 6). The Indian trial ‘required schools to possess basic digital infrastructure of tablets, internet access, and reasonably reliable electricity’ (Oreopoulos et al., 2026, p. 22). These are the infrastructural prerequisites of Chapter 4. Across the world, 94% of people in high-income countries use the internet, against 23% in low-income countries (International Telecommunication Union, 2025a, p. 2).

Many Pakistani students lack those prerequisites. In rural Pakistan, 23% of households have an internet connection and 61% have a smartphone. Only 12% of rural government schools have a computer lab, and 14% have internet (Idara-e-Taleem-o-Aagahi, 2024, p. 3). Phone sharing adds another barrier. Among women in Pakistan who use mobile internet, 28% do so only on another person’s handset, against 4% of men (GSMA, 2026, p. 18). Power cuts run three to four hours a day in cities and six to eight in rural areas (The News International, 2025, as cited in Ilyas, 2026, p. 17).

Teachers fill the gaps themselves. ‘If the internet isn’t working for them, I share my phone’s hotspot,’ the Islamic Studies lecturer in my study said (Ilyas, 2026, p. 194, Participant M04). Rori’s makers designed it for budget phones and WhatsApp, and students still needed access to a phone for an hour a week (Henkel et al., 2024, pp. 1, 7).

Pakistan is now trying the tutor from Tennessee. In March 2025, the federal education ministry agreed with Khan Academy to bring Khanmigo to 432 government schools (Geo News, 2025). The plan covers over 300,000 students and starts with middle schools in Islamabad. Khan Academy will train the teachers first. Before the agreement, a ministry official told Dawn that the service would be free in schools equipped with IT labs, and that about half of Islamabad’s schools had one (Dawn, 2025). I have found no published evaluation of the programme so far. The Tennessee and Indian trials suggest what any evaluation should watch. Do students ask the tutor anything, and who protects the time for them to do so?

Free answers are not new in Pakistani classrooms. As a teacher and later as an assistant education officer in Gujranwala, I saw the ‘key’ books sold beside textbooks in the bazaars. They print a model answer to every exercise in the textbook. Students copied them, and I often found the same answer, in the same words, across a whole row of exercise books. A chatbot is a key to every book at once, and it answers follow-up questions too.

So the question of free answers reaches students without devices too, through printed answer guides, older siblings and shared phones. The routines in this chapter need little more than paper and a teacher’s attention. Attempting first, quizzing from memory, explaining one’s own work and showing drafts all work with chalk. Chapter 7 returns to who gains from AI and who loses out.

IFDC has four levels, from basic conditions up to skilled choices (Ilyas et al., 2026b). Protecting thinking belongs to the top level, skilled choices. Here the choice is when students should work without help. This needs very little equipment, so it is one of the fairest places for schools with few resources to start.

Questions for reflection

Take these questions to your next department meeting, or into a quiet moment after school.

  1. Where in your subject does the struggle itself do the teaching, and where is it only a barrier?

  2. Think of a recent homework task. What would a student lose if a chatbot did it for them?

  3. How would you know, without a written answer, whether a student understands work they have handed in?

  4. At what point in your lessons should AI help appear, before the attempt, during it or after it?

  5. Which of your students would not know what to ask a tutor, and how could you teach them?

Framework connections

Protecting thinking draws on several DigCompEdu competences (Redecker, 2017). Deciding when to offer help is part of guidance and self-regulated learning. Checking what students know through quizzes, drafts and questions is part of assessment. Keeping students mentally active is part of engaging learners. Helping them judge whether an AI answer deserves trust is part of information and media literacy. UNESCO’s framework for teachers says training should discuss ‘the risks AI poses to student learning’, including weaker critical thinking (Miao & Cukurova, 2024, p. 33). At its second level, it asks teachers to study how AI affects ‘students’ agency, thinking and learning processes’ (p. 36).
