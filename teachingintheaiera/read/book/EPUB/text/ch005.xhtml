Chapter 3. The Part of Teaching No Machine Can Do

The oldest teaching aid anyone described to me was not a screen. It was a piece of broken pottery, thousands of years old.

‘Which tools do you mostly use?’ I asked a historian during my doctoral interviews.

‘If we talk about digital tools, I use them, but not extensively,’ he said. In his subjects, ‘tangible objects hold more significance’. So he brings real finds into the room: stones, shards of pottery and other artefacts collected during site exploration at places like Mohenjo-daro and Harappa. They are, in his words, ‘actual items from ancient civilizations’. He opens his laptop only when the real thing is not available (Ilyas, 2026, Participant H02).

A slide can show a pot. Deciding when a class needs to hold one is the teacher’s call.

An English lecturer in another department spoke about knowing students. ‘You have to address the psychology of a person under 22 who gets distracted by numerous things,’ she said. ‘You cannot simply expect the person to behave as you wish’ (Ilyas, 2026, Participant H08).

One answer is about judgement, the other about knowing the learner. Both describe work that stays with the teacher, whatever tools are in the room. Teachers in England recently made a similar point, when a machine offered to read their students’ writing for them.

In early 2024, twelve teachers from nine schools in England tried out an unusual assistant. A demonstration tool from a government project, it read samples of students’ typed writing and flagged errors in spelling, punctuation, grammar and vocabulary. It then set each child a follow-up task. Its makers had trained it only on Year 4 work, from students aged eight and nine, but its general format suited every age group (Doyle et al., 2025). The teachers could see the time it might save. Several could also see what it might cost.

One secondary teacher summed up the problem in seven words. ‘They want you to read their work.’ If students thought a machine had marked it, the teacher went on, ‘I think their investment in that is gone’ (Doyle et al., 2025, p. 1931). A student in the same study, aged between 13 and 17, made the point from the other side of the desk. ‘The artificial intelligence won’t ever really be able to match how a teacher has known you through the class’ (p. 1932).

The teacher and the student in that study state this chapter’s claim more plainly than any policy paper. Teaching rests on relationships, judgement, care and the example a teacher sets. Machines can now draft feedback, explain fractions and even sound kind. The student’s words still point to a difference between someone knowing you and something processing your work.

That claim deserves a real test, because the machines are better than many teachers expect. In 2023, researchers took 195 health questions from a public online forum, where a doctor had already replied to each. They put the same questions to ChatGPT and showed both answers to a panel of health professionals, without revealing which came from where. The panel preferred the chatbot’s answer in 78.6% of 585 ratings and judged far more of its answers empathetic, 45.1% against 4.6% (Ayers et al., 2023).

So the central question is sharper than it first looks. If a machine can sound more caring than a doctor, what remains essentially human in teaching? This chapter tests the usual answer, that relationships, judgement, care and modelling form the human core, against what AI can already do. The evidence turns out to be uneven. Some of it is strong, some is very new and some barely exists. The chapter ends with a professional identity that teachers can hold with confidence, because it does not rest on overstating the case.

What machines can already do

Start with the honest list, since many readers will know parts of it already. Tutoring has the longest record. A 2011 review compared human tutors with intelligent tutoring systems, computer programs that coached students through each step of a problem. Against no tutoring, human tutoring produced an effect size of 0.79, and the intelligent tutoring systems reached 0.76, almost as much (VanLehn, 2011). That review predates ChatGPT by more than a decade. Chapter 1 described a newer case, a purpose-built AI tutor that beat active learning in one university physics course (Kestin et al., 2025).

Researchers have tested physical machines too. A 2018 review found that social robots, working as tutors or classmates, reached outcomes similar to human tutoring on restricted tasks, largely thanks to their physical presence (Belpaeme et al., 2018). For early language learning, another review that year judged robots useful but less effective than human teachers (Kanero et al., 2018). Its authors added that nobody had yet tested language-tutoring robots rigorously. The phrase 'restricted tasks' matters here. Machines come closest to human tutors when the task is narrow and the steps are clear. That is also where a teacher can most safely let a machine take some of the practice, while keeping the open-ended work for the class.

Feedback comes next. A US team compared 200 comments from trained, experienced educators with 200 from ChatGPT, all on the same secondary-school essays. The educators did not know the students, and they had more time than most teachers do. They did slightly better on most measures of quality, including accuracy and clear next steps. ChatGPT did better at linking its comments to the marking criteria (Steiss et al., 2024). Well-trained educators, the authors concluded, still gave better feedback than the free version of ChatGPT available at the time. They saw a place for AI on early drafts, or where no trained educator is available.

Machines can also coach the people who teach. In a US trial, a tool called Tutor CoPilot suggested to more than 700 tutors, in real time, what an expert might say next. Their 1,000 students were 4 percentage points more likely to master a maths topic, and 9 points more likely when the tutor had a weaker rating (Wang et al., 2026). The tool cost about $20 per tutor per year. It nudged tutors towards probing questions and away from generic praise. Gains on end-of-year maths tests were not significant, and the study is still a preprint. For a school, the larger gain for tutors with a weaker rating points to a possible use as support for new or less experienced staff, not as a replacement for them.

The doctors’ study from the start of the chapter belongs on this list too. Therapy offers a similar result. In 2025, 830 people read replies to couples-therapy scenarios. They could rarely tell ChatGPT’s replies from a therapist’s, and they rated ChatGPT’s higher on key principles of therapy (Hatch et al., 2025). Both studies come with limits, and both tested one-off written replies with no relationship behind them. The chatbot’s answers in the Ayers study ran about four times longer than the doctors’, and length alone may have looked like empathy. Its raters were also members of the research team, and nobody checked the chatbot’s answers for accuracy. The authors noted that real doctors may answer on the basis of relationships with their patients, which the study did not capture (Ayers et al., 2023).

Taken together, these studies show that machines can now produce the outward form of several things teachers do. They explain, give feedback, coach and console. Most of the tests were short, though, and most involved strangers. None of the studies behind this chapter compares an AI tutor with a teacher who knows the class over a full school year. The Nigerian trial described in Chapter 1 lacked even a teacher-only comparison, as its own authors pointed out (De Simone et al., 2025).

Relationships

Relationships have the deepest research base of the four. A 2011 meta-analysis of 99 studies found that positive teacher–student relationships went with higher engagement (r = .34) and, more weakly, with higher achievement (r = .16) (Roorda et al., 2011). An update in 2017 pooled 189 studies covering 249,198 students. Positive relationships went with engagement, and engagement went with achievement. A rise of one standard deviation in relationship quality went with a rise of .14 standard deviations in achievement (Roorda et al., 2017).

One long study shows how early this begins. Researchers followed 179 children in the United States from kindergarten to the end of eighth grade. When kindergarten teachers reported conflict and dependency in their relationship with a child, that child tended to do worse on later grades, test scores and discipline. The pattern held when the researchers allowed for gender, ethnicity, ability and early behaviour. It was strongest for boys and for children who already showed behaviour problems in kindergarten (Hamre & Pianta, 2001). For a teacher, the practical point is to notice conflict and dependency with a young child early, and not to treat them as small matters.

These findings are consistent, yet they cannot show which causes which. Most of the studies in the 2017 update captured a single moment in time (Roorda et al., 2017). A review of 46 studies of adolescents concluded that the evidence, at least in secondary schools, was not yet enough to prove or disprove a causal link (Quin, 2017). Students who engage may draw warmth from their teachers as much as warmth draws them in.

Why might relationships matter for learning itself? The educational philosopher Gert Biesta offers one answer. Education, he argues, means that students ‘learn something, that they learn it for a reason, and that they learn it from someone’ (Biesta, 2015, p. 76). The someone is not decoration. Education, he adds, always involves questions of content, purpose and relationships.

Students put this in their own words. In workshops with 40 children aged 9 to 11 in Scotland, students in both schools talked about emotional intelligence and what AI might lack. As the children put it, ‘AI is not emotionally intelligent’ (Aitken et al., 2025, p. 20). These are students’ beliefs, not evidence about AI, but a teacher could use them as a starting point for a class discussion about what AI can and cannot offer.

Even young children sort their sources. Two US studies tested children aged 4 to 8 with a voice assistant and a human informant. As they grew older, children trusted the assistant more for facts and the person more for personal knowledge, such as details about the informant’s own friend (Girouard-Hallam & Danovitch, 2022). The children came mostly from White, upper-middle-class families, so the pattern may not hold everywhere. I link this to the student in the English study at the start of the chapter. Knowledge of a particular student is the part of a teacher's work that a machine has the least access to.

My own doctoral research adds a view from university teaching in Pakistan. The interviews concerned digital competence, not care, yet the value of sharing a room emerged as a theme. The computer scientist in my study said they caution students that ‘purely online learning misses out on domestic examples and interactive discussion, so attending class is still important’ (Ilyas, 2026, p. 183, Participant H01). Another argued for a hybrid model ‘because face-to-face interaction and social skills are also very important’ (Ilyas, 2026, p. 183, Participant M09).

Relationships run in both directions. The same computer scientist described learning from ‘talented students’ who suggest new tools ‘because they understand my style of teaching’ (Ilyas, 2026, p. 177, Participant H01). Students who know their teacher can help that teacher grow.

Relationships are also where AI now competes most directly for young people’s time. In a nationally representative survey of 1,060 US teenagers in 2025, 72% had used an AI companion and 52% used one regularly (Robb & Mann, 2025). Nearly a third found those conversations as satisfying as talking with real friends, or more so. Most still put people first, and 80% of users spent more time with real friends than with AI companions. A teacher does not need to approve or forbid these apps in order to ask about them. A calm question about which apps students use, and why, tells a teacher more than guessing. The tutor-group routine later in this chapter is one way to start.

The children with the fewest people to turn to seem the most drawn to chatbots. In a UK survey, 35% of children who used chatbots said it felt like talking to a friend, rising to 50% among vulnerable children. One in eight said they used chatbots because they had no one else to speak with, and nearly one in four vulnerable children said the same (Internet Matters, 2025). A teacher who notices a child sitting alone at break can do something a chatbot will not.

Judgement

Judgement is harder to see, because it happens inside a teacher’s head. Biesta calls it ‘central and essential’ to education, because teachers must keep deciding what matters most, not only in general but ‘in relation to each individual student’ (Biesta, 2015, p. 78). Every lesson involves many such decisions, and teachers make most of them in seconds.

Part of that judgement rests on a special kind of knowledge. Lee Shulman called it pedagogical content knowledge, the ‘ways of representing and formulating the subject that make it comprehensible to others’ (Shulman, 1986, p. 9). It also includes knowing the preconceptions that students of different ages and backgrounds bring to a topic. A chatbot can produce a dozen explanations of photosynthesis in a minute. Choosing the one that will untangle this class’s particular confusion is the teacher’s work.

Studies of expert teachers show this knowledge in action. The education researcher David Berliner reviewed several such studies. In one, expert health teachers often described how they would change a lesson while teaching it, based on what students were doing (Cleary & Groer, 1994, as cited in Berliner, 2001). Novices stayed close to their plans. In another, expert primary teachers predicted how their own students would rank on standardised tests. Their predictions correlated with the actual results at .74 in reading and .73 in mathematics. Student teachers in the same classrooms managed .51 and .54 (Mullholland, 1991, as cited in Berliner, 2001).

Judgement also covers what a comment will do to a particular child. In the English study from the opening, a teacher at a primary special school saw a risk. Asking a child to reflect on something they found particularly tricky, the teacher warned, ‘could actually trigger behaviour’ (Doyle et al., 2025, p. 1932). Knowing which student can take which comment, and on which day, is judgement of the most practical kind.

This kind of knowing grows from time with particular children, and teachers can also train it. In two year-long video clubs, mathematics teachers met monthly to watch clips from one another’s lessons. Over the year, their talk shifted from what the teacher did towards what students were thinking, and the change reached their own teaching (Sherin & van Es, 2009). Noticing, in other words, is a professional skill.

AI changes the conditions for judgement in one specific way. It hands teachers a ready-made answer before they have formed their own. A 2026 experiment with 1,339 school teachers in Greece tested what happens next. Each teacher reviewed the same student’s work beside a deliberately wrong mark, which the researchers presented either as a colleague’s mark or as an AI’s. When the wrong mark was too generous, teachers corrected it equally well under either label. If it was too harsh, the gap they left was 22% larger under the AI label (Goulas et al., 2026).

Deference was strongest among younger, highly educated teachers who felt confident with technology, a group many would expect to lead the way with AI. A smaller study with 214 university teachers in China found a similar pull, though its authors call the findings preliminary. Teachers who saw a fictitious AI-detection score of 87% rated the same paper lower on quality than those who saw 7% (Du et al., 2026).

A third experiment points to a simple defence. Preservice teachers at a Chinese university who wrote down their own judgement before seeing an AI recommendation copied misleading advice less often. Faced with misleading advice, they had nearly twice the odds of reaching the expert answer (Yan, 2026). When the AI’s advice was sound, judging first made no significant difference.

All three studies are new, and all used prepared tasks outside real classrooms. Their message is still clear. Teacher judgement does not survive contact with AI automatically. It needs habits that protect it, and the most promising habit is also the simplest. Decide first, then look. UNESCO’s framework for teachers names the wider risk, warning that over-reliance on AI could wear down teachers’ own core skills. It also holds that teachers remain accountable for the decisions they make with AI (Miao & Cukurova, 2024).

Care

Care is the hardest of the four to pin down, and the most contested. Andy Hargreaves interviewed 32 teachers of 12- to 14-year-olds near Toronto and concluded that ‘teaching cannot be reduced to technical competence or clinical standards’ (Hargreaves, 1998, p. 850). Several of his teachers spoke about their students in terms of love. Many of their rewards came from watching individual students change and grow.

Care also leaves a measurable trace. A 2007 meta-analysis of 119 studies and 355,325 students found that teachers’ empathy and warmth each correlated with positive student outcomes at about .32 (Cornelius-White, 2007). Its author judged these associations above average for educational innovations, though, like the relationship findings, they cannot show cause.

The philosopher Nel Noddings explains why care may need a person on both sides. In her account, a caring relation needs the full attention of the one caring and the recognition of the one cared for. When the one cared for misses that warmth, she writes, he ‘feels like an object. He is being treated, handled by formula’ (Noddings, 2013, Chapter 3). A message can sound warm and still feel like a formula, if nobody stands behind it.

Care also costs the carer something. The sociologist Arlie Hochschild called this effort emotional labour, the work of shaping one’s own feelings so that others feel cared for (Hochschild, 2012). That cost may be part of what makes care count. The psychologist Anat Perry makes exactly this argument. Human empathy has limits, so a person who chooses to spend it on you signals that you matter. A machine’s reply costs it no time or effort. For that reason, she argues, AI empathy ‘fails to convey authentic care or to indicate that the recipient holds any unique importance’ (Perry, 2023, p. 1808).

Perry’s group then tested the idea. Across nine studies with 6,282 participants, people rated the same AI-written replies as more empathic and supportive when they believed a human had written them. Given a choice, they also preferred a human when they wanted emotional connection (Rubin et al., 2025). The words alone did not settle how people felt. Who stood behind the words mattered too.

Not everyone sees AI care as hollow. Michael Inzlicht and colleagues argue that AI could add to human flourishing if it helps more people feel heard and cared for (Inzlicht et al., 2024). Critics answer that empathic AI is ‘impossible, immoral, or both’ (Montemayor et al., 2022, p. 1353). These are arguments, not experiments, and none of them concerns schools.

Care is also not the same as comfort. Chapter 2 showed that chatbots lean towards agreeing with the person typing (Sharma et al., 2024). Praise on its own does little for learning, and a classic review placed teacher praise among the weakest forms of feedback (Hattie & Timperley, 2007). A teacher who cares will sometimes say ‘not yet’, and mean it kindly.

In mental health, the gap is plainest. In 2025, Common Sense Media and Stanford Medicine tested four widely used chatbots in long conversations about teenagers’ mental health, and rated them an ‘Unacceptable’ risk for this use. The chatbots repeatedly missed what the testers called ‘breadcrumbs’, warning signs that appeared across several messages. They treated each message on its own, without the clinical judgement to see a crisis building (Common Sense Media & Stanford Medicine Brainstorm Lab for Mental Health Innovation, 2025).

That is precisely the work a caring teacher does across days and weeks. A student goes quiet, stops handing in homework and sits apart at lunch. No single sign means much. Together they prompt a word, a question and, where needed, a referral under the school’s safeguarding procedures (its rules for protecting children).

Countries draw the line in different places. China’s 2025 guidance for schools allows students, under the guidance and supervision of teachers and parents, to confide distress such as learning anxiety to an AI assistant and receive comfort. The same guidance forbids teachers to use generative AI as a substitute for teaching (Ministry of Education of the People’s Republic of China, 2025). Even this permission keeps adults in charge. That fits the finding above about missed warning signs. A school that allows such use would still need a person watching for that pattern.

On care, then, the honest position has three parts. People value care more when they believe it comes from a person, and solid experiments support that. In the tests so far, machines miss patterns that a caring adult notices over time. Whether simulated care helps or harms children in the long run, nobody yet knows.

Modelling

Modelling is the least tested of the four claims. None of the research behind this chapter watches a teacher model how to think, how to treat others or how to handle a mistake, and then measures the effect. Here the case rests on argument and experience.

Biesta offers a frame. Education, he argues, also shapes how young people come to exist ‘as subjects of initiative and responsibility rather than as objects of the actions of others’ (Biesta, 2015, p. 77). He adds that students ‘not only learn from what we say, but also from how we do’ (p. 79). In his view, they often attend more closely to the doing. That is the argument for modelling in a sentence.

In the AI era, modelling has an urgent new subject, which is how to check. After the first year of Estonia’s national AI programme, 73.4% of teachers agreed that students could produce high-quality work whose reasoning they could not explain. Only 11.7% believed students had built the habit of verifying AI output against other sources (TI-Hüpe, 2026). Habits like that grow slowly, through seeing someone else do the checking again and again.

A teacher who asks a chatbot a question in front of the class, doubts the answer aloud and checks it against a textbook is teaching more than the topic. So is a teacher who admits a mistake and corrects it calmly. AI can even help teachers set a better example. Tutor CoPilot nudged tutors towards probing questions and away from empty praise (Wang et al., 2026). The tool shaped the example, but the tutor still set it.

Until someone studies modelling directly, this part of the claim stands on theory. It may still be the part that matters most, and it deserves research of its own.

Who teachers become

Official bodies now say much the same thing about AI and teachers. England’s Department for Education states that technology, including generative AI, ‘should not replace the valuable relationship between teachers and pupils’ (Department for Education, 2025b). European teachers’ unions add that empathy, mentorship and personalised guidance ‘cannot be replicated or multiplied’ by AI (European Trade Union Committee for Education, 2024, p. 4). The Santiago Consensus, which UNESCO’s World Summit on Teachers adopted in 2025, calls for technologies that ‘amplify the relational and affective dimension of learning’ (UNESCO, 2025b, p. 6).

These statements say what should stay human. They do not show what must. The unions’ own paper admits that AI’s benefit for education is ‘yet to be proved as there is little data available’ (European Trade Union Committee for Education, 2024, p. 2).

Some plans for AI in schools leave teachers out altogether. Pakistan’s National Artificial Intelligence Policy 2025 sets out to bring AI into the curriculum from primary school to university and to train 10,000 trainers by 2027. The word ‘teacher’ does not appear in it (Ministry of Information Technology and Telecommunication, 2025). A plan that never names the people who will carry it out has skipped the hardest question.

Many teachers are still finding their footing. In my study, less digitally competent teachers described AI ‘primarily as a threat, to academic integrity, to the value of teacher knowledge, and to established pedagogical relationships’ (Ilyas, 2026, p. 209). One of them, the botanist, responded by watching devices. ‘I monitor their phones or laptops during class to ensure they’re not seeking outside assistance’ (Ilyas, 2026, p. 167, Participant L5). The thesis reads this as a defensive response, one that controls devices without building students’ ability to engage critically with AI.

Estonian teachers describe a different path. After the first year of their national programme, 64.9% said they had moved from monitoring students’ use of AI towards guiding it. Asked about their role, 44% said it had shifted from source of knowledge to facilitator of critical thinking (TI-Hüpe, 2026). Students rated the programme’s student app at 3.07 out of 5, short of its 4.2 target. These figures are teachers' own reports, so they show how teachers see their role, not what changed in their classrooms. The contrast with the defensive response in my own study is still useful, because it shows that monitoring is not the only option.

Guiding students’ use of AI is real work, because how students use it matters. In two lab experiments, a chatbot made no difference to learning overall (Lehmann et al., 2025). Students who used it to replace their own work, for example by generating solutions, covered more topics but understood each one less well. Those who asked it for explanations understood more. The chatbot also widened the gap between students with low and high prior knowledge. These usage findings come from exploratory analyses and a field study, and the paper is still a preprint. In the Turkish trial described in Chapter 1, hints that teachers helped design softened the harm of unguarded use (Bastani et al., 2025). Teachers’ judgement shaped that tool before any student touched it.

Saved time belongs in this picture too, because care takes energy and many teachers have little to spare. In the UK’s 2025 Teacher Wellbeing Index, 76% of school teachers reported stress. Of those who had considered leaving because of pressure on their wellbeing, 65% named workload as the main reason (Education Support, 2025). In a 2025 survey of 2,232 US public school teachers, weekly AI users gave a self-estimate of 5.9 hours saved a week, about six weeks a year (Gallup & Walton Family Foundation, 2025). They said they spent the time on fuller feedback, more individual lessons, emails to parents and getting home at a reasonable hour. Each of those is a fair use. Yet none of the research behind this chapter checks whether time saved by AI turns into more time with students.

Chapter 13 returns to this claim once more evidence is in. Three kinds of study would change it. One would compare AI tutoring with a teacher who knows the class over a full year. Another would show a machine noticing and safely responding to a child’s distress over time. A third would track whether hours saved by AI go back to students.

Until then, think of the part of teaching no machine can do as a relationship, not a list of tasks. It means being a particular person to a particular student, someone who knows them, decides with them in mind and whose attention costs something. Machines can help with almost every task around that relationship. They cannot yet be the person in it.

In practice

These routines turn the argument into ordinary practice. They draw on the research above, and nobody has yet tested them as programmes.

A primary teacher marking writing. A teacher lets an AI tool flag spelling and punctuation in a set of stories, then reads every story herself. On each one she writes a sentence that only a reader could write, such as a link to the student’s last piece or a question about a character. The tool saves time on the mechanics, and the students still know that someone read their work (Doyle et al., 2025).

A weekly noticing log. A class teacher writes a line or two about each child every week, noting anything that has changed in mood, friendships or work. Over a month, patterns emerge that no single day reveals, much like the ‘breadcrumbs’ the chatbots missed (Common Sense Media & Stanford Medicine Brainstorm Lab for Mental Health Innovation, 2025). Any concern goes to the school’s safeguarding lead, never to a chatbot.

A secondary teacher using AI marks. Before opening any mark that an AI suggests, the teacher records her own grade for a sample of scripts, then compares. Where the AI is harsher than she is, she looks twice, because teachers in one large experiment let harsh AI errors stand more often (Goulas et al., 2026). Judging first protects against misleading advice (Yan, 2026). She reads the work before she looks at any AI-detection score, if she looks at one at all (Du et al., 2026).

A lesson that models checking. A history teacher asks a chatbot a question in front of the class and reads the answer aloud. Students decide what they would need to check, and together the class tests two claims against the textbook and one against a library source. The teacher’s doubt becomes the lesson.

A tutor-group conversation. A form tutor (a secondary school class teacher) asks students, without judgement, which chatbots and companion apps they use and what for. Silence from adults sends its own message. One 16-year-old in a UK study said of his teachers, ‘The [teachers] don’t care. Never mentioned it [AI]’ (Internet Matters, 2025, p. 49).

A department meeting about saved time. Colleagues agree what time saved by AI is for, whether that is longer feedback conversations, calls home or simply a fair working week. A term later, they check whether it happened (Gallup & Walton Family Foundation, 2025).

In a university course. Keep one human meeting that no tool replaces: a short one-to-one conversation with each student about their project at least once a term. The historian who opens this chapter brings real finds into class for the same reason. The object, and the conversation around it, are the lesson (Ilyas, 2026, Participant H02).

When resources are thin

The strongest challenge to this chapter comes from places with too few teachers. UNESCO estimates that the world needs 44 million more primary and secondary teachers by 2030, including 15 million in sub-Saharan Africa (UNESCO, 2024). Where a class has no teacher at all, asking whether AI is better than nothing is a practical question.

The trials from low-resource settings show what AI did there and what teachers still did. In the Nigerian trial described in Chapter 1, teachers gave no direct instruction. After a three-day training, they set each session’s opening prompt, then circulated while students worked in pairs with the chatbot. They kept the conversations relevant and on task (De Simone et al., 2025). In Ghana, teachers stayed in the room mainly to fix technical problems while students worked alone with a maths tutor on WhatsApp (Henkel et al., 2024).

Student volunteers who teach in rural India see the same balance. In interviews with 23 of them in Rajasthan and Delhi, researchers heard hope that AI tutors could personalise learning and lighten workloads. The volunteers also named poor connectivity, a lack of AI training and parents’ doubts as barriers, and they saw AI as a complement to teaching (Goyal et al., 2025).

Both trials raised learning. Neither compared AI with a teacher working without it. So neither shows that teachers are unnecessary. AI can help a scarce teacher do more. A low-cost coaching tool could spread expert habits to novices who have no mentor (Wang et al., 2026). Stretching teachers is different from replacing them. A shortage of teachers is a question of pay, training and funding before it is a question of software.

The human core of teaching needs no device at all. Knowing every child’s name, reading their work, noticing who sits alone and deciding before looking at a machine’s answer all work in a classroom with one shared phone, or none. Chapter 7 returns to the conditions a tool needs before it can work in such classrooms.

My IFDC framework separates the skills a teacher has from the skills they can actually use in their working conditions (Ilyas et al., 2026b). Relationships and judgement need no device. That is why they hold up best where resources are thin. AI arrived in every education system at the same moment; what each system makes of it will depend on the conditions it builds for its teachers. What will matter over the next ten years is how each system uses it, and how well its teachers learn to master it.

Questions for reflection

Take these questions to your next department meeting, or into a quiet moment after school.

  1. In your own class, who would be the first to feel the difference if a machine, and not you, read their work?

  2. When did you last change a lesson halfway through, and what did you notice that made you change it?

  3. How would you know if a student had started turning to a chatbot instead of a person?

  4. Which AI suggestions do you accept without checking, and which do you judge for yourself first?

  5. If AI saved you an hour this week, where would you want that hour to go?
