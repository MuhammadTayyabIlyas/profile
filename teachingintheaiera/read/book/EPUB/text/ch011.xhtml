Chapter 9. Raising AI-Literate Learners

At US workshops supported by the National Science Foundation and the Jacobs Foundation, middle-school girls studied screenshots of ChatGPT’s answers. Researchers asked them to judge each answer and explain their judgement. One screenshot showed a multiplication problem with its working. Another offered research papers, complete with authors and dates. Both contained errors (Solyst et al., 2024, p. 907).

The students had already heard that generative AI could produce biased answers. Yet the working and the orderly presentation helped persuade them. Some expected a computer to handle arithmetic as reliably as a calculator. Across the incorrect items, 59% of their judgements accepted the answers as correct (Solyst et al., 2024, p. 908).

These were workshops with 26 girls in university outreach programmes, using prepared screenshots. Nobody watched a representative class using chatbots freely. Still, the study reveals an ordinary teaching problem. The students had reasons for trusting the answers. Those reasons simply did not establish whether the answers were true.

When researchers revealed the errors, the students’ views changed. They began to question the tool’s limits while retaining interest in its possibilities (Solyst et al., 2024, p. 911). No student needed a personal chatbot account for that conversation. Looking closely at an answer became the lesson.

This chapter asks a question that follows from that room. What should students learn about AI, and how?

My proposed answer brings understanding, judgement and responsible creation together. Students should learn enough about AI to explain its limits, question its outputs and make considered choices about using it. Their learning should grow through primary and secondary school. Direct access to a chatbot need not begin that journey.

Chapter 2 explained the machinery for teachers. In Chapter 5, we examined how free answers can weaken the thinking a task should produce. Here the focus shifts to what we teach students about the machinery itself. Chapter 8 supplies the safety boundaries for that teaching.

This need goes beyond students who already use AI. A child can see an AI-generated image or a machine-made claim on somebody else’s phone. We should teach about those moments alongside books, websites and other sources of information.

Pakistan supplies a close example. In interviews at one private Lahore school, only one student raised concerns about ChatGPT’s accuracy. She had checked references for Shakespeare and discovered that they were false (Khan et al., 2024, p. 322). That account cannot describe all Pakistani students. It does show why checking deserves an explicit place in teaching.

More than knowing the buttons

A student who writes a clear prompt has learned something useful. That skill alone cannot tell us whether the student understands AI. Nor does frequent use establish competence. We need a broader account of what the learning includes.

UNESCO’s student framework brings together a human-centred mindset, ethics, techniques and applications, and AI system design (Miao et al., 2024). Its progression moves through understanding, applying and creating. These describe increasing depth. They do not assign a birthday to each step.

DigCompEdu gives teachers a related duty. Its Area 6 is about helping learners build their digital competence. This includes information literacy, communication, content creation, responsible use and problem solving (Redecker, 2017, pp. 23–24). Learning to work a tool fits inside that wider duty. It cannot take its place.

For classroom planning, I would keep three connected purposes in view. Students need to understand how a system produces an output. They also need to decide whether that output deserves acceptance. Finally, they need to create something whose choices and consequences they can explain.

Understanding starts with telling things apart. A robot can follow fixed instructions without learning from examples. A classifier sorts inputs into groups. Generative AI makes new content, such as text or images. Chapter 2 explains this more fully. The task in class is to make these differences easy to see.

The opening study shows why these differences matter. When its students explained ChatGPT, they mixed ideas about search engines, voice assistants and calculators (Solyst et al., 2024). Each comparison gave part of the picture. Together, they led students to expect things the tool could not do.

Bewersdorff and colleagues’ review also identifies human-like accounts of AI among learners’ misconceptions (Bewersdorff et al., 2023). Its evidence mixes school and university settings, mostly in Europe and North America. We should listen for students’ own explanations before deciding which misconception needs attention.

Asking students what a system does can reveal more than asking whether they have used it. A child might identify a chatbot correctly yet believe that it checks every answer against a book. Another might explain training data clearly but accept any answer with headings. Those students need different next steps.

Understanding the mechanism should help a student predict something. After learning about training examples, students might predict where a classifier will struggle. An explanation that never changes a prediction may remain a set of remembered words. Teachers can ask for a reason before showing the outcome.

Generative AI needs its own explanation. The card-sorting task later in this chapter cannot explain every part of a chatbot. Students should go back to the difference Chapter 2 draws between producing a likely continuation and showing that something is true. A short generated passage can then help us check whether they understand that difference.

We should also distinguish the system’s job from the student’s goal. A tool might produce a convincing paragraph while the student needs to learn how an explanation works. Another might sort pictures successfully while leaving the student unsure why. Naming the learning goal makes those differences discussable.

These explanations can stay plain. A student does not need to recite technical vocabulary to explain that missing examples may lead to errors. Older students can add detail as their computing knowledge develops. The teacher can ask the same practical question throughout. What would this explanation lead you to test next?

Judgement also includes knowing when a tool has no useful role. Chapter 5’s attempt, help and later check routine remains relevant. This chapter adds a different question. Can the student explain why this particular tool suits this particular purpose?

Making something responsibly means thinking about other people as you work. UNICEF’s guidance includes children’s rights and the limits of AI as part of AI literacy (UNICEF Innocenti, 2025, p. 38). A student should meet those questions while making something. They belong inside the task, next to choices about accuracy and audience.

Consider a proposed poster for a school event. Students could ask which details require checking, whose work supplied the images and what readers might mistake for a real photograph. A finished poster gives those questions a purpose. The lesson can also end with a decision to use a drawing instead.

This wider account avoids making AI literacy a collection of product tricks. Buttons change, and school accounts differ. Understanding data, checking claims and explaining choices give teachers learning goals that can survive those changes.

Growing through primary and secondary school

No study behind this chapter gives a tested order, by age, for teaching AI literacy. National curricula choose an order. Research usually looks at only a short part of the journey. The steps below are a careful suggestion for teaching. Teachers will need to fit them to students’ language, what they already know and what access they are allowed.

Pakistan’s national curriculum makes that journey explicit from Grade I to Grade XII (National Curriculum Council Wing [NCC], 2026, p. 1). Its primary outcomes move from robots and instructions towards patterns, data and testing models. Grade III (ages eight to nine) includes recognising patterns learned from data and testing a basic model with visual tools (NCC, 2026, p. 7).

The document acknowledges the Media Tiz Foundation and the National Centre of Artificial Intelligence at NUST among its contributors. That is a curriculum partnership, with no evidence here that it establishes effectiveness. The curriculum tells us what its authors want students to learn.

Other systems draw the lines in different places. Singapore’s ministry keeps required direct AI use out of Primary 1 to 3 (Ministry of Education, Singapore, 2026a). India’s CBSE curriculum starts computational thinking in Classes 3 to 5 and brings in AI in Classes 6 to 8 (Central Board of Secondary Education [CBSE], 2026a). These are policy choices. They do not settle which order gives better learning. How your own students explain the ideas is a better guide to where to start.

In early primary, I would begin with things children can observe and describe. Students can sort objects, follow instructions and compare what happens when an instruction changes. Their teacher can name the difference between a person making a choice and a machine following a procedure. Words such as pattern, example and mistake can carry much of the explanation.

At this stage, we can ask where an answer came from and which adult could help check it. Children can recognise that a voice from a device belongs to a designed system. They need no account to learn that a pleasant voice can give a wrong answer. The teacher can supply an example on paper or read it aloud.

Sorting alone does not teach machine learning. A child sorting cards chooses a rule using human understanding. The teacher should explain what the activity represents and where the comparison stops. A later model demonstration can show software applying patterns from examples. Without that explanation, the game may leave the wrong idea intact.

In later primary, I would introduce the relationship between examples and results. Students can inspect a small collection of labelled pictures and notice what it leaves out. They can predict which unfamiliar picture might cause trouble. The point is to connect an error with the examples available to the system.

Where policy and resources allow, the teacher can then show a real model and link it to the paper task. Students should predict before they see the result and explain it afterwards. A demonstration that works deserves as much discussion as one that fails. Otherwise, children may learn only to expect mistakes.

Later primary also offers room for simple creation decisions. Students can explain what they made themselves, what a tool supplied and which details they checked. A teacher can model that explanation with their own prepared material. The account should use language children understand, without turning every task into paperwork.

In lower secondary, students can join these ideas to explain how a system works. They should tell apart the examples used for training and the new examples used for testing. They can compare sorting into groups with generating a response. Checking a chatbot’s claim then becomes a separate step from improving its prompt.

At this stage, a student might train a simple classifier with approved resources. Then the student can test it on purpose with cases outside the training set. Another task could compare a generated summary with the passage it came from. These tasks teach different things. The first shows how examples shape a model. The second checks whether an output keeps the meaning.

Subject knowledge should carry some of this learning. A science class can check whether a generated explanation fits observations. History students can trace a factual claim to its evidence. Language lessons can examine what changes when a tool rewrites somebody’s account. Computing lessons can explain the systems behind those outputs.

Upper secondary can ask students to justify a whole decision. They might examine who selected the data, who could challenge an error and what evidence would justify using the system. Coding can deepen this work where a course provides for it. Students without specialist programming should still learn to question an automated decision.

Creation should become more demanding too. Older students can specify an audience, compare alternatives and explain why they rejected part of an output. They should recognise when a convincing product rests on weak evidence. An honest account of uncertainty can count as good work.

The stages should revisit the same questions with harder material. Where did the output come from, what supports it and who takes responsibility? A primary student might answer through talk and drawings. An older student can defend a decision using several sources and a record of testing.

Research offers support for starting early, with limits. Su and colleagues’ early-childhood review found that all included studies came from developed countries or regions (Su et al., 2023, p. 4). That leaves a large gap for Pakistani teachers. An activity that works with well-supported children elsewhere still needs checking in our own classrooms.

Age limits also affect who can use a tool. Chapter 2 explains why teachers must check the rules for the actual tool and account. Learning about AI never means getting around those rules. UNICEF’s guidance also protects children’s freedom to opt out without losing chances to learn (UNICEF Innocenti, 2025, p. 38).

The progression should follow learning, not account ownership. A student who cannot log in can still explain a model’s error, check a claim and defend a design choice. Those are substantial achievements. Direct use can join the learning when the conditions allow it.

What teaching has actually changed

The strongest claim we can make is modest. Students can learn ideas about AI, and some teaching changes their immediate behaviour. Evidence about lasting judgement remains emerging. Large headlines often conceal small studies, short tests or weak comparisons.

A study funded by the US National Science Foundation followed teachers who taught the DAILy curriculum, a course about AI, to middle-school students (Zhang et al., 2024). The students who were taught improved their scores on AI concepts. The reported effect, d = .88, describes how much that group changed over time (Zhang et al., 2024, p. 23322).

The comparison group was a class from the year before, who did not take the course. That group also improved, though by less, and after adjustment the comparison favoured the students who were taught. This supports testing the curriculum further. But it does not show a .88 advantage over a comparison group taught at the same time.

Yu and colleagues combined 16 studies and report an average effect of g = 0.892 (Yu et al., 2026, p. 7). Most of the studies compared students with themselves, before and after teaching. Only two had a separate comparison group. The results also varied a great deal from study to study.

Combining studies cannot fix weaknesses in the studies themselves. A before-and-after gain may come from practice with the test, from other lessons, or from changes that would have happened anyway. The meta-analysis is encouraging evidence that students learn during these programmes. But it cannot tell a teacher how much gain to expect from next term’s course.

A French study comes closer to what teachers want students to do. In a preprint, Clerc and colleagues describe a workshop where students practised judging prompts, checking answers and asking follow-up questions (Clerc et al., 2026). They compared whole classes as they were, without placing students in groups at random. The analysis included students aged 13 to 15.

After accepting underspecified prompts, workshop students asked follow-up questions in 59.2% of trials, against 27.9% among controls (Clerc et al., 2026, p. 6). That is a change in action. The authors also warn against reading their immediate findings as durable learning.

A different preprint tested a brief explanatory text with Estonian school students aged 18 to 19 (Puppart & Aru, 2025). The Estonian Research Council and a centre funded by the education ministry supported the work. These were adults still in school. Reading about ChatGPT did not significantly reduce acceptance of wrong answers. It did increase rejection of correct ones.

The intervention group rejected correct advice in 36.84% of relevant trials, against 19.12% among controls (Puppart & Aru, 2025, p. 8). A warning can overshoot. Teaching that AI sometimes fails does not automatically teach someone when to trust it. If I only show students AI mistakes, I may teach them to reject good answers as well as bad ones. This is why the later lessons in this chapter include correct claims alongside wrong ones. It is one preprint with students aged 18 to 19, so I would watch for the same effect in younger classes without assuming it.

Fütterer and colleagues tested something else again. They tried chatbots designed to support students in German secondary lessons (Fütterer et al., 2026). These designs did no better than standard ChatGPT for knowledge, effort, interest or elaboration. This study tested how a tool was designed. It did not compare a lesson that explains AI with practical teaching.

My cautious reading is that students need opportunities to practise decisions as well as explanations of the technology. These studies cannot establish a general victory for one teaching method. Their ages, tasks and measures differ. Together, they give us a reason to check what students actually do after a lesson.

Trust that earns its place

‘How do you encourage your students to learn new tools or to try something new like ChatGPT?’ I asked a lecturer in Islamic Studies.

‘Sometimes I pretend I don’t know the answer, and then ask them what we should do,’ he said. That sends students to ChatGPT or Google Scholar. ‘If the internet isn’t working for them, I share my phone’s hotspot.’ Sometimes he offers a small prize, ‘like candy or a balloon’, ‘so they’ll quickly search and find the answer’ (Ilyas, 2026, Participant M04).

A teacher pretending not to know. A class racing to find out.

A linguist in my study worked on the other half of the skill. When I asked how students learn which sources to trust, her answer was a direction. Students should find what they need in the digital library, ‘rather than relying on YouTube where everyone is a YouTuber providing information’ (Ilyas, 2026, Participant H06).

One teacher made students eager to look. The other taught them where to trust what they found.

The aim of checking is a justified decision. An answer may deserve acceptance, rejection or a pause while we seek better evidence. Students need practice with all those outcomes. Constant suspicion can fail them as surely as easy trust.

Oh and colleagues followed children using a chatbot at home in a US study funded by the National Science Foundation and Harvard (Oh et al., 2025). Children endorsed 45.7% of responses classed as false, a category including unanswered questions (Oh et al., 2025, p. 552). They also accepted accurate responses much more often. Their trust showed some discrimination, with substantial errors remaining. This links to the opening workshops, where 59% of judgements accepted wrong answers. One study used prepared screenshots and the other followed children at home, yet both found that children accepted many wrong answers. Children already show some judgement, since they trust accurate answers more often, and a lesson can strengthen it.

The Lahore study adds a different warning. Most interviewed students treated ChatGPT as a source of authentic information (Khan et al., 2024). Teachers still need to find out what their own students count as a reason for trust.

I would ask students to separate three judgements. Does the answer sound clear, does it address the question and does evidence support it? A fluent answer may pass the first judgement and fail the others. Keeping them separate gives a teacher something specific to discuss.

The idea of checking outside the answer comes from older work on media literacy. One study of adults was backed by the McCormick and Spencer foundations. In it, fact checkers left sites they did not know and looked them up elsewhere (Wineburg & McGrew, 2019). Historians and university students often stayed on the first site. The researchers called this move away from the site lateral reading.

That adult evidence gives us a useful model of action. It does not prove transfer to students checking a chatbot. A chatbot’s paragraph may conceal its sources entirely. The checking task starts with finding a source for the claim itself.

A US school assessment, funded by the Hewlett Foundation, gives strong descriptive evidence about how students check sources (Breakstone et al., 2021). Only three of 3,119 students traced an anonymous video back to its source (Breakstone et al., 2021, p. 510). Teachers cannot assume that students pick up this skill just by coming across sources.

In a lesson, the teacher can select a claim students have enough knowledge to investigate. Students leave the generated answer and look for evidence in a textbook, library source or suitable website. They then explain whether that evidence supports the exact claim. Finding a page on the same topic is only a beginning.

A second chatbot saying the same thing does not finish the check. Neither does a list of links that nobody has opened. The student needs to inspect the relevant passage and explain the connection. A teacher can model this slowly before asking students to do it independently.

Prebunking is another approach. It shows students the tricks used to mislead people before they meet them elsewhere. In the game Bad News, players try producing misleading content themselves. Evidence from classrooms is still disputed. Spotting a trick is not the same as telling true claims from false ones.

A Swedish study including the game’s developers found students rated fake items less reliable after play (Axelsson et al., 2025). The Swedish Institute for Educational Research funded that study. Discernment improved by only d = 0.06, with no unexposed control group (Axelsson et al., 2025, p. 1002). About a third of the analysed students disengaged. I would give this result little weight. A game like this may start a discussion, but I would not rely on it to teach checking.

In another study funded by the same institute, Nygren and Efimova found no benefit from Bad News when they tested students again later (Nygren & Efimova, 2025). Their research also tested News Evaluator, a tool Nygren made. This longer view makes it harder to promise that one game will build lasting judgement.

A game may introduce manipulation, but ordinary lessons must return to checking. Repeated work should reward a sound reason for accepting an answer too.

Chapter 8 addresses the harms that misleading content can cause. Here the learning goal concerns the student’s judgement about a particular claim. That focus keeps a checking lesson manageable. The wider question of what counts as knowing returns in Chapter 12.

Creating and showing understanding

AI literacy includes making things. Creation gives students a reason to inspect data, test a model and consider an audience. It can also reveal understanding that a vocabulary quiz misses. The finished object alone cannot show all that learning.

Druga and Ko observed children training and coding smart programmes at US after-school sites (Druga & Ko, 2021). Children shifted some explanations of agency from devices towards the people who programmed them. The evidence is emerging and observational. Building helped make human choices visible within the technology.

So making things has a use beyond producing something that looks good. A student can learn that somebody chose the groups, collected the examples and decided which errors mattered. They can also find out that adding examples changes the result. The teacher can ask them to explain those changes in everyday words.

Ethics belongs at each decision. If a class designs a model to sort objects, students can ask which objects the examples leave out. They can consider whether the labels make sense for the task. Where people could face consequences, they should ask who could correct a mistake.

Generative work raises similar choices about who the work is for and who made it. A student writing a summary can compare it with the original before sharing it. A student making a picture can explain which parts came from a tool. Chapter 8’s rules on permission and privacy apply the whole time, so there is no need for another safety lecture here.

We should also let students say no, if they give reasons. An older student might decide that the data on hand cannot support the planned system. Another might decide that a generated image would mislead the people it is meant for. Either decision can make a good lesson, as long as students explain their evidence.

The choice of problem also needs a close look. Before they design anything, older students can ask whether a machine would make the situation better at all. They might compare the planned system with a simple rule, a conversation or a paper record. This makes the choice to use AI part of the learning.

Suppose a school plans a service that gives advice on which library books to borrow. Students could look at which readers its examples stand for and which tastes it misses. They could ask whether readers can say no to its advice. The design discussion is about people’s choices as well as how good the advice is.

These discussions can show that two good aims may conflict. A suggestion that saves time may narrow what a reader comes across. Collecting more information might give better suggestions. But it also asks people to say more about themselves. Students should explain the trade-off. They should not pretend that a technical fix answers every ethical question.

Teachers can keep such work small. Students can study a plan without collecting real borrowing records or building a service. A drawing can show where information goes in, what the system gives out and where a person can step in. Its value is that it shows who is responsible.

This also gives teachers who are not specialists a part in AI literacy. To read the plan with care, question its purpose and explain what follows from it, students need subject knowledge and judgement. Computing can explain in more depth how the system works. Other subjects can look at what people take for granted when they use it.

We need care when we measure this learning. In an EU-funded review, Lintner found that the scales in the review had not been tested for validity across cultures (Lintner, 2024, p. 4). Most relied on self-report. A questionnaire made in another country cannot simply tell a Pakistani teacher what a student can do.

Ng and colleagues’ questionnaire brings affective, behavioural, cognitive and ethical dimensions together (Ng et al., 2024). Its school sample came from Hong Kong, and students rated themselves. Those ratings can help teachers hear students’ perceptions. They should sit alongside evidence from a task.

The distinction also matters in my own doctoral research with university teachers in Pakistan. Participants were university teachers, and the study measured self-assessed digital competence. It did not test school students’ AI literacy.

Among those university teachers, Area 6 classifications were 28% low, 39% medium and 32% high (Ilyas, 2026, p. 146). These figures describe one sample’s digital competence profile. They cannot establish how well those teachers would teach children about AI. Nor can we treat the highest band as evidence of tested AI knowledge.

For students, I would collect a short explanation alongside a practical decision. The explanation shows what the student believes about the system. Their decision shows whether they can use that understanding when an answer looks tempting. Neither needs a long written report.

A primary student could draw where examples go into a model and explain what might cause a mistake. An older student could test a case the model has not seen and give reasons for a changed choice. A later task should change the topic or examples. Otherwise, a student who remembers the lesson’s answer may look as if they understand.

Teachers should compare reasons as well as results. Two students may both reject a false answer, with only one checking evidence. The other might reject everything carrying an AI label. Asking how each student decided reveals the difference.

These checks should remain small enough to use. A teacher can listen to explanations from a sample of students and revisit a misconception with the class. Later work can show whether the explanation changed. Chapter 6’s process evidence and brief oral checks already provide the routines.

The following worked examples are proposed lesson designs, not reports of lessons already taught. They draw on the research above. Teachers should adapt them to their subject, students and available materials.

Secondary testing that can fail usefully. The lesson asks secondary computing students to train and test a classroom model that sorts pictures of recyclable objects, and to explain its limits. The full design is Card 10 in Appendix C.

Upper-secondary creation with a record of choices. The lesson asks upper-secondary language students to produce an information sheet with permitted AI help and to record what they accepted, changed and rejected. The full design is Card 11 in Appendix C.

A short check after the lesson. The design is a short follow-up check in which students decide how they would test a fresh claim, compared with their earlier work. The full design is Card 12 in Appendix C.

In each of these examples, students predict, look at evidence and explain a choice. The subject gives the content. AI gives a reason to look at how an answer or product came about. These lesson designs need testing in real classrooms before anyone promises lasting gains.

In a university course. Set one ‘check it’ task in every module. Students take an AI answer from their own subject and check it against the library database, recording what was right, what was wrong and what was invented. The linguist earlier in this chapter sends students to the digital library ‘rather than relying on YouTube’ for the same reason (Ilyas, 2026, Participant H06).

When resources are thin

A national curriculum can arrive before schools are ready to teach it. Dawn reported that Khyber Pakhtunkhwa needed 10,605 IT teachers but had 3,050 in post (Ashfaq, 2026). The report was based on officials who described the province’s plan for putting the curriculum in place. It also counted 325 IT laboratories that did not work.

Those figures concern IT provision across the province. They do not count teachers already teaching AI literacy well. The figures do show why a curriculum cannot serve as evidence that students receive its lessons.

Pakistan’s national curriculum sees this problem. Where internet access is limited, it suggests resources that work offline, need little bandwidth or can be found locally (NCC, 2026, p. 21). The same document asks Grade III students to test a model with visual tools. We should keep both statements in mind when we read the curriculum.

To show a model working live, you need the infrastructural prerequisites described in Chapter 4. These may include electricity, a device that works and the software the task needs. Time and the right teaching resources matter too.

Chapter 4’s teacher-design burden applies here too, as the next study shows.

A free curriculum still asks for preparation. In a retrospective Day of AI survey, teachers reported about four hours of preparation on average (Hollands & Breazeal, 2024, p. 37). Some spent up to twenty hours adapting the content. The commonest change involved cutting or condensing it to fit available time.

This evaluation includes Breazeal, who directs the programme’s home organisation, MIT RAISE. Its impact report names Amazon Future Engineer and Liberty Mutual among its partners and sponsors (MIT RAISE, 2023). These are teachers’ own estimates, recalled afterwards, with no independent measure of effects on student learning. For planning, I would set aside time to prepare before a first AI literacy unit and expect to cut some content.

Research from Nigeria gives a practical starting point. Sanusi and colleagues combined computer activities with paper and manipulatives in an urban public middle-school club (Sanusi et al., 2023). Students discussed machine learning and its social uses. The study was small and had no comparison group. Its design still helps us see what a mixed route can contain.

A Chinese study funded by the National Social Science Fund compared two kinds of lessons. Some began unplugged and then moved to computers, while others used only computers (Ruan et al., 2025). The two groups did not differ significantly in self-efficacy. Their drawings favoured the group that began unplugged. This early evidence supports trying a mix of approaches. It does not settle the larger claim.

  • With one device, I would place the teacher’s demonstration inside a lesson that already works on paper. Students could make predictions in pairs before the teacher shows an output. Afterwards, everyone would explain a result or a mistake. Taking turns at the keyboard should not determine who gets to think.

  • Where the internet connection is unreliable, the teacher could prepare screenshots and source passages while the connection holds. The lesson would identify these as saved examples, with their date and context. Students could discuss the prompts and test claims against printed sources. Nobody would need to wait through repeated failed logins.

  • Without any device, the card lesson can still teach how selected examples shape an imagined system. A printed exchange can support a checking lesson. Teachers should explain that these are representations. Students have examined ideas about AI without directly testing a live model.

Language needs as much care as equipment. A teacher can ask students to explain in Urdu, Punjabi or another language they know well. Then the teacher can bring in the technical English term. The aim is to hear how students reason. No study here compares the languages to show which route works best.

For a multilingual checking lesson, the teacher could prepare a claim and a source passage in the class’s familiar language. Students would explain the connection before comparing an English version. A translation that sounds smoother might still change the claim. The teacher would need to check both versions beforehand.

This route also shows how much preparation costs. Finding a good passage, checking the wording and making clear copies all take time. A shared resource can save teachers from doing the same work twice, as long as somebody checks it for the next class. Chapter 10 looks at how colleagues can build that capacity together.

An older student could explain a training error aloud while a partner records the reason. Someone with stronger reading could locate a source passage without making every judgement for the group. Roles should rotate. The teacher needs to hear each student’s explanation before treating a shared product as shared understanding.

We must also tell teaching with AI apart from teaching about it. The News described training in Sindh run with UNICEF and Khan Academy Pakistan (“Sindh, Unicef, Khan Academy Celebrate,” 2026). Teachers learned to use Khanmigo for planning, questions and activities. That report does not show that students are getting AI literacy lessons.

UNESCO’s student framework names three conditions for putting it into practice: support for teachers, teaching resources and basic digital facilities (Miao et al., 2024). Unplugged routes can start useful learning while schools build those conditions. But the fact that unplugged routes exist should not hide the question of what schools still lack.

The next step for a teacher can stay small. Choose a concept students can explain, a decision they can practise and a way to hear their reasons. Then identify what preparation that lesson asks of the adult. Chapter 10 takes up the question that follows. How can teachers grow their AI competence without burning out?

In IFDC terms, teaching AI literacy belongs to the top level, where teachers make skilled choices (Ilyas et al., 2026b). It needs a teacher who understands the tools, but very little equipment. One phone, a few printed screenshots and a good question can carry a whole lesson.

Questions for reflection

Take these questions to a phase or department meeting, using a recent lesson as your starting point.

  1. What would your students count as a good reason to trust an AI answer?

  2. Which idea about AI could your youngest students learn without using a tool directly?

  3. How would you distinguish improved judgement from increased suspicion in a student’s work?

  4. What could students create that would make their choices about data, evidence or audience visible?

  5. Which part of your proposed lesson still depends on equipment, language support or preparation time you do not have?

Framework connections

This chapter centres on Area 6 of DigCompEdu, helping learners build their own digital competence (Redecker, 2017, pp. 23–24). Checking claims is part of information and media literacy. Explaining work done with AI is part of digital content creation. Thinking about audience and consequences is part of communication and responsible use. Testing a model and changing a choice are part of digital problem solving.
