Chapter 10. The Teacher as Learner

Between October 2022 and March 2023, maths and science teachers across Utah recorded their lessons with an app called TeachFX. The app turns a recording into a transcript and reports on classroom talk. Researchers at Stanford, Maryland and Harvard worked with the company to run a trial inside it. They randomised 523 teachers, and 224 stayed in the final sample with 1,509 recorded lessons (Demszky et al., 2025, pp. 7–8). Teachers in one group received a weekly email about their ‘focusing questions’, the questions that ask students to explain their thinking. The other group kept using TeachFX with its standard reports and no extra feedback (p. 7).

The feedback was free, automatic and brief. It still went largely unread. Three in four teachers in the feedback group opened at least one email, and 43% ever viewed the fuller analysis on the platform (Demszky et al., 2025, p. 10). When the researchers interviewed thirteen teachers, nine named limited time as a major reason for not using the tool more. One put it plainly. ‘I didn’t have time to sit and read it when it would come in, and then I would forget about it’ (p. 12).

The feedback did change one thing. Teachers asked about a fifth more focusing questions. But how often they took up students’ ideas, how much students reasoned and how much students talked did not change (Demszky et al., 2025, p. 10). The trial was run with TeachFX, which delivered the feedback through its own platform, and one author now works there as an engineer. The Gates Foundation and a University of Maryland award paid for it (p. 1).

I begin with this trial because it shows the shape of the problem. Here was a tool built to help teachers learn, offered to volunteers in a well-funded study. The software worked and the teachers were willing. What ran out was the quarter of an hour needed to read, think and try something different.

I heard a smaller version of the same story in my own interviews. I asked one lecturer how teachers learned the university’s upgraded system.

‘There is training for it,’ she said, ‘and our Director of IT also provides guidance through videos or screenshots.’

Then came the story. The upgrade had moved the power to hide final marks from the examinations office to teachers.

‘They sent a link about it, but perhaps I didn’t follow it closely. I simply saw a “Hide” button next to my final term, clicked it, and assumed the marks were hidden, but they actually weren’t. I did that for three courses without realizing’ (Ilyas, 2026, Participant L4).

Three courses. One button. One link, not read closely. The guidance existed. It arrived as one more link, and links are easy to miss.

That shortage runs through this chapter. Its central question is how teachers can grow their AI competence without burning out. Most teachers are already learning AI alone, in the evening, on their own phones, and the systems around them have barely noticed. Feeling trained is not the same as being competent, yet most of what we know about AI training measures the feeling. Time and money are the real limits, above all in low-resource systems, so the habits worth keeping are the ones that fit the hour a teacher actually has.

Chapter 9 asked what students should learn about AI and handed me the question of the teacher’s own readiness. The time AI can save belonged to Chapter 4. This chapter keeps to what learning AI costs, and how to pay that cost without paying with your health. Whole-school systems for training belong to Chapter 11.

Learning alone

Start with how teachers learn AI now. In a 2025 survey of American teachers funded by the Walton Family Foundation, 68% had received no AI training from their school or district in the 2024–25 school year. More had taught themselves (52%) than had been trained (31%) (Gallup & Walton Family Foundation, 2025). England looks similar. In March 2026 the Royal Society commissioned a weighted Teacher Tapp survey of more than 9,200 teachers. Only 18% had attended any AI training sessions or courses, and 15% had clear whole-school guidance. The rest relied on informal guidance (32%), their own initiative alone (18%), or had no support at all (23%) (Royal Society, 2026, p. 2).

The trend runs towards self-teaching. In a December 2025 Teacher Tapp panel, the share of English teachers who got their AI information by teaching themselves had risen from 27% in 2023 to 52%. Those with no information at all fell from 47% to 13% (Teacher Tapp, 2025). The pattern crosses borders. In a national survey of 34,571 Vietnamese teachers, supported by UNICEF and with one edtech co-author, nearly 61% said they had taught themselves to bring AI into lessons. Courses from the ministry or provincial departments reached 31.87%, and school-run courses 30.51% (Lê et al., 2025, p. 2).

Take care with these rates, because each survey asks a different question of a different population. The OECD’s own number comes from lower-secondary teachers across its members. In the 2024 TALIS survey, 29% reported a high need for training in AI, ‘more than for any other content area surveyed’. Some 38% had taken part in such training, from 76% in Singapore to 9% in France (OECD, 2025, p. 137). Almost one teacher in five reported a need and had received nothing (p. 154). In eight systems, trained teachers reported more need than untrained ones, which may mean weak training or sharper awareness of what is left to learn (p. 154). If a course leaves you feeling that you know less than before, that is not by itself a sign that the course failed. It may mean you can now see more of what there is to learn.

Self-teaching has a texture that surveys miss. A mixed-methods study recruited 298 American school teachers who already used ChatGPT and asked how they used it for their own development. The commonest use, chosen by 167 of them, was inspiration for lessons (Li et al., 2026, p. 27). One teacher explained the appeal. ‘My time is limited’ (p. 30). Another admitted, ‘Professionally, I don’t think I’ve learned to use it to its fullest extent’ (p. 34). The second quote suggests that quick use can feel enough while much of the tool stays unexplored. All 298 already used ChatGPT, so the study tells us how keen users learn, not how most teachers do.

My own doctoral research with university teachers in Pakistan found the same routes. The historian in my study described a just-in-time habit. ‘Whenever I need something for photography or a new editing tool, I search on YouTube for good channels, watch them, and experiment on my own’ (Ilyas, 2026, p. 178, Participant H02). The same teacher added, ‘Because of the generation gap, I also sometimes ask my 20-year-old son for help; he picks things up faster’ (p. 179). The computer scientist in my study had taken the formal route and passed it on. ‘I recently did a two-day training on Generative AI, which covered ChatGPT’ (Ilyas, 2026, p. 178, Participant H01). These are university teachers, and the shape of the problem is shared.

Pakistani school teachers learn the same way with less around them. In an online survey of 64 English teachers in 13 districts of Sindh, 13 had received any AI training. Most reached the internet on a personal smartphone (85.9%), and 29.7% had no internet at school (Ghumro et al., 2026, p. 319). Colleagues sharing ideas scored higher as a source of support than training from the education department (p. 323). The authors head their discussion ‘Willing, self-taught and unsupported’ (p. 327). The sample is small and was recruited online, so it probably over-represents connected teachers.

So the first fact about teacher learning in the AI era is that it is already happening, largely unseen, unpaid and unassessed. That is the ground on which any training offer now lands.

Feeling trained is not being competent

What do we know about whether AI training works? The largest summary of the research is still a preprint, a study not yet checked by other experts. It combined 212 results from 34 studies of AI training for school teachers, and found a large average gain, g = 0.76, in knowledge, skills and attitudes (Ma et al., 2026, p. 2).

The details matter more than that average. Most studies, 26 of the 34, measured one group before and after training, with no comparison group (Ma et al., 2026, p. 26). Most results came from teachers rating themselves. Only 31 of the 212 came from anything else, and those came from just five studies (p. 27). After a correction for the fact that positive results are published more often, the average fell to 0.49, and a true effect of zero could not be ruled out (p. 28). The authors themselves warn that the pooled figure ‘should not be interpreted as equivalent to evidence from a sufficiently powered body of controlled experimental studies’ (p. 36). The 0.76 figure mostly tells us that teachers felt they had gained, not that they teach differently. When a course advertises a large effect, a teacher can ask whether it was measured against a comparison group and by something other than self-rating.

A second preprint goes further. Czech researchers gave 564 teachers a test of AI knowledge, and also asked about their confidence and training. How confident teachers felt about AI had no link to how well they did on the test, r = .02. The number of AI trainings they had attended had no link either, r = .00 (Lintner et al., 2026, p. 13). One country and one test cannot settle the question. But the result fits an older pattern.

Before AI, researchers compared what teachers said they knew about teaching with digital media against what they could show on a test. Among 173 German maths teachers, most of them in service, self-reports and test scores were only weakly related. The correlations were .23 for content and pedagogical knowledge and .13 for the combined measure (Kadluba & Obersteiner, 2025, p. 675). The authors say their results ‘raise concerns about the validity of self-reports’ (p. 663). Another German team concluded that such self-reports are ‘at most a rough measure of teachers’ actual knowledge’ (Kosiol & Ufer, 2024, p. 11). Laura Desimone made the general point years earlier. Teachers over-report what they implement, so self-report gives ‘a too-optimistic view’ (Desimone, 2009, p. 190).

Confidence also rises faster than knowledge after short courses. In the United Arab Emirates, five days of Horizons modules lifted teachers’ self-ratings from 6.3 to 8.8 out of ten (Ministry of Education UAE & Emirates College for Advanced Education, 2025). That is a feeling, measured twice.

My thesis adds a twist from the other direction. The university teachers in my survey completed CheckIn Higher Education, the university version of DigCompEdu’s self-reflection tool. It has 25 items, scored from 0 to 150 (Ilyas, 2026, p. 114). The teachers also rated their overall competence on a single scale, before and after the items. Their overall self-rating was lower than the score built from their own answers to the items, 3.06 against 3.77, a medium gap (pp. 150–151). Both numbers are self-reports, so the gap tells us how teachers see themselves, not how skilled they really are.

One participant rated themselves low, but showed real command of digital tools in the interview. When asked about it, they ‘acknowledged deliberate underreporting in the hope that lower scores would attract training opportunities’ (Ilyas, 2026, p. 202). If a survey seems to lead to resources, scoring yourself low can be a sensible choice.

None of this makes self-assessment useless. It makes a self-rating a starting point for reflection and a poor proof of competence. The frameworks themselves say as much. DigCompEdu, the European framework, sets out 22 competences in six areas and six stages from Newcomer to Pioneer. Its authors write that the progression model ‘is not intended as a normative framework or as a tool for performance appraisal’ (Redecker, 2017, p. 28). Its lowest statement for reflective practice reads like many staffrooms in 2026. ‘I know that I need to enhance my digital skills, but I am unsure how and where to start’ (p. 39). UNESCO’s AI competency framework for teachers crosses five aspects with three levels to give ‘fifteen competency blocks’ (Miao & Cukurova, 2024, p. 22). Its fifth aspect is AI for a teacher’s own professional development.

Used as maps, these frameworks are valuable. They show a teacher what the next step might look like. Even the EU’s SELFIEforTEACHERS tool, with 32 items across the six areas, collects data that its designer admits ‘remains subjective’ (Economou, 2023, p. 86).

A scoping review I co-authored looked at 43 digital competence frameworks and related tools. Only 10 came with an assessment or self-reflection tool from the organisation that made them. Peer-reviewed evidence that the tool measures well was found for only two, both from the DigCompEdu family (Ilyas et al., 2026a, p. 59). None of the organisations behind the AI frameworks had released such a tool (p. 64). A map shows where you might go. It does not prove that you have arrived.

What we already knew about professional development

AI training arrives in a field that has been studied closely, and the results so far are disappointing. A review for the Education Endowment Foundation combined 104 experiments with 205 results. On average, teacher professional development raised student test scores by only .05 standard deviations (Sims et al., 2021, p. 5). In trials whose plans were registered in advance, the average was .01, no different from zero (p. 28). Mary Kennedy’s review of 28 American studies found that most effects fell between .00 and .20 (Kennedy, 2016, p. 957). For a teacher, this means that being sent on a course is no promise that students will gain. The fall to .01 in trials registered in advance matters, because those trials could not choose afterwards which results to report.

Coaching did better. Across 60 studies that tested cause and effect, coaching improved the quality of teaching by 0.49 standard deviations and student achievement by 0.18. Both figures roughly halved in programmes with 100 teachers or more (Kraft et al., 2018, pp. 547, 572). A school that wants coaching to help teachers with AI could start with a few teachers rather than a large group at once.

Hours predict little. Contact hours did not predict effects in a meta-analysis of 95 STEM programmes (Lynch et al., 2019, p. 274). Its authors warned that ‘more hours of a given intervention will not help if the intervention content is not useful’ (p. 285). A World Bank review, part-funded by the Gates Foundation, coded 33 evaluated programmes in low- and middle-income countries. Programmes above the median of 48 face-to-face hours did no better than those below it (Popova et al., 2022, p. 120). The EEF guidance drawn from these reviews reports no link between longer programmes and impact (Collin & Smith, 2021, p. 35). This does not mean a 45-minute module is enough. The programmes that worked in these reviews were long enough to include practice and follow-up.

So what does predict learning? Kennedy found that programmes with volunteers averaged an effect of .16, against .03 where attendance was compulsory. As she puts it, ‘Attendance is mandatory but learning is not’ (Kennedy, 2016, p. 973). She also notes that taking on a new idea means giving up an old habit (p. 948). Popova and colleagues found follow-up visits in 85% of the most effective programmes, but in only half of those run at large scale. Career rewards appeared in 88% of the most effective, against 55% (Popova et al., 2022, pp. 126, 128). Training built around one specific tool was linked to smaller student gains, by 0.118 standard deviations, though this rests on only a few programmes (p. 119).

Two cautions sit beside these results. Teachers can value training that leaves students unchanged. In an English trial of Lesson Study across 181 schools and 12,747 students, there was no effect on Key Stage 2 maths or reading. Teachers still ‘felt Lesson Study was useful professional development’ (Murphy et al., 2017, p. 4). And the tools that training asks teachers to use often get in the way. In the EEF review, ‘most references to technological resources were negative’, including ‘cumbersome and time-consuming feedback apps’ (Sims et al., 2021, p. 51). Programmes were more faithfully run when ‘planned around the limited time available to teachers’ (p. 6). The second caution matters for AI in particular, because AI training usually asks teachers to learn yet another app.

Short courses at national scale

Governments have moved fast, and most have moved short. Singapore announced a teacher AI literacy roadmap in July 2026 (Ministry of Education Singapore, 2026b). Its three online modules each take ‘about 30 to 45 minutes’, and all 29,605 primary, secondary and junior college teachers (a 2024 count) must finish the first by the end of 2026 (The Straits Times, 2026). Kenya’s Teachers Service Commission has tendered a national course for up to 273,000 teachers. The terms of reference specify ‘5-day online training (2 hours/day, 8pm-10pm, Monday to Friday)’, with internet bundles for participants (Teachers Service Commission, 2026, p. 5). The bundles are a rare sight, and the timing is the rule.

Vendors are often in the room. In the United States, the American Federation of Teachers launched a National Academy for AI Instruction with $23 million from Microsoft, OpenAI and Anthropic. It aims to support 400,000 educators over five years and is free for the union’s 1.8 million members (American Federation of Teachers, 2025). In Pakistan, Sindh’s school education department ran the country’s first AI-powered teacher training programme with UNICEF and Khan Academy Pakistan, described in Chapter 9. Certification required teachers to keep using Khanmigo, the company’s AI assistant, for six months afterwards (The News, 2026). The programme’s figures come from the department and its partners, and I have found no independent evaluation. A certificate that depends on six months’ use of one product makes training and product adoption the same thing.

Punjab chose a cascade. In October 2026, the province began a three-day course for 680 master trainers in two batches of 340, who would then train others (City42, 2026). Secondhand notices give the onward numbers, so I leave them out. The World Bank reviewers warn that in a cascade skill ‘may be diluted’ as each layer trains the next (Popova et al., 2022, p. 110).

Set these against the evidence above. The reviews favour practice, follow-up, colleagues and content that teachers can use on Monday. Many national offers give a short online unit, after hours, often built around one vendor’s product. Short does not mean useless, and a 45-minute module can set ground rules. The cascade and the module are cheap to count, though, and that is partly why they are chosen. Headcount is easy to report. Changed teaching is not.

The cost before the saving

Chapter 4 counted the minutes AI can save in planning. It also introduced two terms of my own. Teacher-design burden is the effort a teacher must put in before a tool helps learning, as distinct from simply having access to the tool. Infrastructural prerequisites are the conditions that must hold before a tool can work at all. None of the studies in this chapter uses either term. Both apply here in a particular form, because learning a tool is itself design work, done before any saving arrives.

The teachers in my thesis described this cost. ‘Multiple courses, research duties and paperwork leave little time for learning new technologies,’ said one (Ilyas, 2026, p. 168, Participant L7). My thesis reads this as ‘a temporal impossibility rather than a motivational deficit’ (p. 168). Chapter 4 quoted another teacher who could not build the online resource that would eventually have saved them time. The thesis calls that a cruel paradox and adds, ‘Time poverty becomes self-perpetuating’ (p. 168). The pattern ends in a hard sentence. ‘Teacher resilience enables institutional neglect’ (p. 169). These are university teachers, and their hours differ from a primary teacher’s. The arithmetic is the same.

School teachers describe the same squeeze without the research duties. Teachers in England work an average of 49.5 hours a week (Collin & Smith, 2021, p. 35). Only 29% think the time set aside for development is used well, against 73% of headteachers (p. 5). A college leader told the EEF what happens when staff are offered a course or a marking day. ‘Most teachers will go, “Oh, I’m just going to mark my books”’ (p. 34). Before planning AI training, a leader could ask staff how they would want to use the time, rather than assume.

AI has not lightened this. Education International, the global federation of teachers’ unions, argues that AI shifts workload instead of reducing it (Education International, 2025). Time moves into ‘crafting prompts, checking and rewriting outputs’. EdTech Hub, a research group funded by UK aid, the Gates Foundation, the World Bank and UNICEF, agrees. Training must address ‘the hidden labour involved in reviewing, adapting, or correcting AI-generated content’ (Adam & Lester, 2025, p. 24). Where teachers help design tools, ‘teachers’ time must be remunerated’ (p. 23). It is the only source in this chapter that says so. In Lima, a World Bank trial part-funded by Microsoft gave teachers a day of training and a Copilot licence. They became better judges of AI output and saved no time at all (Glave et al., 2026).

The cost is emotional as well. Fourteen secondary English teachers in Hong Kong kept reflective journals about generative AI, and ten raised inadequate training (Kohnke, 2024, p. 110). ‘The training was too generic and brief,’ said one (p. 110). Another said, ‘The introduction of AI tools felt like another mountain to climb’ (p. 111). It is a tiny study in one city, and the voices will be familiar in many staffrooms.

Low-resource systems add the clock. Teachers must find time to train after a full day’s work, as in Kenya’s evening course. EdTech Hub’s snowball survey of more than 800 teachers in 29 countries recorded a teacher who said, ‘I can’t learn AI at 10 p.m. after correcting papers. Teach me when I still have energy to listen’ (EdTech Hub, 2025). In Pakistan, state duties take the day as well as the evening. A survey of 250 elementary teachers in Sahiwal listed 23 extra duties, from election work and polio vaccination to census and dengue campaigns, most of them ‘performed outside the school’ (Nasreen & Naz, 2019, pp. 412–414). It is a descriptive study from before AI. The duties have not gone away.

So the honest account of AI competence starts with a debit. A teacher who learns a tool well spends hours before saving minutes. Who pays for those hours is a decision, and at present most systems leave it to the teacher.

Stress, and what buffers it

Burnout has a definition. Christina Maslach and Michael Leiter describe it as exhaustion, cynicism and a sense of inefficacy at work (Maslach & Leiter, 2016, p. 103). Their review of health care rests on six domains of working life, and workload is the first. Overload with no recovery drives exhaustion. ‘A sustainable and manageable workload, in contrast, provides opportunities to use and refine existing skills’ (p. 105). Learning anything new, including AI, needs a workload with room in it. Most interventions, they add, target the individual and leave the job alone (p. 109). The authors wrote the standard burnout inventory.

Research on technostress among teachers points the same way, with thin evidence. A cross-sectional survey of 378 STEM teachers in Zhejiang, China, found that the pressures technology creates predicted burnout. Organisational supports such as training and involvement in decisions reduced those pressures (Tu et al., 2025). The study concerns technology in general, and one survey at one time cannot show cause.

Support does seem to travel with confidence. In the Royal Society survey, teachers in schools with structured development and whole-school guidance were ‘roughly three times more likely to be confident across all dimensions of AI literacy’ (Royal Society, 2026, p. 2). That is an association in survey data, and confident schools may simply attract or keep confident teachers. In Sindh, colleagues who shared ideas scored above department training as a source of help (Ghumro et al., 2026, p. 323). Across more than 9,000 teachers in 336 Miami-Dade schools, better collaboration went with teachers improving faster, though the design was correlational (Ronfeldt et al., 2015, pp. 505, 511). None of this is a trial of AI support. Together it suggests that the cheapest buffer against technostress is a colleague down the corridor and an hour protected for the two of you.

Frameworks are beginning to say so. UNESCO asks policy to ‘mitigate the negative impact of AI use on teachers’ workloads and well-being’ (Miao & Cukurova, 2024, p. 46). The draft update to DigCompEdu was open for comment until the end of October 2026 and is due in spring 2027. It proposes protecting ‘the well-being of both educators and learners by addressing workload, screen time, boundaries and the right to disconnect’ (European School Education Platform, 2026). The National Education Union in England argues that ‘Teachers should have sufficient time to plan and prepare’ before new technologies arrive (National Education Union, n.d.-b). If the final DigCompEdu keeps that line, a teacher’s own working limits become part of what competence means. I think that is right.

When the machine gives the feedback

AI can also be the trainer. Automated feedback on classroom talk is the best-studied case, and the Utah trial that opened this chapter is its strongest result. An earlier pilot of a similar dashboard, built and evaluated by its own developers, gave 21 volunteer maths teachers feedback on six ‘talk moves’ across 233 lessons. None changed significantly. Teachers ‘were often unsure how to distill what the feedback’ meant for their own lessons (Jacobs et al., 2022, pp. 8, 11). I would not adopt such a tool without also setting aside time to discuss what it shows.

The same team had better luck online. In a five-week programming course with 1,136 volunteer instructors, automated feedback raised their uptake of students’ ideas by 13% (Demszky et al., 2024, p. 483). The cost was about one US dollar a session once the system existed (p. 501). Those instructors were mostly novices teaching online, so this is not school evidence. Recording a real classroom, the authors note, means ‘extra tasks that teachers may not want to engage in during the course of their busy workday’ (p. 501). The money is small, and the teacher’s time is not.

Generic chatbot coaching is weaker still. When expert teachers rated ChatGPT’s suggestions on transcripts of elementary maths lessons, 82% described something the teacher was already doing (Wang & Demszky, 2023). A coach who tells you what you already do is not yet a coach.

Human coaching at a distance carries its own warning. In South Africa, 180 primary schools were assigned at random to on-site coaching, virtual coaching by phone and messaging, or neither. After three years, on-site coaching raised students’ English oral skills by 0.31 standard deviations and reading by 0.13. Virtual coaching raised oral skills by 0.12, did nothing for English reading, and cut home-language reading by 0.19 (Cilliers et al., 2022, pp. 1–2). USAID funded the trial, and three authors worked for the department that ran the programme. The authors suggest that remote messages are ‘relatively easy to ignore’ (p. 4). They propose starting face to face and sustaining the change virtually (p. 14).

Other reviews lean the same way without settling it. Distance delivery went with lower student gains in the World Bank review (Popova et al., 2022, p. 124), and with smaller effects wherever part of a STEM programme was online (Lynch et al., 2019, p. 279). A coaching meta-analysis found no significant gap between virtual and in-person formats, with errors too large to rule much out (Kraft et al., 2018, p. 564). None of these studies used AI. Taken together, they say that a screen alone rarely carries the relationship that changes practice.

Keeping the skill you hand over

UNESCO’s framework names a risk to teachers themselves. ‘Over-reliance on AI could lead to the atrophy of teachers’ essential competencies’ (Miao & Cukurova, 2024, p. 15). Chapter 3 made the case for what stays human. This section asks a narrower question. How does a teacher keep a skill while using a tool that performs it?

No study has yet measured whether AI makes teachers lose their own skills. Cognitive scientists who reviewed the field in 2024 found ‘no research’ on how AI assistants affect the user’s own skill (Macnamara et al., 2024, p. 3). They warn that ‘AI-induced skill decay may operate outside the performer’s awareness’. The person stays busy, but stops doing the thinking (p. 4).

The only direct evidence comes from other professions, so it is a comparison, not proof. In four Polish medical centres, experienced doctors found fewer growths during colonoscopies done without AI after they had started working with AI help. Their rate fell from 28.4% to 22.4% (Budzyń et al., 2025). The study was observational, and medicine is not teaching. In aviation, 16 Boeing 747 pilots flew a simulator. Their hand control stayed sharp, but thinking skills such as keeping track of their position had slipped. ‘Pilots who kept their minds on task more performed better’ (Casner et al., 2014, p. 1514). The authors suggest ‘more frequent practice with these cognitive skills’ (p. 1515).

Lisanne Bainbridge observed in 1983 that the most successful automated systems ‘may need the greatest investment in human operator training’ (Bainbridge, 1983, p. 777). A medical review adds a second idea, ‘upskilling inhibition’: trainees who never learn a skill because the machine always did it for them (Natali et al., 2025, p. 2). It recommends regular practice ‘without AI’ (p. 33).

One small classroom study points the same way. Ten Korean elementary teachers each planned a lesson with ChatGPT for an hour while researchers watched. Experienced teachers who knew AI well questioned and refined what it gave them. Novice teachers tended to ‘accept AI-generated responses as they are or make only minimal edits’, whatever their AI skill (Choi et al., 2026, pp. 8, 10). The authors warn that ‘uncritical acceptance of AI outputs can weaken teachers’ professional judgment and autonomy’ (p. 11). Ten teachers and one session each prove little. They fit the warning in Chapter 4 about trainees who never learn to plan.

So teacher learning in the AI era has two halves. Teachers must learn the tools. They must also keep practising, on purpose, the skills the tools take over. A new teacher may need to plan some lessons from scratch and mark some sets by hand while the habit of judgement forms. An experienced teacher needs to notice when the first draft has quietly become the final one. Kennedy’s point about letting go of old habits applies in reverse here. Some habits are worth refusing to let go.

In practice

These habits turn the argument into routines that fit an ordinary week. They draw on the evidence above, and nobody has tested them as a programme.

Start with one task. A teacher of Year 3 (ages seven to eight) picks one job she already does every week, such as writing a reading passage at two levels. She learns to do that one job with AI until it is quick, and leaves everything else alone for a term. In the Lima trial, teachers’ prompting improved over eight months of light follow-up (Glave et al., 2026).

Learn with one colleague. Two science teachers agree to try the same tool on the same unit and compare notes for ten minutes every Friday. Collaboration is linked to faster improvement (Ronfeldt et al., 2015, p. 505), and colleagues outscored department training as a source of help in Sindh (Ghumro et al., 2026, p. 323).

Test yourself, then rate yourself. Before a department rates its confidence with AI, each teacher tries a short task without help, such as finding the two errors in a generated worksheet. The result goes beside the self-rating. Self-reports track tested knowledge weakly (Kadluba & Obersteiner, 2025, p. 675), and a test calibrates the feeling.

Plan one lesson unaided. A mentor asks an early-career teacher to plan one lesson a week with no AI and talk it through aloud. Experienced teachers keep the same habit once a fortnight. The aviation and medical evidence is analogy, and it points to deliberate practice of the skill being automated (Casner et al., 2014, p. 1515; Natali et al., 2025, p. 33).

Read the feedback together. If a school adopts a tool that analyses lessons, two teachers open their reports side by side in a protected twenty minutes. Fewer than half of the Utah teachers ever opened theirs alone, and the pilot teachers did not know what theirs meant (Demszky et al., 2025, p. 10; Jacobs et al., 2022, p. 11).

Count the hours. A teacher keeps a two-week note of every minute spent learning, prompting, checking and fixing AI output, evenings included. The total goes to whoever decides on training. No study yet measures these hours, so the only figures will be the ones teachers keep.

Ask who made the course. Before signing up, a teacher checks who built the training, who paid for it and whether certification depends on using a product. A course that teaches principles survives a change of tool, and tool-specific training went with lower student gains in the World Bank review (Popova et al., 2022, p. 119).

Protect the hour. A head of department moves one meeting a month into shared learning time and defends it. Teachers asked for training ‘when I still have energy to listen’ (EdTech Hub, 2025), and the EEF found programmes ran better when planned around the time teachers actually had (Sims et al., 2021, p. 6).

In a university course team. Hold a half-hour meeting each month where one colleague shows one AI use from their own teaching, including what went wrong. The computer scientist in my study learns new tools from his strongest students and then passes them on to colleagues (Ilyas, 2026, Participant H01).

When resources are thin

Every habit above assumes a device, some data and an hour. For a teacher’s own learning, the list of infrastructural prerequisites is short and often unmet. It is a working phone, an affordable data bundle, electricity in the evening, a course in a language the teacher reads easily, and time not already spent.

Pakistani data show how often the list fails. In Sindh, English teachers were surveyed about the problems they face. Data cost and load shedding both scored high as challenges, at 3.88 and 3.70 on a five-point scale (Ghumro et al., 2026, pp. 319, 323). In Punjab, 343 secondary English teachers were asked about professional development, and 120 had attended none. Family responsibilities were the main barrier. One woman said, ‘We do not have complete freedom to go and avail the opportunities’ (Butt et al., 2021, p. 90). Another turned down training in Lahore because it ‘may cost me too much’ (p. 91). That study predates AI. The barriers do not.

Online courses remove the cost of travel but add the cost of mobile data. A review funded by the UK’s FCDO looked at Khyber Pakhtunkhwa’s induction programme for new teachers. The lead master trainers had only a five-day course, and there was ‘no mechanism in place for the CPD’ of the trainers themselves. Teachers and trainers worked on mobile phones, which ‘reduces the efficiency’ of delivery. When videos would not load, teachers downloaded them and shared them ‘through mediums like WhatsApp’. Distance to the training site was a problem ‘particularly pronounced for female teachers’ (Institute of Social and Policy Sciences, [2024]). Chapter 7 describes a teacher in Islamabad who tried until 2 a.m. to open an app on a phone (Aslam et al., 2025). Online training moves its costs onto the teacher’s own phone, data and late hours. Before a course is released, its designers could check that each video loads on the kind of phone teachers actually use.

Teachers then pay for what the system lacks. Chapter 4 quoted the zoologist in my study, who paid for equipment from a personal account and was never reimbursed (Ilyas, 2026, p. 158, Participant M06). My thesis also notes that the work of making technology available has ‘no home in existing frameworks’ (p. 204). DigCompEdu, it argues, ‘assesses what teachers do with technology; it does not assess what teachers do to make technology available’ (p. 204). The same is true of AI training. A course can teach prompting and ethics. It cannot teach a teacher how to charge a phone during a power cut.

The gap may not be in teachers’ skill. In the only objective test I know of among Pakistani school teachers, 477 primary teachers in Punjab took a 25-question test based on UNESCO’s framework. Most were women (72.1%), and most taught in rural schools (75.7%). Their average score was 73.54% of the maximum, and neither gender nor rural location made a measurable difference (Hanif & Hussain, 2026, pp. 366, 368, 372). My thesis also found no gender difference in overall digital competence among university teachers (Ilyas, 2026, p. 148). This is one test in one province, so I would not apply it to all of Pakistan. Still, it is the kind of evidence this chapter has mostly lacked, because it measures knowledge directly instead of asking teachers to rate themselves. Training in such places could spend less time on basics and more on access and practice.

In England, by contrast, men were much more likely than women to say they felt confident in all three parts of AI literacy, 28% against 10% (Royal Society, 2026, p. 1). A gap in tested skill and a gap in confidence are different things. In Pakistan, gender may matter more for access to learning, as the Punjab interviews suggest.

Some systems have begun to budget for the prerequisites. Kerala’s public agency KITE trained 180 master trainers for a month before running three-day batches of 25 teachers, aiming at 80,000 (The Hindu, 2024). Teachers themselves became the trainers, it says, with ‘no external IT vendors’ (Kerala Infrastructure and Technology for Education, 2026). Ghana introduced its AI apps through the weekly professional learning community sessions already held in its 712 senior high schools, with Mastercard Foundation funding (Graphic Online, 2025). In Sierra Leone, a pilot of the WhatsApp chatbot described in Chapter 4 reached 195 teachers in 55 schools, who used it mostly to clarify content and to plan lessons (Adam & Lester, 2025, p. 17); the 98% saving in data cost comes from the larger trial (Björkegren et al., 2025). Rural accounting teachers in South Africa kept a learning community alive on WhatsApp. One of them warned that ‘some teachers in rural schools do not have access to Wi-Fi, and data is also costly’ (Oduro et al., 2024, p. S7). Each route still moves some cost onto the teacher. None removes the need for an hour.

In my years as an assistant education officer in Gujranwala, I carried many training notices to schools. More than once I arrived on the morning the staff had been assigned election or polio duty. The head would nod and pin the notice to the board. We both knew which duty would win. Nobody in that office was unwilling. The calendar simply had no empty day in it.

The cheapest routes, then, are the ones that use what teachers already hold. A colleague, a phone, a WhatsApp group of five teachers in the same tehsil, and a school meeting that already happens. The one thing none of them can supply is paid time. Only Kenya’s data bundles and EdTech Hub’s call for remuneration name it at all. Chapter 11 takes up how a school or a system might.

IFDC treats chances to learn as something institutions must provide, together with the safety to try and fail (Ilyas et al., 2026b). When training arrives only as a link on a phone, the chance to learn is offered, but teachers cannot really take it.

Questions for reflection

Take these questions to your next department or phase meeting, or into a quiet moment after school.

  1. In your own school, how much time did teachers give to learning AI last term, and did anyone in charge know about it?

  2. If you tested yourself tomorrow on the AI tool you use most, what would the test show that your confidence does not?

  3. Which skill have you let a tool do for you this year, and when did you last do it unaided?

  4. Who is the colleague you would most want beside you when you open a new tool, and does the timetable ever put you in the same room?

  5. What would your school have to stop doing to give you one protected hour a month?

This chapter belongs to DigCompEdu’s first area, professional engagement, and to two of its competences: reflective practice and continuing professional development (Redecker, 2017, pp. 38–41).
