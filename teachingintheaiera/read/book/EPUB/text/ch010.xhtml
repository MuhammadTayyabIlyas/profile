Chapter 8. Safe, Fair and Honest

During a discussion about online privacy, a ten-year-old girl explained how she protected herself. She used a name she had invented. ‘I make up a name’, she told the researchers (Zhao et al., 2019, p. 7).

Her answer showed care. She understood that people online did not need to know everything about her. Other children described checking someone’s identity in person before accepting an online request. Yet the group understood much less about information that apps collected quietly. Familiar apps could feel safe because nothing bad had happened before.

These discussions took place with primary children in the UK, before generative AI became widely available. They offer emerging evidence about children’s thinking, from a small study. We cannot infer how those children would use a chatbot today. The distinction still matters. A child can follow the rule about names while adults overlook the system collecting information around her.

Adults face their own version of that gap. At the university in my doctoral study, staff were told not to use WhatsApp for official contact with students.

‘You also said WhatsApp usage has been restricted. So what do you do instead?’ I asked one lecturer.

‘We have a Class Representative (CR) who usually has a personal group. I’ll send any link or critical information to the CR, who then shares it in their informal class group. If I personally need to say something directly, I’ll ask the CR to add me briefly so I can post my message, then I exit afterward’ (Ilyas, 2026, Participant L4).

In, post, out. The rule was kept in form. The messages still travelled through a private group outside the university’s own systems. A safety rule works only when it comes with a safe way to do the job, and providing that way is the school’s task as much as the teacher’s.

Chapter 2 gave us ground rules for starting safely. The discussion in Chapter 7 asked who could gain from AI under unequal conditions. This chapter brings those questions together. What does safe, fair and honest use of AI with children ask of a teacher, and what must a school have in place first?

My starting position is that responsibility needs both classroom judgement and support beyond the classroom. A careful teacher needs somewhere to take a concern. An approved product needs someone who can explain what approval covers. Children need adults who will listen when a tool causes trouble, including trouble outside a lesson.

Safe use concerns what happens to a child and to information about them. Fair use concerns how tools describe, judge and treat different students. Honest use concerns what we tell one another about the help a machine supplied. These responsibilities meet in ordinary decisions. A generated report comment can raise all three before anyone presses send.

Data, permission and monitoring

Privacy is about more than keeping a secret. A school may collect information for teaching, and then pass it to a company with different aims and rules. Lupton and Williamson (2017) describe how organisations turn what children do into data. That data is then used to sort children and make predictions about them. Their argument was written before generative AI. It still explains why a useful classroom feature does not answer every question about a product.

Consider the difference between displaying a prepared explanation and asking students to use individual accounts. Both may support the same lesson. The second can create records of each student’s questions, mistakes and activity. Whether those records leave the school depends on the product and its terms. A teacher needs that answer before choosing the activity.

Children cannot carry the whole checking task. Stoilova and colleagues mapped research on children’s understanding of personal data and privacy. Their review covered 105 studies (Stoilova et al., 2021, p. 564). Most concentrated on privacy between people. The authors found no research on children’s understanding of consent in commercial settings within that evidence base. This is strong evidence of a research gap before generative AI.

The child in our opening understood a visible request for information. Background collection asked something harder of her. A lesson about careful typing can help, while leaving adults responsible for the less visible conditions. We should resist treating a student’s successful completion of a privacy lesson as permission to stop checking products.

Schools struggle with that work too. In a small US study, officials described teachers bringing free tools into classrooms without district checks (Chanenson et al., 2023). The research concerned educational technology before widespread generative AI use. It found organisational problems that a new chatbot can enter easily. Teachers wanted something useful for tomorrow, while the people assessing data practices lacked time and resources. The problem here is one of time and staffing, not of careless teachers. Still, a teacher who finds a useful free tool can ask one simple question before using it with a class: who in this school has checked it?

A study of Australian policy reaches a related concern. Pangrazio and Bunn (2024) looked at how education authorities checked digital products. They found that questionnaires can leave authorities relying on what sellers say about their own practices. They also found that authorities said little about how checks were done, and gave children’s views little place. When a product is approved, someone needs to explain what was checked and what could change later.

For an ordinary classroom decision, I would begin with the learning purpose and the information the activity would create. If students can meet that purpose without personal accounts, I would choose that route first. Where accounts offer a clear benefit, the school should explain the data arrangements before students begin. A teacher should not have to invent that explanation from a sales page.

Chapter 2’s rule about data that can identify a student still applies. Here the next question is about the whole activity, including what students may type themselves. A reading task might invite a student to write about home life. The prompt can lead a student to share private details even when the teacher types no names. So checking the task means asking what an ordinary student might well type.

Permission deserves the same attention. Akgun and Greenhow (2022), in a conceptual review, question how freely families can choose when public schools require a system. Their argument does not establish a legal rule for every country. It exposes a practical difficulty. A signature means less when declining it leaves a child unable to complete the lesson.

Teachers can make that difficulty visible. Before requesting permission, explain what the tool does, what information it receives and what alternative the child can use. Families also need to know whom to contact and whether permission can change. An explanation should separate a teacher using AI to prepare materials from a student speaking directly to it. Those activities create different questions.

The Internet Watch Foundation (IWF) and National Crime Agency (NCA) offer guidance for settings working with children. Their guide advises reviewing consent for photographs and videos, including whether settings need the images at all. It also recommends involving children in discussions about their images (Internet Watch Foundation & National Crime Agency, 2026, p. 5). This is English practitioner guidance. Its focus on a real choice travels well as a question for schools elsewhere.

A photograph already published for one purpose does not remove the need to consider another use. For example, a teacher might want to turn class photographs into illustrated characters. That proposal needs a fresh decision about necessity, permission and the service receiving the images. A pleasing result cannot answer those questions on its own.

Parents may need help without needing a lecture. A preliminary survey partly funded by The Walt Disney Company found that some parents felt poorly equipped to guide adolescent AI use. Its small, self-selected international sample limits what we can infer (Eira et al., 2025). The finding supports a conversation about what families need explained.

A useful talk with families starts with the task, then moves to the choices. Show a sample activity with no student information in it. Explain what the adult does and how to raise a concern. Let families tell you about problems in private, since it may be hard to disagree at a public meeting. Keep another way to reach the same learning open until the questions are answered.

Detailed consent arrangements and procurement belong in Chapter 11. The teacher’s immediate part is smaller and still demanding. Recognise when an activity needs a school decision, and make the learning purpose clear enough for someone to assess it.

Monitoring creates a difficult promise. A school collects more information because it hopes to notice danger sooner. That promise deserves scrutiny, especially when the information includes private writing or conversations. Protecting children’s safety and respecting their privacy can pull in different directions within the same product.

England’s statutory guidance gives a useful example of how duties differ from advice. Schools must have regard to Keeping children safe in education. It asks schools to review their filtering and monitoring at least once a year (Department for Education, 2026c, p. 48). This sets an expectation within England’s safeguarding system. It does not show that any one monitoring product prevents harm. Readers outside England can still ask the question it raises. When did someone last check what our filtering and monitoring really do?

The Department also has separate product standards for suppliers. They expect products to keep a record of prompts and replies. They also expect products to alert local supervisors when a student may have raised a safeguarding concern (Department for Education, 2026a). These are expectations for products, not law. Their value depends on what happens once the alert reaches a person.

A record can reveal a concern and create another place where sensitive information sits. Teachers need to know who can see it and what they should do with it. Students need a plain explanation of those limits before using the system. A chatbot conversation cannot honestly be presented as private if school staff may read it.

Research does not support the idea that more watching brings more safety. Sheikh and colleagues reviewed high-tech school surveillance, and much of the evidence came from US schools. The review found that the effects on safety were unclear (Sheikh et al., 2025, p. 2450). Most of the evidence was about cameras, so this was not a review of current AI chat monitoring alone.

The same review found that surveillance was more common in schools with more Black students and more students from low-income homes. It also found that no study in the review showed that minoritised groups’ outcomes were affected more than others’. Both findings matter. We should pay attention when some students are watched more than others. But a claim that the harm is proven to be unequal would go beyond this evidence. Schools should not tell students or families that a monitoring feature guarantees safety.

An alert also needs interpretation. A student may discuss distress in a history assignment, repeat something heard elsewhere or ask a personal question. The words alone cannot settle what the student needs. A teacher who knows the task can supply context, and safeguarding staff can judge the concern. Chapter 3’s work of noticing remains relevant here.

Before a monitored lesson, I would want a named person in charge of alerts. I would also want an agreed response during school hours. The school also needs to explain what happens outside those hours. An automatic message must not lead students to believe that an adult is always there when nobody has agreed to do that job.

Here, infrastructural prerequisites mean more than power and an internet link. They also mean people, safe records and a way to respond that works.

Teachers should be able to raise a practical objection. A tool may offer alerts that the school cannot reliably handle. That problem needs an organisational answer before the feature becomes part of classroom practice. Chapter 11 will examine who owns that decision and how schools check the promises suppliers make.

Meanwhile, the classroom explanation should stay concrete. Tell students what the system records, who may review it and where to seek help directly. A child with an urgent concern should know how to reach a person without waiting for software to notice.

Fair judgement and honest explanations

Bias can begin before a student has produced any work. Sakunkoo and Sakunkoo (2025) asked language models to make predictions from names. In one table, GPT-4o-mini predicted male students’ mathematics scores at 87.8 for Chinese names and 79.2 for Thai names (2025, p. 701). The model had no student work to judge.

That is an emerging audit finding about assumptions. It is not a measured gap in real students’ marks. What the audit reveals is the willingness of a model to supply an unsupported judgement about a child.

Tests of bias also need to fit the local society. Khandelwal and colleagues built a test about caste and religion in India. When given a choice, GPT-3.5 picked the stereotyped option in 79.52% of the caste questions (Khandelwal et al., 2024, p. 236). The test used an older model and prepared comparisons. So it cannot tell us how often AI discriminates in Pakistani schools today.

Its value lies partly in the question it asks. A fairness test built around one country’s groups may miss differences that matter elsewhere. Teachers should ask whose experiences a test includes. Knowing a school community helps a teacher see what questions a test should ask. It does not make a teacher responsible for testing a whole model. What a teacher can do is notice and report. If an output describes students from one group in a fixed or unfair way, I would save the example without any student details and pass it to whoever oversees the tool. The In practice section returns to this step.

Real writing produces a more mixed picture. Yamashita examined GPT-4o’s ratings of English language learners’ essays from US school assessments. The corpus contained 6,482 essays and ratings from 27 human raters (Yamashita, 2025, p. 344). GPT-4o showed no substantial gender or socio-economic bias, but it showed a significant race effect.

Yamashita also cautioned that the race effect could partly reflect unequal group sizes. Its size resembled effects found among human raters. Yet the bias ran opposite to some of those raters, which suggests the model had a bias of its own. The study tested a single model on a particular corpus. Its findings temper a simple claim that AI always grades every group unfairly. They also leave plenty for schools to check.

The name audit and the essay study address different tasks. One asks what the model assumes without evidence. The other asks how it rates writing against criteria. Neither cancels the other. Teachers need to know what evidence a tool actually used before treating its output as a judgement about a student.

Fairness also depends on what an error leads to. A poor practice question and a decision about who gets support do not carry the same weight. A teacher can replace the question at once. A judgement about ability, once written down, may shape later decisions. How hard we check should depend on what the school might do with the answer.

Chapter 3 introduced the habit of deciding before looking at AI advice. A Greek experiment shows why that habit deserves attention. Teachers reviewed an incorrect grade that researchers labelled as coming from either AI or a colleague. When the grade was too harsh, the AI label left teachers further from the fair mark (Goulas et al., 2026).

The experiment gives strong evidence for that one response to a label. It used a prepared task, not a term of normal classroom marking. Teachers did not respond in the same way to a grade that was too generous. A broad claim that teachers always give way to AI would miss that difference. The practical point for me is about low marks. When a tool suggests a harsh grade, I would read the work again against the criteria before accepting it.

Other evidence from public services points the other way. In Dutch experiments, civil servants were less likely to follow advice from an algorithm than advice said to come from a human expert (Alon-Barkat & Busuioc, 2023). These were adults making made-up decisions for public services. Taken together, the studies show that automation bias is a disputed concern. Whether it appears depends on the setting.

A human checking a result needs an independent basis for disagreement. Looking at a fluent comment and deciding that it sounds reasonable offers little resistance. Reviewing the work against a criterion gives the teacher something firmer. An explanation of the teacher’s decision also helps colleagues understand why they should accept it.

I would keep decisions about grouping, support and reported progress tied to evidence the teacher can inspect. A tool may suggest a possibility, but the teacher should record the observations supporting any resulting decision. If those observations are missing, the suggestion should remain a question. It should not quietly become a description of the child.

Writing criteria, checking examples and looking hard at a doubtful suggestion all take work. Chapter 4 called this work the teacher-design burden.

A school cannot remove this burden by writing that teachers keep final responsibility. Staff need time, a standard they can use and the right to reject what a tool produces. Otherwise, the final signature may tell us who took responsibility. It tells us little about what checking took place.

Honesty asks something of teachers as well as students. If a tool helped prepare feedback or an explanation, students may reasonably want to know what the teacher did. They also need clear expectations about their own use. Silence leaves everyone guessing whether the same action counts as sensible help or misconduct.

Disclosure can cost trust. Schilke and Reimann’s (2025) experiments found that people trusted someone less after learning that AI had contributed to their work. One experiment concerned university grading, while others involved adults in different work settings. The size of the response varied greatly. These studies do not show how primary students would respond to their own teacher.

A further experiment found a greater trust cost when someone else exposed AI use than when the person disclosed it. That finding concerned a tax-advice scenario. It offers a reason to examine the temptation to stay silent, while leaving the classroom implications open.

My cautious position is to explain meaningful AI assistance alongside the work the teacher retained. A teacher might say that AI suggested examples, which the teacher checked and changed. For feedback, the explanation should say who read the student’s work and decided what advice to give. A vague claim that everything received human oversight tells students too little.

Children also need a workable way to describe their help. The routines in Chapters 5 and 6 already ask them to show their thinking and work in progress. Here the additional responsibility is to make the rule clear before the task. A student cannot reliably disclose against a boundary the teacher only defines afterwards.

Early research in universities suggests that fear needs attention. In a small survey of Canadian undergraduates, students who feared being judged also tended to report stigma and to hide their AI use (Chang et al., 2026). The study found links between these things. It cannot show that one teacher’s tone made students secretive. Nor can it show that school students would respond in the same way.

A university study in Singapore adds students’ own accounts (Qu et al., 2025). They were unsure about the rules for declaring AI use, and they told of false charges. Those students were undergraduates. Their concerns help us ask better questions about school rules. They do not tell us how often children are falsely accused.

Chapter 6 looks at AI detectors and how accurate they are. We do not need to repeat those results to see the duty here. When a teacher suspects a student, it can change how that student feels about school. Questions about work should leave room for the student to explain before anyone is accused.

When work raises a concern, I would start with the task’s stated rules and the student’s own account of what happened. The teacher should name exactly what is unclear and discuss related work she already has. Any formal charge should follow the school’s procedure, with a way to challenge the decision. Questioning a student in front of classmates adds pressure and does not improve the evidence.

Clear rules also protect students who are honest about the help they used. Suppose a secondary teacher lets AI suggest revision questions. But she does not allow AI-written answers in the final work. A student who says they asked for questions has kept the rule. What they told us should not, on its own, make us suspect everything else they wrote.

Families need the same distinction in plain language. An instruction to avoid cheating says little about a parent using a chatbot to explain a difficult word. The teacher can identify the learning that students must do themselves, then state which assistance fits. Consistent expectations make honest accounts easier to interpret.

Such routines will not remove misconduct. They give teachers a fairer starting point when they need to investigate it. The aim is a classroom where students can describe their process accurately, including mistakes, without guessing which answer the adult wants to hear.

Safeguarding beyond the lesson

AI-generated nude images are a safeguarding matter, even when the event they show never happened. A teacher’s first duty is to the child and to the route for responding. Questions about how the image was made can wait. The IWF and NCA guide makes clear that people working with children do not need to decide whether abuse material is AI-generated (2026, p. 4).

England’s statutory guidance now names AI-generated nudes and semi-nudes directly (Department for Education, 2026c). Schools must have regard to that guidance. The IWF and NCA document gives advice for people who work with children and explains the criminal law that applies. Keeping these two kinds of document apart helps readers in other countries. They can see which parts set out an English duty and which give practical advice.

The available school research shows a real concern without giving us a universal prevalence figure. In a large Czech survey co-funded by the EU, 2.77% of participating students reported creating deep nudes (Kopecký & Voráč, 2026, section 4). The sample was non-random and the answers were self-reports. Creation is also a different measure from experiencing abuse or hearing about it.

A teacher should avoid combining those measures into a claim about a typical class. Awareness surveys cannot tell us how many students have suffered abuse. Nor does a report count tell us how much abuse went unreported. Serious preparation does not require an inflated estimate of how often harm occurs.

Teacher readiness looks less reassuring. Wei and colleagues interviewed US secondary teachers about fake intimate images made with AI. None knew of a case at their own school, so their answers were mostly about imagined cases (Wei et al., 2025). Teachers said they were unsure how to look into a case and how the school should respond. A school should agree its response before a first case, not during one. If these teachers felt unsure about imagined cases, I would not expect a real case to feel easier. A short staff discussion of the reporting route can happen now.

Pakistan’s Digital Rights Foundation gives a different kind of record. For the first time, its yearly helpline report lists deepfake intimate image abuse as its own category. It describes AI-generated intimate content in those cases (Digital Rights Foundation, 2025, p. 38). These are records of people who asked for help, not a national survey of students. They do not give a rate for schools. Nor do they show that there are no unreported cases.

What should an ordinary teacher do when a student reports such material? The IWF and NCA guide says to report it at once to the designated safeguarding lead or the person in that role. It tells staff to follow safeguarding procedures. They should not share, download or save the content, even to report it. Its response also includes reporting to the police and support for wellbeing (2026, p. 6).

A request for proof must not create another copy. Teachers can record the concern through the school’s secure procedure without collecting the image on a personal device. Decisions about viewing belong with the safeguarding lead under the relevant procedure. If a child faces immediate danger, the response must reach emergency help promptly.

Two English guidance documents give different advice about deleting images. UKCIS advises asking students to delete the images when the school handles a case without other agencies. The Internet Watch Foundation and the National Crime Agency advise keeping possible evidence on the young person’s device. The two documents are written for different kinds of response (UK Council for Internet Safety, 2024; Internet Watch Foundation & National Crime Agency, 2026).

This chapter cannot settle which instruction fits an individual incident. The difference belongs with the safeguarding lead and the relevant authorities. Teachers need an agreed route for reaching them. A corridor conversation with a distressed child is a poor place to discover that the school has never considered the question.

Care must go on after the referral. The IWF and NCA guide includes support for the child’s feelings and mental health as part of the response. A school also needs to think about how the child returns to normal lessons, friendships and school life. Keeping the case private means staff limit how widely they discuss it. The teacher can keep checking what support the child needs without becoming the person who looks into the case.

Teaching to prevent harm should explain consent and responsibility. It should not show abusive material or show how a tool makes it. Students can discuss a made-up written story about harmful sharing. The teacher can make clear that passing material on adds to the harm, even when the sender calls it a joke. Expert safeguarding advice should guide the words used and what suits each age.

Punishment alone cannot give a harmed child everything they need. The studies held for this chapter do not show which response, through discipline or through teaching, stops the harm happening again. Schools still need to respond seriously, protect the child and follow the law that applies. Chapter 11 takes up the written procedures and shared duties that make that possible.

A chatbot used for schoolwork can also receive a personal question. That possibility brings the safety question back into ordinary teaching. The issue here extends Chapter 3’s discussion of care. Teachers need to recognise when a learning tool enters a student’s emotional life and when concern needs a person.

A US survey funded by the National Institute of Mental Health asked young people about mental health advice from generative AI. Some 13.1% reported doing so (McBain et al., 2025, Results). The sample covered ages 12 to 21, so it included adults as well as school-age participants. Most users described the advice as helpful. That was their own judgement, without a measured clinical outcome.

We should take perceived helpfulness seriously without treating it as proof of safety. A young person may value immediate responses or an opportunity to speak freely. Those experiences do not tell us whether the advice recognises danger or helps over time. Teachers need room to hear why a student uses a chatbot before deciding what the use means.

A Danish survey of upper-secondary students found that those who used chatbots for social support reported more loneliness than those who did not. This group was small, and the study could not show which came first (Herbener & Damholdt, 2025). Chatbot use might follow loneliness, and there are other possible explanations. Taken together, the US and Danish surveys tell me that some young people already turn to chatbots for support, and that we do not yet know whether this helps or harms them. That doubt is a reason to ask students about their use rather than guess.

A disclosure of companion use should open a calm conversation about what the student seeks and whether they have people to approach. The teacher can ask what happens when an answer feels upsetting or wrong. Any concern about immediate safety should follow the school’s safeguarding route. Ordinary curiosity should not turn into a demand to read every private conversation.

We also need to read safety filters with care. In a test run by a campaign group, the Center for Countering Digital Hate looked at how ChatGPT replied to made-up teenage users. The testers found that requests made to seem academic could get past refusals (Center for Countering Digital Hate, 2025). The test used an API, so the app people use could behave differently. It gives a warning about the replies tested. It does not measure harm to students using the app.

This narrow conclusion matters for schools. If a tool refuses a request in a teacher’s short trial, that does not show every later conversation will stay safe. In the same way, a test designed to break a tool cannot tell us how often ordinary students meet harmful replies. We need to be careful about the product and honest about the evidence.

England’s supplier expectations also deal with how these tools present themselves. They advise developers not to present products as if they were people or had feelings (Department for Education, 2026a). This is guidance about how products behave. It does not show that every friendly phrase makes a child dependent. Nor does it show that every warm exchange harms a child.

The classroom response can keep the distinction between responsiveness and responsibility visible. A chatbot can produce an immediate reply. An adult can act within relationships and safeguarding arrangements beyond the conversation. Students need to know where that adult is and how to ask for help.

I would avoid introducing a companion chatbot as a substitute for pastoral support. Where students already use one, I would keep the discussion factual and avoid ridicule. Dismissing something a student values could close the conversation we need to have. The practical question is whether the child can reach suitable human help when they need it.

The evidence held for this chapter does not show that companion chatbots are safe for children over the long term. Nor does it show that every student who tries one becomes emotionally dependent. Teachers can act on a smaller duty now. Notice concerns, listen without shaming and use the usual support routes when a child needs more help. Lawmakers have begun to act. From January 2026, California requires companion chatbots to tell a known minor that they are talking to AI. They must repeat this every three hours (California Legislature, 2025). Washington passed a similar law in 2026 (Washington State Legislature, 2026).

In practice

These worked examples draw on the evidence and guidance above. No study has tested them as a package, so adapt them to your school. Each keeps an ordinary teaching purpose in view while identifying the decision that needs another person.

Primary privacy without student accounts. For example, a primary teacher might plan a reading task about a made-up character who uses an online app. The teacher writes the character’s details on separate paper cards. Some are about the task. Others are private family information. Students discuss which details the task needs, and what the character should ask an adult before sharing.

The teacher then explains that apps may collect information people do not type deliberately. This develops the distinction in Zhao and colleagues’ (2019) study without assuming children can assess a service themselves. Students can suggest questions for the adults choosing the app. Nobody needs to create an account or reveal a personal experience.

The teacher keeps personal stories out of the class discussion. A child may start to describe a worrying experience in front of classmates. The teacher can say she has heard the concern and arrange to talk in private through the safeguarding procedure. If she explains that route beforehand, children have a way to ask for help without telling the whole class.

Before sending the activity home, the teacher writes a short note in words families can understand. It says what students will learn and whether they need any device. Parents can talk about the made-up example without showing family messages or account settings. Children whose families would rather keep those things private can still do the learning.

Secondary feedback with a reason. A secondary teacher might use an approved tool to draft general advice about paragraph structure. She checks the advice against the lesson’s writing goal before students see it. Her explanation identifies the AI assistance and her own checking. Students then use the advice on an example that contains no personal information.

When the teacher comments on individual work, the student can ask which feature of their writing supports the judgement. A generic sentence about weak reasoning needs a specific example from the work. The teacher can revise an unsuitable comment immediately. This gives oversight a visible task and gives the student a way to question the result.

Chapter 6’s assessment routines give the evidence of thinking. Here the teacher adds a clear account of what help is allowed before the task starts. Students can note whether they used an explanation, suggested questions or AI-written wording. If something is unclear, the teacher discusses it against that rule. If a concern remains, she follows the school’s procedure.

A fairness question before a recommendation. Before considering AI suggestions for reading groups, a teacher might record what the class’s recent work shows. She identifies the purpose of each group and how students can move between them. Any AI suggestion then has something independent to meet. An unexplained recommendation cannot decide where a child belongs.

The teacher also considers who can question the placement. A student’s language background or unfamiliar name should not become evidence of low ability. If the teacher cannot explain a decision through observed learning, she returns to the work. Repeated concerns about the tool go to whoever oversees its use, with examples that protect student privacy.

A concern without another copy. If a student reports an abusive image, the teacher listens and follows the safeguarding route. She does not ask for a screenshot through a personal account. A secure written record can name the concern and the referral without copying the image. The safeguarding lead makes the choices about viewing and evidence, under the procedure that applies.

A later conversation concerns support and safety in school. The teacher checks how the student can reach help and who needs to know enough to provide it. She avoids asking the child to retell the account for every adult. Information-sharing follows the safeguarding procedure, including situations where contacting a family could increase risk.

A personal chatbot conversation. For example, a secondary student might mention talking to a chatbot when upset. The teacher asks what help the student wanted and whether anyone else knows they are struggling. She explains the limits of what she can keep confidential if safety concerns arise. The conversation concerns the student’s needs before it concerns the product’s features.

Where the student needs further support, the teacher makes the referral and checks that someone has received it. A general reminder about online safety cannot replace that follow-through. If the student describes immediate danger, the school’s urgent response applies. Teachers should know that route before a disclosure arrives.

A family explanation worth reading. Before students use a tool directly, a teacher can show families a sample task and explain what it is for. The school gives checked information about accounts, data, monitoring and where to get help. Families get a way to ask questions in private, and an explanation of the other activity on offer. Children hear a version that suits their age too.

A family who cannot read the explanation still needs access to the choice. The school can arrange an oral explanation or a translation that someone checks. Teachers should allow time for questions before requesting a decision. A missing reply does not tell them whether the family understood, declined or never received the message. The school needs to follow its agreed permission procedure.

The teacher passes any question she cannot answer to the colleague in charge. She does not cover what she does not know with comforting words. If an answer matters to safety or to a family’s choice, it must be settled before that use begins. Chapter 11 comes back to the plans that make such explanations possible.

In a university course. Put a one-page AI statement in the course outline. It should say which tools are allowed, what students must declare and how suspected misuse will be discussed before any penalty. Keep student data out of any tool the university has not approved.

When resources are thin

A single shared phone changes the privacy question. The person who opens a conversation may not control who next holds the device. Free accounts may also offer different protections from school-managed services. Advice written around a personal laptop and a separate account can miss these everyday conditions.

A report largely funded by the 5Rights Foundation records children’s experiences in Brazil, India, Kenya and Thailand. Participants from less advantaged settings described shared devices, free versions and little formal guidance (Stoilova et al., 2025). These qualitative accounts cannot represent every child in those countries. They do show why safe-use advice needs to ask who controls the device.

Pakistan has related evidence beyond schools. A Google-led study examined adult women’s mobile privacy practices in South Asia, including Pakistan (Sambasivan et al., 2018). Participants managed privacy under social and practical constraints around phone access. The study predates generative AI and does not concern students. It warns against assuming that an individual can simply choose private settings and remain private.

My own doctoral research records a different constraint: the WhatsApp workaround described at the start of this chapter (Ilyas, 2026, p. 159, Participant L4).

A university habit is not a reason to copy that workaround in a school.

My thesis also argues that platforms which seem free bring hidden costs. ‘They extract data value while providing the appearance of access’ (Ilyas, 2026, p. 90). In plain words, the company gains from users’ data while the users feel they have been given access. That is an argument in my thesis, not a measured finding. It helps us question a cheap workaround without denying how much it helps teachers who face unreliable provision.

  • With one teacher device, I would keep the first activity teacher-facing and use prepared material that contains no student information. The class can discuss printed outputs or examples on the board. A paper lesson can teach consent, disclosure and how to seek help. An unreliable connection should change how the lesson is delivered, while the learning purpose stays clear.

  • Without a device, teachers can still use the fictional privacy task from In practice. Secondary students can examine two printed comments and ask what evidence would justify each judgement. A written scenario about harmful sharing can identify the adult to approach. None requires students to show personal messages or install an app.

  • A paper reporting route also needs privacy. Writing a concern in an open staffroom register can expose a child even without an internet connection. Teachers need secure storage and a named recipient for sensitive information. The absence of software does not remove responsibility for who reads a record.

I would also avoid requiring AI use at home while treating the tool as optional in school. That transfers both access costs and safety decisions to families. Offer a route that reaches the same learning goal on paper or through classroom discussion. Keep private the reasons a child chooses it.

Safe, fair and honest use can begin before a school buys anything. Children can learn to question a judgement, explain their assistance and approach a trusted adult. Chapter 9 now asks what students should learn about AI, and how teachers can build that learning across the school years.

In IFDC, feeling safe comes just above the basic conditions (Ilyas et al., 2026b). A teacher who fears blame for a data mistake may avoid tools completely. Clear rules, a list of approved tools and a named person to ask make safe use possible, even where resources are thin.

Questions for reflection

Bring these questions to a phase or department discussion, keeping real students’ details out of the conversation.

  1. Think of an AI activity you plan for your own class: what information about your students does it use, and could they learn the same thing without it?

  2. Who receives a safeguarding alert in your school, and what happens when that person is unavailable?

  3. What independent evidence would let you reject an AI recommendation about a student?

  4. How could a student question a judgement or disclose permitted assistance without feeling accused?

  5. What equivalent learning route can a family choose if they decline direct use of an AI service?

DigCompEdu links this work to protecting digital resources and sensitive content, and to helping learners use technology responsibly (Redecker, 2017, pp. 48, 84). Responsible use includes well-being and managing risks. UNESCO’s framework has three blocks in its ethics of AI aspect: ethical principles, safe and responsible use, and creating ethical rules together (Miao & Cukurova, 2024, p. 22).
