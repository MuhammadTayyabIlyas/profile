A Last Word

I began this book in an elementary classroom in Gujranwala, with a printed guidebook and a dozen notebooks that carried the same sentence. I end it with a teacher in Sacramento who read her students’ writing herself and gave the machine her planning instead. The two rooms are far apart in time and place. The question in both is the same. What does a teacher do when a student can get the answer without doing the thinking?

I have tried to answer it without promise or alarm. AI has changed the shortcut, the speed of planning, the shape of homework and the meaning of a finished piece of work. It has not changed what a student needs: a person who knows them, who notices what they can and cannot yet do, and who answers for the judgements made about them. Where a tool helps that person, this book has said use it. Where it replaces that person, it has said set it aside, and explain why.

Three habits appear in every chapter. Know what has changed, so that you can see a problem early. Read the evidence by its strength, and say so when it is thin. Decide which parts of your work you will keep doing yourself. A decision log and a quiet question to one student are small acts. They are also the acts that no tool can do for you.

Teachers cannot do all of this alone. The teachers in my study had the same unreliable power and connection whatever their skill, and the most skilled among them chose fewer tools, with more care (Ilyas, 2026, pp. 229–230). I take that as a lesson for schools as much as for teachers. Skill grows only where four conditions already exist: power and a connection that work, permission to try and to make mistakes, time to learn, and a part in the decisions a school makes about its tools. My advice to a teacher who lacks them is to ask for them together with colleagues. A head of school can refuse one teacher more easily than a whole staff.

If you remember one thing from this book, let it be the question I would still ask a child who showed me a page that a machine had written. Read me this sentence. Now tell me what it means. The whole book has argued for two things: a teacher who stays in the classroom to ask that question, and a school that acts on the answer.
