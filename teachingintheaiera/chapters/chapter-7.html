Chapter 7. Access, Equity and AI

From Teaching in the AI Era by Dr Muhammad Tayyab Ilyas

A teacher in Islamabad wanted to use the lesson plans. The phone would not cooperate. In a focus group, the teacher described trying to open the app while the accompanying training also required online access. ‘I tried till 2 AM at night’ (Aslam et al., 2025, p. 59).

That is all the source tells us about the scene. We know nothing about the room, or the lesson planned for the next morning. But two things are clear. The teacher wanted to use the plans, but the technology would not allow it.

The account comes from EdTech Hub’s evaluation of AI-generated lesson plans made by Taleemabad for the NIETE programme. GSMA funded the research, through an innovation programme paid for by the UK. After teachers asked again and again, the app was changed so that they could save plans offline, although the training still needed a connection (Aslam et al., 2025, p. 59). The change met a practical need. A lesson plan cannot help anyone while it is stuck inside an app that will not open.

In this chapter

This chapter follows the gap between having a tool and being able to use it. It looks at personalisation, disability, language and unequal access. Then it asks what teachers can do with the resources they really have.

What the chapter covers

  • What must hold before a tool helps
  • Personalisation for whom
  • Disability and the right kind of help
  • A language the learner understands
  • Access, use and unequal benefit
  • In practice
  • When resources are thin
  • Questions for reflection
  • Framework connections

Questions for reflection

Use these questions with colleagues, keeping a particular class in mind.

  1. Think of one AI tool you plan to use with your class: what must be in place for it to work, and which of your students do not have that?
  2. What would show that a tool improves access, and what further evidence would show learning?
  3. Whose language, disability or shared device might your usual classroom resource overlook?
  4. If the average result improves, how will you check whether the students needing most support also benefit?
  5. What equivalent route can students take when the connection fails or their family cannot provide a device?
