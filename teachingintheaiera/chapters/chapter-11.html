Chapter 11. The Whole School

From Teaching in the AI Era by Dr Muhammad Tayyab Ilyas

Ko Ho-dam, a high school junior on Jeju Island, met a new kind of textbook in March 2025. South Korea had approved about a dozen publishers to build AI-powered digital textbooks for maths, English and computer science. The government promised personalised learning, fewer dropouts and a lighter load for teachers. Ko’s first weeks went differently. ‘All our classes were delayed because of technical problems with the textbooks,’ he told a reporter (Kwon, 2025).

Korea’s Board of Audit and Inspection later examined the programme, and a business newspaper reported its findings in December 2025. In schools that had chosen the textbooks, the audit found that 8.1% of students had used the textbooks on ten or more days between March and May. On average, 60% of students never opened the system at all. Among 513 English and maths teachers at pilot schools, 85.5% had never used the textbooks or had stopped. The commonest reason, given by 53%, was that the product was little more than a digitised paper textbook (Lim, 2025).

Questions for reflection

Take these questions to your next leadership team or staff meeting.

  1. In your own school, which use of AI has stayed in use the longest, and who or what has kept it going?
  2. Who owns your school’s AI policy, and when did staff last see it change?
  3. What did your last free tool cost in staff time, data and training?
  4. Which teachers had a say in the last technology your school bought?
  5. What would parents in your community need to hear first, and in which language?
