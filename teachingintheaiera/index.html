What the book is

A chatbot can now write your students’ homework in seconds. It cannot do their learning for them. What should a teacher do?

Not panic, and not pretend nothing has changed. Most of what teachers hear about AI is hype or fear. Neither helps on an ordinary Tuesday. This book does.

By the last page you will be able to set homework a chatbot cannot do for your students. You will know a strong study from a hopeful one. You will plan with AI and keep your own judgement. And you will be able to explain each choice to a colleague, a parent or a child.

The book rests on nearly five hundred studies and policy papers. Each is weighed for how much it can bear. It also draws on the author’s own study of 127 teachers in Pakistan. Every chapter ends with classroom routines, a route for schools with one shared phone or no signal, and questions to take to the staffroom. Twelve lesson cards and seven worksheets are ready to copy.

For school teachers, university staff and school leaders, wherever they teach.

AI has changed what students can hand in. It has not changed what they need to learn. That part still belongs to the teacher.

About the author

Muhammad Tayyab Ilyas began as a science and mathematics teacher at a government elementary school in Gujranwala, Pakistan, in classrooms where a printed guidebook gave students a ready answer to every exercise. From 2017 to 2022 he was an assistant education officer and teacher trainer for the district, supervising a cluster of public schools, observing lessons and running in-service workshops.

He completed a PhD in Education at the Universitat Autònoma de Barcelona in 2026. His thesis studied how university teachers in Pakistan bring technology into their teaching, through a survey of 127 teachers and interviews with 32 of them, and it produced the Infrastructure-First Digital Competence framework that runs through this book. He has published four journal articles, including a scoping review of digital competence frameworks and a study of teachers’ WhatsApp use under institutional bans.

He is also an AI engineer and the founder of PakEdX, an AI lesson-planning and assessment platform for Pakistani school teachers that works in Urdu and in schools with poor connectivity. He writes a regular Urdu column on learning and AI, and lives in Barcelona.

Who is Teaching in the AI Era for?

For school teachers, university staff and school leaders, wherever they teach.

What does the book cover?

A teacher’s guide to AI in schools and universities: what the evidence shows, what it does not yet show, and what to do in a classroom with thirty students, one timetable and a weak connection. Thirteen chapters on homework, planning, learning, assessment, equity, safeguarding, AI literacy, teacher learning, the whole school and higher education, with routines, worksheets and lesson cards.

What evidence is the book based on?

The book rests on nearly five hundred studies and policy papers. Each is weighed for how much it can bear. It also draws on the author’s own study of 127 teachers in Pakistan. The full list of 482 references is on the references page.

What is the IFDC framework?

The Infrastructure-First Digital Competence (IFDC) framework came out of the author’s PhD research and runs through the book. It has four floors, and the upper floors stand only when the floors beneath are in place (Figure 1.1).
